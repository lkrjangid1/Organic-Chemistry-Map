## What is HI and red phosphorus?

**HI and red phosphorus** (written HI/red P) is a **strongly acidic, very powerful reducing system** that replaces oxygen and halogen atoms in organic compounds with hydrogen. Hydroiodic acid does the reducing and is oxidised to iodine; **red phosphorus** converts that iodine back into HI, so the reaction keeps going until the oxygen is removed. Heated long enough, it can strip a molecule down to the parent **alkane**, which is why it was used in classical structure proofs such as that of glucose.

## Composition and how the reagent works

The reagent is simply concentrated hydroiodic acid (aqueous HI) heated with red phosphorus. The reduction of organic compounds with HI was first described by Kiliani more than 140 years ago, and the addition of red phosphorus makes it possible to use HI in catalytic amounts.

Red phosphorus recycles the iodine through phosphorus triiodide:

<Eq ce="2 P + 3 I2 -> 2 PI3" />
<Eq ce="PI3 + 3 H2O -> H3PO3 + 3 HI" />

So the red phosphorus, not the HI, is the reductant that is used up; each mole of phosphorus can reduce at least 1.5 mol of iodine.

## What it reduces

| Substrate | Product | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Benzylic alcohol, Ar–CH(OH)–R | Ar–CH₂–R | reduced easily, even with catalytic HI |
| Alkyl iodide, R–I | R–H | HI reduces the C–I bond; I₂ formed |
| Simple (non-benzylic) alcohol, R–OH | R–I, then R–H | mild conditions stop at R–I; prolonged heating with conc. HI goes on to R–H |
| Glucose (aldehyde + five OH) | n-hexane | prolonged heating with HI removes all oxygen |
| Aromatic nitro compound, Ar–NO₂ | Ar–NH₂ (aniline) | HI acts as the reducing agent |

<Eq ce="C6H12O6 ->[HI, \Delta (prolonged)] CH3(CH2)4CH3" />

<KeyPoint title="Glucose and n-hexane">
On prolonged heating with HI, glucose gives **n-hexane**. Because the product is the unbranched alkane, this showed that all six carbon atoms of glucose are joined in a straight chain. This is the NCERT example of HI as a complete reducing agent.
</KeyPoint>

## What it does not touch (under mild conditions)

A 2012 study that re-examined the reaction in a two-phase toluene–water mixture found that benzylic alcohols were reduced while **keto, ester, amide and ether groups survived**, and that non-benzylic alcohols stopped at the alkyl iodide. Allylic and propargylic alcohols gave mixtures or decomposed. "Complete reduction to alkane" therefore depends on forcing conditions: concentrated HI, red phosphorus and long heating.

## Mechanism

Reduction of an alcohol takes place in two stages:

1. **Substitution.** The acid protonates the OH group and iodide displaces water, giving the alkyl iodide.
2. **Reduction of the C–I bond.** HI reduces the alkyl iodide to the hydrocarbon, apparently by stepwise single-electron transfer (a radical pathway), while iodide is oxidised to iodine.
3. **Recycling.** Red phosphorus turns I₂ back into HI.

<Eq ce="R-OH + HI -> R-I + H2O" />
<Eq ce="R-I + HI -> R-H + I2" />

Water elimination competes with substitution: low HI concentrations favour loss of water (alkene formation), while high HI concentrations favour substitution and reduction.

## Comparison with similar reducing agents

| Reagent | Medium | Typical job | Stops at |
|---|---|---|---|
| HI / red P | strongly acidic, hot | removes O and halogen completely | alkane |
| Zn-Hg / conc. HCl (Clemmensen) | strongly acidic | C=O of aldehydes and ketones to CH₂ | alkane (alcohols are not reduced) |
| NH₂NH₂ / KOH (Wolff–Kishner) | strongly basic | C=O to CH₂ | alkane |
| LiAlH₄ | basic hydride, dry ether | C=O, COOH, esters to alcohols; R–X to R–H | alcohol (for carbonyls) |
| NaBH₄ | mild hydride, alcohol solvent | aldehydes and ketones | alcohol |

The key difference: hydride reagents such as LiAlH₄ and NaBH₄ stop at the **alcohol** level for carbonyl compounds, whereas HI/red P keeps going because it can also remove the OH group.

## Exam tips

<ExamTip>
In JEE and NEET questions, "HI/red P, Δ" is shorthand for **complete reduction to the alkane with the same carbon skeleton**: count the carbons, remove every O and halogen, and saturate with hydrogen. Glucose → n-hexane is the standard example.
</ExamTip>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not assume HI/red P removes oxygen from every group under any conditions. In practice benzylic alcohols react easily, but ketones, esters and amides can survive mild conditions, and simple alcohols may stop at the alkyl iodide. Answer with the alkane only when the question implies prolonged heating with excess reagent.
</ExamTip>

- HI alone (without red P) with an ether gives cleavage to an alcohol and an alkyl iodide, not reduction: see [ether cleavage with HX](/reactions/ether-cleavage-with-hx/).
- For the C=O to CH₂ change with a less drastic reagent, use [Clemmensen](/reactions/clemmensen-reduction/) (acidic) or [Wolff–Kishner](/reactions/wolff-kishner-reduction/) (basic).
