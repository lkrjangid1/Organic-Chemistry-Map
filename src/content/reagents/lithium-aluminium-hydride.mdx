## What is lithium aluminium hydride?

**Lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄, LAH)** is an ionic compound, Li⁺[AlH₄]⁻, in which aluminium carries four hydrogens with an oxidation number of −1. It acts as a source of **hydride ion (H⁻)**, so it is a powerful **nucleophilic reducing agent** (and a strong base). It reduces almost every **polar multiple bond** (C=O, C≡N, N=O) and C–X bonds, but leaves **isolated C=C and C≡C** bonds alone.

## Preparation and handling

LAH was first made by treating lithium hydride with aluminium chloride in ether:

<Eq ce="4LiH + AlCl3 ->[ether] LiAlH4 + 3LiCl" />

The [AlH₄]⁻ ion can be pictured as LiH combined with AlH₃. Industrially, sodium aluminium hydride is made first and converted to the lithium salt.

LAH is used in **dry diethyl ether or THF**. It reacts violently with water, releasing hydrogen:

<Eq ce="LiAlH4 + 4H2O -> LiOH + Al(OH)3 + 4H2 ^" />

So the reduction is always a two-step process: (1) LiAlH₄ in dry ether, (2) H₃O⁺ or water work-up to protonate the alkoxide or amide salts.

## What LiAlH₄ reduces

| Substrate | Product after work-up |
|---|---|
| Aldehyde, R–CHO | 1° alcohol, R–CH₂OH |
| Ketone, R–CO–R′ | 2° alcohol, R–CH(OH)–R′ |
| Carboxylic acid, R–COOH | 1° alcohol, R–CH₂OH |
| Ester, R–COOR′ | R–CH₂OH + R′–OH |
| Acid chloride, R–COCl | 1° alcohol, R–CH₂OH (the aldehyde formed first is reduced again) |
| Acid anhydride, (RCO)₂O | 2 R–CH₂OH |
| Amide, R–CONH₂ | Amine, R–CH₂NH₂ (C=O becomes CH₂) |
| Nitrile, R–C≡N | 1° amine, R–CH₂NH₂ |
| Isocyanide, R–NC | 2° amine, R–NH–CH₃ |
| Aliphatic nitro compound, R–NO₂ | 1° amine, R–NH₂ |
| Aromatic nitro compound, Ar–NO₂ | Azo compound, Ar–N=N–Ar |
| Alkyl halide, R–X | Alkane, R–H (iodide faster than bromide, faster than chloride) |
| Epoxide | Alcohol (H⁻ attacks the less hindered carbon) |
| Imines, oximes, azides | Amines |

<KeyPoint>
Acids, esters, acid chlorides and anhydrides all end as **primary alcohols**. An aldehyde is formed on the way, but it is more reactive than the starting compound, so it cannot be isolated with LiAlH₄. To stop at the aldehyde, use [DIBAL-H](/reagents/dibal-h/) or the [Rosenmund reduction](/reactions/rosenmund-reduction/).
</KeyPoint>

## What LiAlH₄ does not reduce

- **Isolated alkenes, alkynes and arenes.** There is no polarity in the C=C bond for H⁻ to attack.
- **Ethers (R–O–R) and sulphides (R–S–R).** Ethers are in fact the solvent for the reaction.
- **Alcohols, thiols and amines (R–OH, R–SH, R–NH₂).** These are not reduced. Their acidic H simply reacts with hydride, giving H₂ and a metal alkoxide (or thiolate or amide), and the original compound comes back on work-up.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
R–OH + LiAlH₄ does **not** give R–H. The only reaction is acid–base: R–OH + H⁻ → R–O⁻ + H₂. To remove an OH group, convert it first into a halide or tosylate and then reduce that with LiAlH₄.
</ExamTip>

### α,β-Unsaturated carbonyl compounds

A C=C conjugated with C=O is polarised, so it is the one kind of alkene LiAlH₄ can sometimes touch. α,β-Unsaturated ketones are normally reduced only at C=O, giving allylic alcohols. With cinnamaldehyde, the outcome depends on conditions: excess LiAlH₄ under reflux also reduces the C=C, giving 3-phenylpropan-1-ol, whereas slow addition of the hydride at low temperature stops at cinnamyl alcohol. In exam answers, LiAlH₄ is usually taken to give the saturated alcohol:

<Eq ce="C6H5CH=CHCHO ->[1. LiAlH4 (excess)][2. H3O+] C6H5CH2CH2CH2OH" />

[NaBH₄](/reagents/sodium-borohydride/) and the [MPV reduction](/reactions/meerwein-ponndorf-verley-reduction/) are the reagents chosen when the C=C must be kept.

## Mechanism (short)

1. **Hydride transfer.** The polar Al–H bond delivers H⁻ to the electrophilic carbonyl carbon, while Li⁺ coordinates to oxygen. A free hydride ion is never present.
2. **Alkoxide formation.** An aluminium alkoxide forms; one [AlH₄]⁻ can deliver all four hydrides in turn.
3. **Work-up.** Water or dilute acid protonates the alkoxide to the alcohol.

For esters, the tetrahedral intermediate expels R′O⁻ to give an aldehyde, which takes a second hydride. For amides, oxygen leaves as an aluminate, giving an **iminium ion** that takes the second hydride, so nitrogen stays and the product is an amine. Nitriles take two hydrides (imine anion, then dianion) before water gives the amine.

## Comparison with similar reagents

| Property | LiAlH₄ | NaBH₄ | DIBAL-H |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength | Very strong | Mild | Moderate, electrophilic |
| Solvent | Dry ether, THF | Water, MeOH, EtOH | Toluene, hexane |
| Aldehyde, ketone | Alcohol | Alcohol | Alcohol |
| Ester | 1° alcohol | Very slow or none | Aldehyde at −78 °C |
| Acid, amide | Alcohol, amine | No reaction | Not used |
| Nitrile | 1° amine | No reaction | Aldehyde |

LAH is stronger than NaBH₄ because aluminium is less electronegative than boron, which makes the Al–H bond more polar and weaker.

## Exam tips

- R–CO–NH₂ → R–CH₂–NH₂ keeps every carbon; [Hofmann bromamide degradation](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/) loses one. This pair is a favourite "same reactant, different reagent" question.
- R–C≡N gives R–CH₂–NH₂ (1° amine), while the isomeric isocyanide R–N≡C gives R–NH–CH₃ (2° amine), because the carbon of NC ends up as a methyl group on nitrogen.
- Ether, alkene, alkyne and benzene ring survive; count only the polar groups when predicting the product.
