## What is the Hofmann mustard oil reaction?

The **Hofmann mustard oil reaction** is a test for **primary amines**. The amine is warmed with **carbon disulphide (CS₂)** to form an **N-alkyldithiocarbamic acid**, which is then heated with **mercury(II) chloride (HgCl₂)**. The acid loses sulphur and gives an **alkyl isothiocyanate, R–N=C=S**, recognised by its sharp **mustard-oil smell**. Secondary and tertiary amines do not give an isothiocyanate, so the smell confirms a 1° amine.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

| Reagent | Role |
|---|---|
| Carbon disulphide, CS₂ (often in ethanol) | Electrophile; adds to the amine to give a dithiocarbamic acid |
| Mercury(II) chloride, HgCl₂ (aqueous) | Desulphurising agent; removes H₂S as insoluble HgS |

No special preparation is needed: both are used as ready solutions. Other sulphur-removing reagents, such as lead nitrate or tosyl chloride, give the same isothiocyanate in preparative work.

## Procedure

1. Warm a few drops of the amine with a little CS₂ in ethanol.
2. Add HgCl₂ solution and heat the mixture.
3. Carefully waft the vapour towards the nose. A pungent smell like mustard or horseradish is a positive result.

Work in a fume cupboard: CS₂ is highly flammable and toxic, and mercury salts are poisonous.

## Observations and inference

| Amine | Step 1: with CS₂ | Step 2: with HgCl₂ | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1° (RNH₂) | RNH–CS–SH (dithiocarbamic acid) | R–N=C=S formed | **Mustard-oil smell: positive** |
| 2° (R₂NH) | R₂N–CS–SH formed | No isothiocyanate | Negative |
| 3° (R₃N) | No dithiocarbamic acid | Nothing to react | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

**Step 1: addition to CS₂.** The nitrogen lone pair attacks the electrophilic carbon of S=C=S. A proton moves from nitrogen to sulphur, giving an N-substituted dithiocarbamic acid:

<Eq ce="RNH2 + S=C=S -> RNH-C(=S)-SH" />

**Step 2: desulphurisation.** HgCl₂ binds strongly to sulphur. The dithiocarbamic acid loses the N–H hydrogen and the SH group as the elements of H₂S, which mercury captures as black HgS. The remaining carbon forms a double bond to nitrogen:

<Eq ce="RNH-C(=S)-SH + HgCl2 ->[\Delta] R-N=C=S + HgS + 2HCl" />

**Worked example (ethylamine):**

<Eq ce="C2H5NH2 + CS2 -> C2H5NHCSSH ->[HgCl2] C2H5NCS + HgS + 2HCl" />

**Secondary amine:** the dithiocarbamic acid forms, but nitrogen has no hydrogen left to lose:

<Eq ce="(CH3)2NH + CS2 -> (CH3)2N-C(=S)-SH ->[HgCl2] \text{no isothiocyanate}" />

<KeyPoint>
Two N–H hydrogens are needed: one is used when the amine adds to CS₂, the second is lost when the N=C=S group forms. Only primary amines have both, so only they give mustard oil.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The dithiocarbamic acid R–NH–C(=S)–SH is only the intermediate. **Mustard oil is the isothiocyanate, R–N=C=S.** Natural mustard oil is mainly allyl isothiocyanate, CH₂=CH–CH₂–N=C=S. Do not confuse either with **mustard gas**, bis(2-chloroethyl) sulphide, Cl–CH₂CH₂–S–CH₂CH₂–Cl.
</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>
Both the mustard oil reaction and the [carbylamine reaction](/reactions/carbylamine-reaction/) are tests for 1° amines that rely on smell. Carbylamine gives an isocyanide (R–N≡C, offensive smell) with CHCl₃ and KOH; mustard oil gives an isothiocyanate (R–N=C=S, pungent mustard smell) with CS₂ and HgCl₂.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Smell is subjective, and the reagents themselves smell strongly, so the result needs care to read.
- The test uses toxic, volatile CS₂ and a mercury salt, so it is rarely done in school laboratories today; the Hinsberg and carbylamine tests are preferred.
- It shows only whether the amine is primary. It does not separate secondary from tertiary amines; use the [Hinsberg test](/tests/hinsberg-test/) for that.
