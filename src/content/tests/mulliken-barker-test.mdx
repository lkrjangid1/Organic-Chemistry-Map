## What is the Mulliken–Barker test?

The **Mulliken–Barker test** detects the **nitro group (–NO₂)**. The compound is warmed with **zinc dust and ammonium chloride**, a neutral reducing mixture that converts R–NO₂ into an **N-substituted hydroxylamine, R–NHOH**. The filtered solution is then warmed with **Tollens' reagent**: the hydroxylamine reduces Ag⁺ to **metallic silver**, seen as a **grey to black precipitate** or a silver mirror. S. P. Mulliken and E. F. Barker reported the reactions in 1899.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

| Step | Reagent | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Zinc dust + aqueous NH₄Cl (in ethanol–water) | Neutral reduction: R–NO₂ → R–NHOH |
| 2 | Tollens' reagent, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (freshly made from AgNO₃, a drop of NaOH and just enough NH₃) | Detects the reducing hydroxylamine |

Tollens' reagent must be prepared fresh and discarded after use, because old ammoniacal silver solutions can form explosive solids.

## Procedure

1. **Blank:** warm a little of the original compound with Tollens' reagent. It should give no silver; otherwise the test cannot be used.
2. Dissolve about 0.2 g of the compound in a few mL of ethanol. Add about 0.5 g of zinc dust and 1 mL of NH₄Cl solution.
3. Heat to boiling, allow to stand for a few minutes, and filter.
4. Warm the filtrate with Tollens' reagent. A grey or black precipitate of silver indicates a nitro group.

## Observations and inference

| Compound | Tollens' on original | Tollens' after Zn/NH₄Cl | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrobenzene, nitroalkane | No silver | **Grey-black Ag precipitate** | Nitro group present |
| Aniline (amine) | No silver | No silver | No nitro group |
| Aldehyde (e.g. benzaldehyde) | Silver mirror | Silver mirror | Test not applicable |
| Nitrobenzoic acid | No silver | Often negative | False negative (see limitations) |

## Chemistry behind it

**Step 1: neutral reduction.** In the almost neutral Zn/NH₄Cl mixture the nitro group takes up four electrons and four protons and stops at the hydroxylamine stage:

<Eq ce="C6H5NO2 + 2Zn + 4NH4Cl -> C6H5NHOH + 2ZnCl2 + 4NH3 + H2O" />

The same reagent reduces aliphatic nitro compounds:

<Eq ce="R-NO2 + 2Zn + 4NH4Cl -> R-NHOH + 2ZnCl2 + 4NH3 + H2O" />

**Step 2: Tollens' reagent.** Hydroxylamines are good reducing agents. The hydroxylamine is oxidised to the nitroso compound and silver(I) is reduced to silver metal:

<Eq ce="C6H5NHOH + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 2OH- -> C6H5NO + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O" />

<KeyPoint>
The key is the **neutral** medium. In acid (Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl) the nitro group is reduced all the way to an amine, and amines do not reduce Tollens' reagent. Stopping at R–NHOH is what makes the silver test work.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip>
Answer pattern: "R–NO₂ → (Zn/NH₄Cl) → R–NHOH; R–NHOH + Tollens' reagent → R–N=O + Ag (grey/black ppt)." Name the intermediate for nitrobenzene as N-phenylhydroxylamine and the oxidised product as nitrosobenzene.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Reducing compounds interfere.** Aldehydes, α-hydroxy ketones, hydrazines and other reducing substances give silver with Tollens' reagent on their own, so the blank in step 1 is essential.
- **Nitro compounds that also carry a COOH group** (for example nitrobenzoic acids) often fail to give a positive result, a known weakness of the test.
- A compound that is already a hydroxylamine gives silver without the reduction step, so the blank also guards against this.
- On standing in acid, N-phenylhydroxylamine rearranges to 4-aminophenol ([Bamberger rearrangement](/reactions/bamberger-rearrangement/)), so the filtrate should be tested promptly.
