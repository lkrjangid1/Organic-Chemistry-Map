## What is Fehling's test?

**Fehling's test** detects **aliphatic aldehydes** and **reducing sugars**. The sample is heated with **Fehling's solution**, a deep blue alkaline complex of **copper(II) with tartrate ions**. An aliphatic aldehyde is oxidised to its carboxylate ion while Cu²⁺ is reduced to Cu⁺, which separates as a **reddish-brown precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O**. Ketones and aromatic aldehydes do not respond.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

Fehling's reagent is supplied as two stable solutions that are mixed in **equal volumes just before use**, because the combined copper complex slowly decomposes:

| Solution | Composition | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Fehling A | Aqueous copper(II) sulphate, CuSO₄ | Blue |
| Fehling B | Sodium potassium tartrate (Rochelle salt, Seignette salt) in aqueous NaOH | Colourless |
| Mixed reagent | Bis(tartrato)cuprate(II) complex in alkali | Deep blue |

The tartrate acts as a ligand. It holds Cu²⁺ in solution in the strongly basic medium, where it would otherwise precipitate as Cu(OH)₂.

## Procedure

1. Mix 1 mL each of Fehling A and Fehling B in a test tube.
2. Add a few drops (or a few mg) of the sample.
3. Heat on a boiling water bath for 2–5 minutes.
4. Look for a reddish-brown precipitate.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Aliphatic aldehyde (ethanal, propanal) | Reddish-brown Cu₂O precipitate | Positive |
| Aromatic aldehyde (benzaldehyde) | Blue colour stays | Negative |
| Simple ketone (propanone) | Blue colour stays | Negative |
| α-Hydroxy ketone, ketose (fructose) | Red precipitate | Positive |
| Formic acid (HCOOH) | Red precipitate | Positive |
| Reducing sugars (glucose, maltose, lactose) | Red precipitate | Positive |
| Sucrose | Blue colour stays | Negative (non-reducing) |

## Chemistry behind it

The copper(II) complex oxidises the aldehyde group. Two Cu²⁺ ions each gain one electron and leave as one Cu₂O:

<Eq ce="RCHO + 2Cu^2+ + 5OH- -> RCOO- + Cu2O v + 3H2O" />

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CHO + 2Cu^2+ + 5OH- -> CH3CH2COO- + Cu2O v + 3H2O" />

Formic acid is oxidised further, to carbon dioxide (carbonate in the alkaline reagent), which is why it is the only simple carboxylic acid that gives the test.

α-Hydroxy ketones and ketoses such as fructose are positive because the base in the reagent tautomerises them, via an enediol, into aldehydes. Cyclic hemiacetals (2-hydroxytetrahydropyran, the ring forms of glucose) open to the free aldehyde, so they react too.

<KeyPoint>
Fehling's solution is a weaker oxidising agent than Tollens' reagent. It oxidises aliphatic aldehydes, α-hydroxy ketones, HCOOH and reducing sugars, but not aromatic aldehydes. In exam answers, R–CHCl₂ is also counted as positive, because it hydrolyses to R–CHO in the hot alkaline reagent.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip>
To separate ethanal and benzaldehyde: both give a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent, but only ethanal gives a red precipitate with Fehling's solution. To separate ethanal and propanone, either reagent works.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Aromatic aldehydes give no result, so a negative Fehling's test does not rule out an aldehyde.
- α-Hydroxy ketones and ketoses also give a positive result, so the test cannot by itself separate aldoses from ketoses. Use the [bromine water test](/tests/bromine-water-test/) for that.
- The mixed reagent must be prepared fresh, because the copper tartrate complex slowly decomposes in the alkaline solution.
- Fehling's test cannot separate benzaldehyde from acetone, since both are negative.
