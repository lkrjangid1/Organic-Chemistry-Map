## What is the ceric ammonium nitrate test?

The **ceric ammonium nitrate (CAN) test** detects the **alcoholic –OH group**. A few drops of orange-yellow **ceric ammonium nitrate, (NH₄)₂[Ce(NO₃)₆]**, are added to a solution of the compound. If an alcohol is present, the alcohol molecules replace nitrate ligands on cerium(IV) and form a **red complex**, so the solution turns red. The red colour is short-lived and fades on standing.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

CAN is an orange-red, very water-soluble salt of cerium in the +4 oxidation state. Its anion is the hexanitratocerate(IV) ion, [Ce(NO₃)₆]²⁻, in which each of the six nitrate groups binds to cerium through two oxygen atoms (bidentate). The salt is made by dissolving cerium(III) oxide in hot concentrated nitric acid.

For the test, the crystals are dissolved to give an orange-yellow solution, usually made up in dilute nitric acid so that Ce⁴⁺ does not hydrolyse.

## Procedure

1. Dissolve the organic compound in a suitable solvent and take about 1 mL of the solution in a test tube.
2. Add a **few drops** of ceric ammonium nitrate solution and shake.
3. Look for a red colour at once.

Avoid excess reagent: too much orange CAN hides the red colour of the complex.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Example | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aliphatic alcohol | Ethanol, propan-2-ol | Orange-yellow → red | Alcoholic –OH present |
| Phenol | C₆H₅OH | No red alcohol colour (Ce(IV) oxidises it instead) | Not an alcohol; confirm with FeCl₃ |
| Compound without –OH | Hexane, diethyl ether | Orange-yellow colour stays | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

NCERT writes the colour change as ligand exchange: three alcohol molecules take the place of two nitrate ions in the coordination sphere of cerium(IV).

<Eq ce="(NH4)2[Ce(NO3)6] + 3ROH -> [Ce(NO3)4(ROH)3] + 2NH4NO3" />

<Eq ce="(NH4)2[Ce(NO3)6] + 3C2H5OH -> [Ce(NO3)4(C2H5OH)3] + 2NH4NO3" />

Cerium(IV) is a strong **one-electron oxidant**. After the complex forms, Ce⁴⁺ slowly oxidises the bound alcohol and is reduced to Ce³⁺, which is nearly colourless (pale yellow). This is why the red colour disappears after some time. The same oxidising power is used in organic synthesis, where CAN oxidises alcohols, phenols, ethers and benzylic C–H bonds.

<KeyPoint>
A red colour with CAN means an **alcoholic –OH group**. It says nothing about whether the alcohol is 1°, 2° or 3°. The colour is temporary, so observe immediately and use only a few drops of reagent.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip>
In exam answers, phenol is said **not** to give the CAN test (no red colour), while aliphatic alcohols do. So to tell ethanol from phenol, either test works: CAN gives red with ethanol, and neutral FeCl₃ gives violet with phenol.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **The red colour is temporary.** It fades as Ce(IV) oxidises the alcohol, and it is masked if excess reagent is added.
- **No classification.** It cannot separate primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols; use the [Lucas test](/tests/lucas-test/), the [Victor Meyer test](/tests/victor-meyer-test/) or the iodoform test ([haloform reaction](/reactions/haloform-reaction/)) for CH₃CH(OH)– groups.
- **Phenols interfere.** CAN is an oxidant for phenols (it turns catechols and hydroquinones into quinones), so phenols do not give the clean red alcohol colour. Identify them separately with [ferric chloride](/tests/ferric-chloride-test/).
- Other easily oxidised compounds can also reduce Ce(IV) and change the colour of the reagent.
