## What is the Lucas test?

The **Lucas test** distinguishes **primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols** by how quickly they react with **Lucas reagent**, a solution of **anhydrous ZnCl₂ in concentrated HCl**. The alcohol is converted into an alkyl chloride, which is insoluble in the reagent and makes the solution **turbid**. **Tertiary alcohols turn cloudy immediately, secondary alcohols within about five minutes, and primary alcohols show no turbidity at room temperature.** The test was reported by Howard Lucas in 1930.

## Observations

| Alcohol class | Example | Result with Lucas reagent at room temperature |
|---|---|---|
| 3° | 2-methylpropan-2-ol | Turbidity **immediately** |
| 2° | propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol | Turbidity in **about 5 minutes** |
| 1° | ethanol, propan-1-ol | **No turbidity** (reacts only on heating) |

Benzylic and allylic alcohols behave like tertiary alcohols and give turbidity at once, because they also form stabilised carbocations.

## The underlying reaction: alcohol + HX

The Lucas test is a special case of the general conversion of alcohols into alkyl halides:

<Eq ce="R-OH + HX -> R-X + H2O" />

- **Reactivity of HX:** HI > HBr > HCl (HF is generally unreactive).
- **Reactivity of alcohols:** 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl.
- **NaX alone does not react** with alcohols, because there is no acid to protonate the OH. A mixture of NaBr or NaCl with conc. H₂SO₄ works, because it generates HX in situ.
- **HCl is the weakest nucleophile** of the three acids, so it needs ZnCl₂ to react with primary and secondary alcohols. This is exactly why Lucas reagent separates the three classes so clearly.

## Mechanism (SN1 for 2° and 3° alcohols)

1. **Activation of OH.** The oxygen lone pair takes up H⁺ from HCl (or binds to ZnCl₂). The –OH becomes –OH₂⁺ (or –O(H)–ZnCl₂), a good leaving group.
2. **Carbocation formation (slow, rate-determining).** The leaving group departs and a carbocation forms.
3. **Capture by chloride (fast).** Cl⁻ attacks the carbocation to give the alkyl chloride, which separates as an oily, cloudy layer.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-OH ->[H+] (CH3)3C-OH2+ ->[slow][-H2O] (CH3)3C+ ->[Cl-][fast] (CH3)3C-Cl" />

<KeyPoint>
The rate of the Lucas reaction follows carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1°. Carbocation formation is the rate-determining step, so the more stable the carbocation, the faster the turbidity.
</KeyPoint>

Primary alcohols cannot form a stable carbocation. When they do react (for example on heating), chloride displaces the activated OH group directly in an **SN2** step, which is slow with Cl⁻.

## Role of anhydrous ZnCl₂

ZnCl₂ is a **Lewis acid**. It complexes with an oxygen lone pair of the alcohol and makes the hydroxyl group leave more easily. It must be anhydrous so that the reagent stays strongly acidic and concentrated.

## Limitations and exceptions

- The test works only for alcohols that dissolve in the reagent, roughly up to five or six carbons. Larger alcohols form a separate layer whether or not they react.
- Carbocations can rearrange, so the chloride formed from some secondary alcohols may have a rearranged carbon skeleton.
- Allylic and benzylic primary alcohols give a positive result immediately, so a fast result does not always mean a tertiary alcohol.
