## What is the Tollens' test?

The **Tollens' test** (silver mirror test) distinguishes **aldehydes from ketones**. The sample is warmed with **Tollens' reagent**, a freshly prepared **ammoniacal silver nitrate** solution containing [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺. An aldehyde is oxidised to its **carboxylate ion**, and the silver(I) ions are reduced to **metallic silver**, which forms a shiny **silver mirror** on the inside of the tube. Both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes respond; ordinary ketones do not.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

Tollens' reagent cannot be stored, so it is made just before the test:

1. Add a few drops of dilute NaOH to aqueous AgNO₃. A brown precipitate of silver(I) oxide forms.
2. Add dilute aqueous ammonia drop by drop until the precipitate **just dissolves**. The clear solution contains the diamminesilver(I) complex.

<Eq ce="2AgNO3 + 2NaOH -> Ag2O v + 2NaNO3 + H2O" />

<Eq ce="Ag2O + 4NH3 + H2O -> 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 2OH-" />

Adding ammonia directly to AgNO₃ also works: the first drops precipitate Ag₂O, and more ammonia redissolves it.

## Procedure

1. Take about 2 mL of freshly prepared Tollens' reagent in a clean test tube.
2. Add a few drops of the sample (dissolved in a little water or ether if needed).
3. Warm in a water bath for a few minutes. Do not heat strongly.
4. After the test, acidify the mixture with dilute acid before discarding it.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Aliphatic aldehyde (ethanal) | Silver mirror or black precipitate | Positive |
| Aromatic aldehyde (benzaldehyde) | Silver mirror | Positive |
| Simple ketone (propanone) | No change, clear solution | Negative |
| α-Hydroxy ketone (fructose) | Silver mirror | Positive |
| Formic acid (HCOOH) | Silver mirror | Positive |
| Reducing sugars (glucose, maltose) | Silver mirror | Positive |
| Terminal alkyne (R–C≡CH) | White precipitate of silver acetylide, no mirror | Acidic C–H, not oxidation |

## Chemistry behind it

The diamminesilver(I) ion is a mild oxidising agent. The aldehyde loses its C–H hydrogen and becomes a carboxylate (the medium is alkaline, so the free acid is not formed until the mixture is acidified). Each Ag⁺ gains one electron:

<Eq ce="RCHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 3OH- -> RCOO- + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O" />

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 3OH- -> CH3COO- + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O" />

α-Hydroxy ketones and ketoses react because the base in the reagent converts them, through an enediol, into an aldehyde that is then oxidised. Cyclic hemiacetals (for example 2-hydroxytetrahydropyran, or the ring form of glucose) are in equilibrium with an open-chain aldehyde, so they also reduce the reagent.

Terminal alkynes give a white precipitate for a different reason: the acidic ≡C–H hydrogen is replaced by silver.

<Eq ce="R-C#CH + [Ag(NH3)2]+ -> R-C#C-Ag v + NH4+ + NH3" />

<KeyPoint>
Tollens' reagent oxidises the –CHO group selectively. In exam questions an unsaturated aldehyde such as hex-4-enal is converted (after acidification) into hex-4-enoic acid with the C=C left intact. This makes Tollens' reagent useful when a stronger oxidant like KMnO₄ would also attack the double bond.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Fehling's and Benedict's solutions do not oxidise aromatic aldehydes, but Tollens' reagent does. So benzaldehyde is Tollens-positive and Fehling-negative, which is a favourite way to separate aliphatic from aromatic aldehydes.
</ExamTip>

In exam answers, a gem-dichloride with a terminal CHCl₂ group (R–CHCl₂) is also listed as Tollens-positive, because in the alkaline reagent it can hydrolyse to the aldehyde R–CHO, which is then oxidised.

## Limitations

- Most ketones are negative, but α-hydroxy ketones, 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds, hydrazines and formic acid are positive, so a mirror does not prove an aldehyde by itself.
- The reagent must be fresh and the tube clean; old reagent may form explosive silver compounds, and dirty glass gives a black precipitate instead of a mirror.
- Water-insoluble aldehydes react slowly; a co-solvent such as ether helps.
- Confirm a carbonyl group first with the [2,4-DNP test](/tests/2-4-dnp-test/); then use Tollens' test to decide between aldehyde and ketone.
