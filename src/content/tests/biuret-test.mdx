## What is the biuret test?

The **biuret test** is the general colour test for **proteins and peptides**. The sample is made alkaline with sodium hydroxide and a few drops of dilute **copper(II) sulphate** are added. If the molecule contains **two or more peptide bonds (–CO–NH–)**, the blue colour of Cu²⁺ changes to **violet (purple)**. Free amino acids and dipeptides do not respond, so the test shows whether amino acids have been joined into chains.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

- **On the bench:** the sample solution, then sodium hydroxide solution (about 10%), then 1–2 drops of dilute (1–2%) copper(II) sulphate solution.
- **Ready-made biuret reagent:** CuSO₄ in NaOH or KOH, with **sodium potassium tartrate** added. Tartrate chelates Cu²⁺ and keeps it stable in solution in the strongly alkaline medium.

The reagent is **blue** before use. It contains no biuret.

## Procedure

1. Take about 2 mL of the test solution (for example egg albumin in water) in a test tube.
2. Add about 2–3 mL of sodium hydroxide solution and mix.
3. Add 1–2 drops of dilute copper(II) sulphate solution and shake. Do not heat.
4. Note the colour after a minute or two. Run a blank (water) side by side for comparison.

## Observations and inference

| Sample | Peptide bonds | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein (egg albumin, casein, gelatin) | Many | Violet / purple | Protein present |
| Polypeptide, tripeptide | Two or more | Violet to pinkish violet | Peptide present |
| Dipeptide | One | Stays blue | Negative |
| Free amino acid (glycine, proline) | None | Stays blue | Negative |
| Biuret, NH₂CONHCONH₂ | Two –CO–NH– units | Violet | Positive (gives the test its name) |
| Urea | None | Stays blue | Negative until heated to biuret |

## Chemistry behind it

### Biuret from urea

When urea is heated to about 150 °C for some hours, two molecules condense and **ammonia is given off**. The product, biuret, contains the –CO–NH–CO– unit that mimics a peptide chain.

<Eq ce="2 NH2CONH2 ->[\Delta][about 150 °C] NH2CONHCONH2 + NH3 ^" />

### Origin of the violet colour

In strongly alkaline solution, the N–H protons of peptide (amide) groups are displaced and the **nitrogen atoms coordinate to Cu²⁺**. A copper ion needs several neighbouring nitrogen donors from the same chain to form a stable chelate, which is why at least two peptide bonds are needed. The violet copper–peptide complex absorbs around 540 nm.

<Eq ce="Cu^2+ (blue) + peptide chain ->[OH-] Cu(II)-peptide complex (violet)" />

<KeyPoint>
The biuret test detects the **peptide bond itself**, not any particular side chain. That makes it the test for proteins in general, unlike tests that respond only to particular amino-acid side chains.
</KeyPoint>

Because the colour intensity is proportional to the number of peptide bonds present, the same reaction is used in laboratories to **estimate protein concentration** by measuring absorbance (the biuret assay).

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
When biuret is made from urea, ammonia is a product: the balanced equation is 2 NH₂CONH₂ → NH₂CONHCONH₂ + NH₃. Also, the biuret reagent does not contain biuret; only the name comes from it.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Free amino acids and dipeptides** give no colour, so a negative test does not rule out amino acids.
- The test is **not very sensitive**; very dilute protein solutions may give only a faint tint.
- **Ammonia and some buffers** (such as Tris) interfere with the colour.
