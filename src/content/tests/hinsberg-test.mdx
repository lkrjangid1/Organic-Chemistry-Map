## What is the Hinsberg test?

The **Hinsberg test** distinguishes **primary, secondary and tertiary amines** using **benzenesulphonyl chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl)**, known as Hinsberg's reagent, in aqueous NaOH or KOH. A **primary amine** forms a sulphonamide that **dissolves in the alkali**, a **secondary amine** forms a sulphonamide that is **insoluble in alkali**, and a **tertiary amine** forms **no sulphonamide**. Oscar Hinsberg described the reaction in 1890.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

- **Hinsberg's reagent** is benzenesulphonyl chloride, a colourless oily liquid made industrially by chlorosulphonation of benzene. It is used as supplied, not prepared at the bench.
- **Alkali:** 5–10% aqueous NaOH or KOH, in excess. The alkali neutralises the HCl formed and also tests whether the sulphonamide is acidic.
- **Dilute HCl** is kept ready to acidify the mixture at the end.

The sulphonyl chloride is stable to cold water but is hydrolysed on heating, so the test is done by shaking at room temperature.

## Procedure

1. Put a few drops (or about 0.5 g) of the amine, about 10 mL of 5% NaOH and about 1 mL of benzenesulphonyl chloride in a test tube.
2. Stopper and shake until the sharp smell of the sulphonyl chloride has gone. Keep the mixture alkaline.
3. If a solid or oil separates, dilute with water and shake again. A solid that still does not dissolve points to a **secondary amine**.
4. If the alkaline solution is clear, acidify it carefully with dilute HCl. A precipitate now points to a **primary amine**.
5. If the original amine simply stays undissolved in the alkali and dissolves on adding dilute acid, the amine is **tertiary**.

## Observations and inference

| Amine | Product with C₆H₅SO₂Cl | In excess NaOH | On adding dilute HCl |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1° (RNH₂) | C₆H₅SO₂NHR (N-alkylsulphonamide) | **Dissolves** as the sodium salt | Sulphonamide **precipitates** |
| 2° (R₂NH) | C₆H₅SO₂NR₂ (N,N-dialkylsulphonamide) | **Insoluble** solid | Stays insoluble |
| 3° (R₃N) | No sulphonamide | Amine stays insoluble | Amine **dissolves** as its salt |

## Chemistry behind it

The amine nitrogen is a nucleophile. Its lone pair attacks the electrophilic sulphur of the sulphonyl chloride, and chloride is displaced. This is the sulphonyl analogue of acylating an amine with an acid chloride.

**Primary amine.** The sulphonamide still carries one N–H. That hydrogen is strongly acidic because the SO₂ group pulls electron density away from nitrogen and stabilises the negative charge left behind. NaOH removes it, giving a soluble salt:

<Eq ce="C6H5SO2Cl + RNH2 -> C6H5SO2NHR + HCl" />

<Eq ce="C6H5SO2NHR + NaOH -> C6H5SO2NR^- Na+ + H2O" />

Acidifying gives back the neutral, insoluble sulphonamide, which is why a precipitate appears at the last step.

**Secondary amine.** The product has no N–H, so it cannot be deprotonated and stays insoluble in alkali:

<Eq ce="C6H5SO2Cl + (C2H5)2NH -> C6H5SO2N(C2H5)2 + HCl" />

**Tertiary amine.** There is no hydrogen on nitrogen to lose, so no stable sulphonamide forms:

<Eq ce="C6H5SO2Cl + R3N -> \text{no sulphonamide}" />

<KeyPoint>
The whole test rests on one question: how many H atoms are on the amine nitrogen? Two H (1°): sulphonamide with an acidic N–H, soluble in alkali. One H (2°): sulphonamide with no N–H, insoluble in alkali. No H (3°): no sulphonamide.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Hinsberg's reagent is **benzenesulphonyl** chloride, C₆H₅SO₂Cl, with sulphur bonded directly to the ring. It is not benzylsulphonyl chloride (C₆H₅CH₂SO₂Cl). Write the formula, not just the name, to avoid losing the mark.
</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>
NCERT examples: ethylamine gives N-ethylbenzenesulphonamide (soluble in alkali); diethylamine gives N,N-diethylbenzenesulphonamide (insoluble in alkali); triethylamine does not react. The same reaction is also used to separate a mixture of amines.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Tertiary amines are not completely inert.** Under some conditions they speed up hydrolysis of the sulphonyl chloride, giving soluble sulphonate salts, so the "no reaction" result holds only for the usual short shaking at room temperature.
- **The mixture must stay alkaline.** If the alkali is used up, the HCl formed protonates unreacted amine and the observations become unreliable.
- **Rate, concentration, temperature and solubility all matter.** The test is reliable only when these are controlled, so it is a classification test, not proof of structure.
