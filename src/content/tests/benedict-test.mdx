## What is Benedict's test?

**Benedict's test** detects **reducing sugars** and other easily oxidised carbonyl compounds. The sample is heated with **Benedict's solution**, a blue alkaline solution of **copper(II) complexed with citrate**. Glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose and aliphatic aldehydes reduce Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺, which separates as a **brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide**. Its job is the same as that of [Fehling's solution](/tests/fehling-test/), but the reagent is milder and keeps well.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

Benedict's reagent is a **single deep blue solution** that is stored ready to use. One litre contains roughly:

| Component | Amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Copper(II) sulphate pentahydrate | 17.3 g | Source of Cu²⁺, the oxidising agent |
| Sodium citrate | 173 g | Complexes Cu²⁺ so that Cu(OH)₂ or CuCO₃ does not precipitate |
| Anhydrous sodium carbonate | 100 g | Keeps the solution mildly alkaline |

While making it, the carbonate and citrate are dissolved together first, and the copper sulphate solution is stirred in slowly.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Unlike Fehling's reagent, Benedict's reagent is not used as two solutions "A" and "B" mixed at the time of the test. It is one stable solution. Its mild alkali (Na₂CO₃ rather than NaOH) and citrate ligand are what make it keep for a long time.
</ExamTip>

## Procedure

1. Dissolve a little of the sample in about 0.5–1 mL of water.
2. Add about 2–3 mL of Benedict's solution.
3. Heat in a boiling water bath for a few minutes and let it cool.
4. Note the colour of any precipitate.

## Observations and inference

| Sample | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose, fructose, galactose | Brick-red precipitate | Reducing sugar present |
| Maltose, lactose | Brick-red precipitate | Reducing disaccharide |
| Sucrose | Solution stays blue | Non-reducing sugar |
| Starch | No change or very faint | Few reducing ends |
| α-Hydroxy ketone, HCOOH | Red precipitate | Positive |
| Simple ketone, aromatic aldehyde | Solution stays blue | Negative |

The colour of the precipitate rises with the amount of sugar: green (low), yellow, orange, then brick-red (high). This makes Benedict's test **semi-quantitative**.

## Chemistry behind it

The citrate-bound Cu²⁺ oxidises the free aldehyde group (or the aldehyde formed from a hemiacetal) to a carboxylate:

<Eq ce="RCHO + 2Cu^2+ + 5OH- -> RCOO- + Cu2O v + 3H2O" />

For glucose the product is the gluconate ion:

<Eq ce="C6H12O6 + 2Cu^2+ + 5OH- -> C5H11O5COO- + Cu2O v + 3H2O" />

- **Hemiacetals** such as the ring forms of glucose are in equilibrium with the open-chain aldehyde, so they react.
- **Ketoses** such as fructose are positive because the alkaline reagent converts them, through an enediol, into aldoses (glucose and mannose).
- **Acetals** (glycosidic links at both anomeric carbons, as in sucrose) cannot open, so they are negative.

<KeyPoint>
Benedict's test gives the same positive list as Fehling's test: aliphatic aldehydes, α-hydroxy ketones, HCOOH, cyclic hemiacetals and reducing sugars. Aromatic aldehydes and simple ketones are negative with both.
</KeyPoint>

## Limitations

- Fructose is positive, so Benedict's test cannot tell an aldose from a ketose. The [bromine water test](/tests/bromine-water-test/) does this.
- In the lab, many simple water-insoluble aldehydes and conjugated aldehydes react poorly, so the test works best for water-soluble compounds such as sugars.
- Other reducing substances (for example ascorbic acid) also give a positive result, which is why it is no longer used for diagnosing diabetes.
- Sucrose gives a positive test only after it is hydrolysed by heating with dilute acid.
