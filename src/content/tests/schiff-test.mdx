## What is Schiff's test?

**Schiff's test** is a quick colour test for **aldehydes**. **Schiff's reagent** is a magenta dye (**basic fuchsine**) that has been made **colourless with sulphur dioxide**. When an aldehyde is added to the cold reagent, the **magenta-pink colour returns**. Ketones are treated as negative, so the test separates aldehydes from ketones without any heating.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

1. Take a dilute aqueous solution of **basic fuchsine** (also called magenta or rosaniline hydrochloride; formulations rich in **pararosaniline hydrochloride** work best). It is deep magenta.
2. Pass **SO₂** through it, or add sulphurous acid or sodium bisulphite, until the colour disappears.
3. The colourless (or faintly yellow) solution is Schiff's reagent. Keep it cold and tightly closed, since loss of SO₂ restores the colour.

| Stage | What is present | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Basic fuchsine | Extended conjugated (quinoid) dye cation | Magenta |
| Schiff's reagent | Dye with HSO₃⁻ added at the central carbon | Colourless |
| After adding RCHO | Aldehyde–bisulphite–dye adduct with conjugation restored | Magenta-pink |

## Procedure

1. Take about 1 mL of Schiff's reagent in a test tube.
2. Add 2–3 drops of the sample (or its solution in water or ethanol) and shake.
3. Keep it at room temperature and watch for about a minute. Do not warm.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Aliphatic aldehyde (methanal, ethanal) | Pink-magenta colour quickly | Positive |
| Aromatic aldehyde (benzaldehyde) | Pink-magenta colour | Positive |
| Simple ketone (propanone) | Stays colourless (trace pink only after a long time) | Negative |
| Glucose | Stays colourless | Negative (cyclic hemiacetal) |
| Alcohols, acids, esters | Stays colourless | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

1. **Decolourisation.** Fuchsine is coloured because its central carbon is part of a long conjugated quinoid system. Bisulphite (from SO₂ and water) adds to that central carbon, breaking the conjugation, so the colour disappears.
2. **Reaction with the aldehyde.** The free –NH₂ groups of the decolourised dye condense with the aldehyde to form imines (Schiff bases) through a hemiaminal intermediate.
3. **Colour returns.** These imines add more bisulphite to give Ar–NH–CH(R)–SO₃⁻ groups. The resulting species restore an extended conjugated system and absorb in the visible region, giving the magenta colour of a positive test.

<Eq ce="\text{colourless reagent} + RCHO -> \text{magenta adduct}" />

<KeyPoint>
The magenta colour of a positive Schiff's test is not simply the original dye coming back. It belongs to a new aldehyde–bisulphite–dye adduct. For exam answers, "SO₂ decolourises the dye; the aldehyde restores a magenta colour" is enough.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip>
Glucose does not give Schiff's test even though its open-chain formula shows a –CHO group. This, together with its failure to form a bisulphite addition product, is classic evidence that glucose exists mainly as a cyclic hemiacetal.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Ketones react slowly and give the same colour, so the result must be read cold and quickly.
- Heating, or a reagent that has lost SO₂, can turn pink without any aldehyde.
- Aldehydes that form stable bisulphite adducts, such as glucose, can give false negatives.
- Schiff's test does not say whether the aldehyde is aliphatic or aromatic; use [Fehling's test](/tests/fehling-test/) for that.
