## What is the 2,4-DNP test?

The **2,4-DNP test** (Brady's test) detects the **carbonyl group of aldehydes and ketones**. The sample is shaken with **Brady's reagent**, an acidified alcoholic solution of **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH)**. Any aldehyde or ketone condenses with it to give a **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone**, which separates as a **yellow, orange or red precipitate**. Carboxylic acids, esters and amides do not respond, so the test is specific for aldehydes and ketones.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

**2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine** is a red-orange solid, C₆H₃(NO₂)₂NHNH₂: a hydrazine carrying a benzene ring with nitro groups at positions 2 and 4.

<Mol smiles="NNc1ccc(cc1[N+](=O)[O-])[N+](=O)[O-]" name="2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine" />

**Brady's reagent** is made by dissolving 2,4-DNPH in methanol (or ethanol and water) with a little concentrated sulphuric acid. The acid catalyses the condensation.

<ExamTip title="Safety">
Dry 2,4-DNPH is sensitive to shock and friction and can explode, so it is always stored and handled wet.
</ExamTip>

## Procedure

1. Take about 1 mL of Brady's reagent in a test tube.
2. Add 2–3 drops of the liquid sample, or a few mg of a solid dissolved in a little ethanol.
3. Shake and observe. A heavy, brightly coloured precipitate that forms at once is a positive result.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Aliphatic aldehyde or ketone (ethanal, propanone, butanone) | Yellow to orange precipitate | Positive: C=O of aldehyde/ketone |
| Aromatic or conjugated carbonyl (benzaldehyde, acetophenone, cinnamaldehyde) | Orange-red to red precipitate | Positive |
| Carboxylic acid, ester, amide | No precipitate; clear yellow-orange solution | Negative |
| Alcohol, ether, alkene | No precipitate | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

The 2,4-DNP test is a **nucleophilic addition–elimination** (condensation) reaction, the same type that forms oximes, hydrazones and semicarbazones:

1. **Activation.** The acid protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
2. **Addition.** The terminal –NH₂ of 2,4-DNPH attacks the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral carbinolamine intermediate.
3. **Elimination.** Water is lost, forming a C=N bond. The product is the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone.

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + H2N-NH-C6H3(NO2)2 -> CH3CH=N-NH-C6H3(NO2)2 v + H2O" />

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + H2N-NH-C6H3(NO2)2 -> C6H5CH=N-NH-C6H3(NO2)2 v + H2O" />

Acids, esters and amides fail because the lone pair on the attached O or N is delocalised into the C=O group. This resonance makes their carbonyl carbon less electrophilic, and adding the reagent would destroy that extra stability. A carboxylic acid also simply gives up its proton to form a carboxylate ion, which repels nucleophiles.

<KeyPoint>
2,4-DNP derivatives are crystalline solids with sharp, characteristic melting points. Before spectroscopy, an unknown aldehyde or ketone was identified by making its 2,4-DNP derivative and matching the melting point.
</KeyPoint>

## Using it in identification problems

A standard sequence in organic analysis questions:

1. **2,4-DNP positive** → the compound is an aldehyde or ketone.
2. **Tollens' or Fehling's test** → positive means aldehyde; negative means ketone.
3. **Iodoform test** → positive means a methyl ketone (CH₃CO–) or acetaldehyde.

For example, a compound C₈H₈O that gives an orange-red 2,4-DNP precipitate and a yellow iodoform precipitate, but does not reduce Tollens' or Fehling's reagent, is acetophenone (C₆H₅COCH₃).

## Limitations

- It does not separate aldehydes from ketones; both are positive.
- A few very hindered or highly enolised carbonyl compounds react slowly.
- Colour is only a rough guide to conjugation; a melting point of the derivative is needed for identification.
