## What is the ferric chloride test?

The **ferric chloride test** detects **phenols** and other compounds with a **stable enol** –OH group. A few drops of **neutral iron(III) chloride solution** are added to the sample. Phenols form an intensely coloured iron(III) complex: **phenol itself gives a violet colour**, while other phenols and enols give blue, green or red. Alcohols do not respond, and the solution keeps the pale yellow colour of FeCl₃.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

The reagent is **neutral ferric chloride**. Ordinary FeCl₃ solution is acidic because Fe³⁺ hydrolyses. To neutralise it:

1. Take dilute aqueous FeCl₃ solution.
2. Add dilute NaOH drop by drop until a small, permanent brown precipitate of iron(III) hydroxide appears.
3. Filter, and use the clear yellow filtrate.

Some lab manuals simply use a 5% aqueous FeCl₃ solution. For samples insoluble in water, ethanol–water mixtures are used, or dichloromethane with a little pyridine.

## Procedure

1. Dissolve a few drops (or about 30–50 mg) of the sample in 1–2 mL of water or aqueous ethanol.
2. Add neutral FeCl₃ solution dropwise and shake.
3. Note any colour change at once; some colours fade or darken on standing.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Example | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenol | C₆H₅OH | Violet colour | Phenolic –OH present |
| Cresols, resorcinol | o-, m-, p-cresol | Violet or blue colour | Phenolic –OH present |
| Catechol (benzene-1,2-diol) | — | Green colour that soon darkens | Phenolic –OH present |
| Compound with high enol content | Ethyl acetoacetate, pentane-2,4-dione | Coloured complex | Stable enol present |
| Naphthols | 1- and 2-naphthol | No characteristic colour | Test unreliable |
| Alcohol | Ethanol, cyclohexanol | Yellow colour of FeCl₃ remains | Negative |
| Simple ketone | Propanone | Yellow colour remains | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

Phenoxide oxygen atoms bind to Fe³⁺ as ligands. With phenol the complex is usually written as the hexaphenoxidoferrate(III) ion, and the protons released make the solution more acidic:

<Eq ce="6C6H5OH + FeCl3 -> [Fe(OC6H5)6]^3- + 3H+ + 3HCl" />

The exact structure of these complexes is still debated, and the colour depends on the phenol, the solvent and the concentration. That is why the observed colour can be red, violet, blue or green.

<KeyPoint>
The ferric chloride test is really a test for an **–OH group on an sp² carbon of a C=C**: the enol unit. Phenol is the most familiar stable enol, because its "keto" form would destroy the aromatic ring. Simple aldehydes and ketones exist almost entirely as the keto form, so they do not respond; β-dicarbonyl compounds have a large enol content and do respond.
</KeyPoint>

Besides phenols and enols, hydroxamic acids, oximes and sulfinic acids also give positive results. This is why the ester (hydroxamic acid) test is always preceded by a check with FeCl₃ alone.

<ExamTip>
To distinguish phenol from ethanol (or from benzyl alcohol, C₆H₅CH₂OH), use neutral FeCl₃: only phenol turns violet. Benzyl alcohol has its –OH on an sp³ carbon, so it behaves as an alcohol, not as a phenol. Bromine water (white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol) is a useful second test.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Not every phenol gives a colour.** Naphthols do not give characteristic colours, so a negative result does not rule out a phenol.
- **Enols give false positives** for phenol: compounds with a high enol content, such as β-keto esters, also form coloured complexes.
- The colour varies with the compound, solvent and concentration, so it cannot identify a particular phenol on its own.
- Excess reagent or excess acid can mask the colour; neutral FeCl₃ gives the clearest results.
- Spectroscopic methods (IR, NMR) are far more reliable for structure determination; the FeCl₃ test is a quick classification test.
