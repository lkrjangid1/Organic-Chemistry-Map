## What is the sodium metal test?

The **sodium metal test** detects an **acidic (active) hydrogen** in an organic compound. A small piece of sodium is dropped into the dry sample; if the compound has a hydrogen that sodium can replace, it forms a sodium salt and **hydrogen gas is evolved as brisk effervescence**. Alcohols, phenols, carboxylic acids and terminal alkynes give a positive result, while ethers, alkanes and alkenes do not.

<Eq ce="2R-OH + 2Na -> 2R-ONa + H2 ^" />

## Reagent and how to prepare it

The reagent is simply **metallic sodium**. A pea-sized or smaller piece is cut fresh (sodium is stored under kerosene or paraffin oil), blotted free of oil and used at once. No solution is prepared.

The sample and the test tube must be **completely dry**. Water also reacts with sodium and releases hydrogen, so a wet sample would give a false positive.

## Procedure

1. Take about 1 mL of the dry liquid (or a solution of a solid in a dry, unreactive solvent) in a dry test tube.
2. Add a small, freshly cut piece of sodium.
3. Watch for bubbles. Bring a burning splint to the mouth of the tube: hydrogen burns with a soft pop.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Example | Observation | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | Ethanol | Effervescence of H₂; sodium dissolves to give sodium ethoxide | Positive |
| Phenol | Phenol | Effervescence of H₂; sodium phenoxide forms | Positive |
| Carboxylic acid | Ethanoic acid | Vigorous effervescence of H₂ | Positive |
| Terminal alkyne | Ethyne | H₂ evolved; sodium acetylide forms | Positive |
| Ether | Diethyl ether | No reaction | Negative |
| Alkane, alkene | Hexane, ethene | No reaction | Negative |

## Chemistry behind it

Sodium is a strong reducing metal. It transfers an electron to the acidic X–H bond, the hydrogen leaves as H₂, and the sodium salt of the conjugate base remains.

**Alcohols** form sodium alkoxides:

<Eq ce="2CH3CH2OH + 2Na -> 2CH3CH2ONa + H2 ^" />

**Phenols** form sodium phenoxides. Phenols are more acidic than alcohols, and unlike alcohols they also dissolve in aqueous NaOH:

<Eq ce="2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5ONa + H2 ^" />

**Carboxylic acids** react in the same way to give carboxylate salts. Like alcohols, they evolve hydrogen with electropositive metals:

<Eq ce="2CH3COOH + 2Na -> 2CH3COONa + H2 ^" />

**Terminal alkynes** have a weakly acidic hydrogen on the sp-hybridised carbon (50% s character holds the C–H electron pair close to carbon). Ethyne gives sodium acetylide, but ethene and ethane give no reaction:

<Eq ce="2HC#CH + 2Na -> 2HC#CNa + H2 ^" />

<KeyPoint>
The sodium test works only for compounds with an **acidic hydrogen**. It confirms an –OH, –COOH or ≡C–H group, but it does not tell which one: a positive result must be followed by more specific tests.
</KeyPoint>

The alkoxides made this way are useful reagents in their own right. Sodium ethoxide, for example, is the nucleophile in the [Williamson ether synthesis](/reactions/williamson-ether-synthesis/).

<ExamTip>
A favourite question pairs functional isomers, such as butan-1-ol and diethyl ether (both C₄H₁₀O). Add sodium: the alcohol gives effervescence of H₂, the ether does not. Write the balanced equation with 2 mol of alcohol giving 1 mol of H₂.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Moisture gives a false positive**, because water reacts with sodium. Dry samples and glassware are essential.
- The test is **not specific**: alcohols, phenols, carboxylic acids and terminal alkynes all respond. Use the [ferric chloride test](/tests/ferric-chloride-test/) for phenols, the sodium hydrogencarbonate test (CO₂ effervescence) for carboxylic acids and the [CAN test](/tests/ceric-ammonium-nitrate-test/) for alcohols.
- It does not separate primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols in a reliable way; use the [Lucas test](/tests/lucas-test/) or the [Victor Meyer test](/tests/victor-meyer-test/) for that.
- Sodium is hazardous: it reacts violently with water, and the hydrogen produced is flammable.
