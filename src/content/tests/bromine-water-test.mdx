## What is the bromine water test?

The **bromine water test** uses the **orange-brown colour of Br₂** as an indicator. The colour disappears when bromine is consumed: by **addition** across C=C or C≡C bonds, by **substitution** on strongly activated rings such as phenol and aniline (which also gives a white precipitate), or by **oxidation** of an aldehyde group. In carbohydrate chemistry it is the standard way to tell an **aldose (glucose)** from a **ketose (fructose)**: glucose is oxidised to gluconic acid, fructose does not react.

## Reagent and how to prepare it

- **Bromine water**: a dilute solution of bromine in water, orange-brown. It is mildly acidic (Br₂ + H₂O gives some HOBr and HBr) and is a mild oxidising agent.
- For water-insoluble alkenes, bromine dissolved in an organic solvent (CCl₄ or CH₂Cl₂) is used instead; the colour change is the same.

## Procedure

1. Dissolve a little of the sample in water (or in CH₂Cl₂ if it is insoluble in water).
2. Add bromine water drop by drop and shake.
3. Watch whether the orange colour disappears at once, slowly, or not at all, and whether a precipitate forms.

## Observations and inference

| Compound type | Observation | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene, alkyne | Orange colour disappears at once | Electrophilic addition |
| Phenol | Decolourised, white precipitate | 2,4,6-Tribromophenol formed |
| Aniline | Decolourised, white precipitate | 2,4,6-Tribromoaniline formed |
| Glucose (aldose) | Decolourised (slowly) | –CHO oxidised to –COOH |
| Fructose (ketose) | Colour stays | No reaction |
| Simple aliphatic aldehyde | Decolourised within about a minute | Oxidation |
| Alkane, benzene, acetophenone | Colour stays | No reaction |

## Chemistry behind it

**1. Unsaturation (addition).** One Br₂ adds across each π bond, so an alkene takes up one Br₂ and an alkyne two:

<Eq ce="CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> BrCH2-CH2Br" />

<Eq ce="HC#CH + 2Br2 -> Br2CH-CHBr2" />

**2. Phenol and aniline (substitution).** The OH and NH₂ groups activate the ring so strongly that all free ortho and para positions are brominated without a catalyst:

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH v + 3HBr" />

**3. Aldose oxidation (glucose vs fructose).** Bromine water oxidises the aldehyde group of an aldose to a carboxylic acid (an **aldonic acid**) without touching the secondary and primary alcohol groups:

<Eq ce="CHO-(CHOH)4-CH2OH + Br2 + H2O -> COOH-(CHOH)4-CH2OH + 2HBr" />

Fructose has a ketone group in place of the aldehyde. In Tollens' and Fehling's tests, the **base** in the reagent isomerises fructose to aldoses, so fructose looks like a reducing sugar there. Bromine water contains no base, so that isomerisation does not occur and fructose is not oxidised.

<KeyPoint>
Glucose and fructose both reduce Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, so those tests cannot separate them. Bromine water can: glucose decolourises it (forming gluconic acid), fructose does not.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not list aromatic aldehydes as reliably positive in the bromine test. In lab testing, conjugated aldehydes such as benzaldehyde react poorly, and NCERT notes that an aromatic ring by itself does not decolourise bromine water. A molecule like acetophenone, which is unsaturated only because of its benzene ring, gives a negative result.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Bromine water is decolourised by several functional groups (alkenes, alkynes, phenols, anilines, enols, aldehydes), so a positive result needs a second test to pin down the group. Baeyer's reagent ([KMnO₄](/reagents/potassium-permanganate/)) is a common companion test for unsaturation.
- Some hindered or electron-poor alkenes react slowly or not at all.
- Aldehydes react more slowly than alkenes; conjugated aldehydes may not react.
- Bromine is toxic and corrosive; the test should be done in a fume cupboard.
