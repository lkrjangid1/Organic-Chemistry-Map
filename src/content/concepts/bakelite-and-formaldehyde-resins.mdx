## What are bakelite, melamine and urea–formaldehyde resins?

**Formaldehyde resins** are **condensation polymers** made by heating formaldehyde (HCHO) with a compound that has several reactive H atoms: **phenol** gives novolac and **bakelite**, **melamine** gives **melamine–formaldehyde resin (Melmac)**, and **urea** gives **urea–formaldehyde resin**. Each step joins two units through a **–CH₂– bridge** and releases water. Because every monomer can react at three or more sites, the final products are **cross-linked, thermosetting** solids that do not melt again on heating.

<KeyPoint>
Phenol in excess (F:P less than 1, acid) → linear **novolac** (thermoplastic). Novolac + more HCHO, heat → cross-linked **bakelite** (thermoset). Melamine + HCHO → **Melmac** (unbreakable crockery). Urea + HCHO → **urea–formaldehyde resin** (laminated sheets). All three are condensation polymers.
</KeyPoint>

## Phenol–formaldehyde resins: novolac and bakelite

The reaction is an electrophilic substitution on the activated phenol ring at the **ortho and para positions**, followed by condensation.

1. **Hydroxymethylation.** Formaldehyde attacks the ring to give o- and p-hydroxymethylphenol. Under base this step resembles an aldol addition of the phenoxide to HCHO.

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + HCHO ->[H+ or OH-] HOC6H4CH2OH" />

2. **Methylene bridge formation.** The –CH₂OH group reacts with another phenol ring, losing water and forming a –CH₂– link between two rings.

<Eq ce="HOC6H4CH2OH + C6H5OH -> HOC6H4-CH2-C6H4OH + H2O" />

3. **Chain growth.** Repeating step 2 gives chains of phenol rings joined by –CH₂– at the 2- and 4-positions. When each ring uses only two positions the chain stays **linear**: this is **novolac**.

4. **Cross-linking.** Heating novolac with more formaldehyde (in practice, hexamethylenetetramine as the formaldehyde source) uses the third free ortho/para position on each ring. –CH₂– bridges now link neighbouring chains into a three-dimensional network: **bakelite**.

| Property | Novolac | Bakelite |
|---|---|---|
| Formaldehyde : phenol ratio | Less than 1 (phenol in excess) | Extra HCHO added (resole route: F:P about 1.5, base) |
| Structure | Linear or lightly branched | Cross-linked 3D network |
| Behaviour on heating | Softens (thermoplastic prepolymer) | Does not soften (thermoset) |
| Uses | Prepolymer for moulding; binders, varnishes; in exam lists, paints | Electrical switches, plugs, saucepan handles, moulded parts; in exam lists also combs |

When the F:P ratio reaches about one, every phenol ring can in principle be linked into one giant molecule. That is why a novolac (F:P less than 1) cannot harden until a formaldehyde source is added, while a base-catalysed **resole** (formaldehyde in excess) already carries spare –CH₂OH groups and cures on heating alone.

Bakelite, patented by Leo Baekeland in 1909, was the **first fully synthetic thermosetting plastic**. It is hard, insoluble and a good electrical and heat insulator, which explains its use in switches and electrical fittings.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Novolac is formed with **phenol in excess** (P/F greater than 1), not formaldehyde in excess. Bakelite is the **cross-linked** product; it is a thermoset and cannot be remoulded. A typical NCERT question: "Phenol and formaldehyde give polymer A, which on heating with formaldehyde gives thermosetting polymer B." A = novolac, B = bakelite.
</ExamTip>

## Melamine–formaldehyde resin (Melmac)

**Melamine** is 2,4,6-triamino-1,3,5-triazine: a six-membered ring of alternating C and N atoms with an –NH₂ group on each carbon. Each –NH₂ can react with formaldehyde, so melamine is a highly polyfunctional monomer.

<Eq ce="R-NH2 + HCHO -> R-NH-CH2OH" />

<Eq ce="R-NH-CH2OH + H2N-R -> R-NH-CH2-NH-R + H2O" />

The –NH–CH₂–NH– (and –NH–CH₂–O–CH₂–NH–) links join triazine rings into a hard, cross-linked thermoset. Uses: **unbreakable crockery** and tableware (sold as Melmac), and the surface layer of high-pressure decorative laminates.

## Urea–formaldehyde resin

Urea, NH₂CONH₂, has four N–H bonds. It first adds to HCHO to give methylolurea, which condenses with further urea or methylol groups:

<Eq ce="NH2CONH2 + HCHO -> NH2CONHCH2OH" />

<Eq ce="n NH2CONHCH2OH -> -(NH-CO-NH-CH2)_{n}- + n H2O" />

The linear repeat unit –[NH–CO–NH–CH₂]ₙ– is what exam answers write; the real resin is further cross-linked through the remaining N–H bonds. Uses: **laminated sheets** and wood laminates, plywood and particle-board adhesive, and moulded articles; exam lists also mention **unbreakable cups**.

## Comparison of formaldehyde resins

| Resin | Monomers | Link between units | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novolac | Phenol (excess) + HCHO, acid | Aryl–CH₂–aryl, linear | Prepolymer for bakelite |
| Bakelite | Novolac + HCHO, heat | Aryl–CH₂–aryl, cross-linked | Switches, plugs, handles |
| Melmac | Melamine + HCHO | –NH–CH₂–NH– | Unbreakable crockery, laminates |
| Urea–formaldehyde | Urea + HCHO | –NH–CH₂–NH– | Laminated sheets, wood adhesives |

## Exam tips

- All formaldehyde resins are **condensation (step-growth) polymers**: water is lost at every bridging step.
- Bakelite and melamine resin are **thermosetting**; novolac on its own is thermoplastic.
- Phenol reacts at **ortho and para** positions (the –OH group activates them), so each ring has up to three reactive sites, which makes cross-linking possible.
- Matching questions: bakelite → electrical switches; melamine–formaldehyde → unbreakable crockery; urea–formaldehyde → laminated sheets.
