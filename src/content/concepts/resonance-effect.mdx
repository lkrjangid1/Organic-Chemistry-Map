## What is the resonance (mesomeric) effect?

**Resonance** describes a molecule whose real structure cannot be drawn with one Lewis structure; the true structure is a **resonance hybrid** of several canonical (contributing) structures that differ only in the placement of π electrons and lone pairs. The **mesomeric or resonance effect** (M or R effect) is the permanent polarity this delocalisation creates: a substituent either **releases** electrons into a conjugated system (**+M**) or **withdraws** electrons from it (**−M**).

Benzene is the classic case: both C–C bond lengths are equal (139 pm), between a C–C single bond (154 pm) and a C=C double bond (134 pm), because the molecule is a hybrid of two Kekulé structures.

## Conditions for resonance

Resonance (conjugation) is possible when a p orbital can overlap with an adjacent p orbital in a planar arrangement:

| System | Example |
|---|---|
| C=C next to a positive charge | allyl cation CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺ |
| C=C next to a negative charge | allyl anion |
| C=C next to an unpaired electron | allyl radical |
| C=C next to another π bond (=, ≡) | buta-1,3-diene, acrolein |
| lone pair next to a π bond | vinyl ethers, aniline, phenol |
| lone pair next to a vacant p orbital | CH₃O–CH₂⁺, BF₃ (F lone pair to empty B 2p) |

Rules for writing canonical structures:
1. Only electrons move; the **positions of all nuclei stay the same**.
2. All structures have the **same number of unpaired electrons**.
3. Each structure must obey normal valency (no five-bonded carbon).
4. The hybrid is more stable than any single contributor; the energy difference is the **resonance energy**.

## +M and −M groups

| Effect | Groups (typical order, strongest first) | Effect on the ring |
|---|---|---|
| +M | −O⁻ > −NH₂ > −NHR > −NR₂ > −OH > −OR > −NHCOR > −OCOR > −C₆H₅ > −F > −Cl > −Br > −I | electron density raised at ortho and para |
| −M | −NO₂ > −CN > −SO₃H > −CHO > −COR > −COOR > −COOH > −CONH₂ > −COO⁻ | electron density lowered at ortho and para |

In aniline, the nitrogen lone pair is pushed into the ring (+M), making ortho and para positions electron rich; in nitrobenzene the ring π electrons are pulled towards the nitro group (−M), leaving ortho and para positions electron poor. This is the basis of directing effects in [electrophilic aromatic substitution](/mechanisms/electrophilic-aromatic-substitution/).

<KeyPoint title="Meta position">
A substituent cannot place its charge on the meta carbon through resonance; at meta only the [inductive effect](/concepts/inductive-effect/) operates. For example, in nitrobenzyl cations the meta isomer is the least destabilised, giving the exam order benzyl⁺ > m-nitrobenzyl⁺ > p-nitrobenzyl⁺ > o-nitrobenzyl⁺.
</KeyPoint>

## Stability of resonating structures

Contributors are not equally important. Use these criteria, roughly in decreasing priority:

| Criterion | More stable structure |
|---|---|
| Aromaticity | keeps an aromatic (4n + 2) π ring intact; an anti-aromatic (4n) ring is strongly destabilising |
| Octet rule | every second-period atom has a complete octet |
| Number of bonds | more covalent bonds |
| Charge | no charge, or the least separation of opposite charges |
| Electronegativity | negative charge on the more electronegative atom, positive charge on the more electropositive atom |
| Like charges | far apart (like charges close together are unfavourable) |
| Geometry | no strained or unrealistic bond lengths and angles |

For whole molecules: **aromatic > homoaromatic > non-aromatic > anti-aromatic**. Equivalent contributors (as in carboxylate or benzene) give a large resonance energy. For polycyclic aromatics, the structure with the most benzene-like sextets contributes most (often taught as the **Fries rule**; compare Clar's rule).

<ExamTip>
In CH₂=CH–CHO, the neutral structure is the major contributor. Of the two charge-separated forms, ⁺CH₂–CH=CH–O⁻ is better than ⁻CH₂–CH=CH–O⁺, because the negative charge sits on oxygen; the second form also leaves O with an incomplete octet.
</ExamTip>

## Bond order from resonance structures

Bond order = total number of bonds between two atoms in all contributors ÷ number of contributors.

| Species | Bond | Calculation | Bond order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benzene | C–C | (2 + 1) ÷ 2 | 1.5 |
| Carboxylate, RCOO⁻ | C–O | (2 + 1) ÷ 2 | 1.5 |
| Nitrite, NO₂⁻ | N–O | (2 + 1) ÷ 2 | 1.5 |
| Nitrate, NO₃⁻ | N–O | (2 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3 | 1.33 |
| Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | C–O | (2 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3 | 1.33 |

Higher bond order means a shorter bond: the C–O bond in carbonate is longer than the C=O bond in carbon dioxide.

## Cross conjugation

In a **cross-conjugated** system, three π units are arranged so that two of them are each conjugated with the middle one but not with each other (divinyl ketone, benzophenone, p-benzoquinone). Delocalisation is incomplete, so cross conjugation gives **less stabilisation** than linear conjugation. One example: in benzoic acid the ring competes with the OH oxygen for conjugation with the C=O group, which is one reason benzoic acid is weaker than formic acid.

## Resonance vs inductive effect

| Feature | Resonance effect | Inductive effect |
|---|---|---|
| Electrons | π electrons and lone pairs | σ electrons |
| Range | the whole conjugated system | about three bonds |
| Strength | usually stronger | weaker |
| Needs | planarity and p-orbital overlap | only a polar bond |

When the two effects oppose each other, resonance usually wins (−OH and −NH₂ are activating despite −I), except for halogens, where −I dominates for reactivity. Bulky ortho groups can twist a group out of the ring plane and switch resonance off; see [steric inhibition of resonance](/concepts/steric-inhibition-of-resonance/). Also compare [hyperconjugation](/concepts/hyperconjugation/), sometimes called no-bond resonance.
