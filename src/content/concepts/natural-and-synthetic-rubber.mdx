## What is natural and synthetic rubber?

**Rubber** is an **elastomer**: a polymer whose chains are held by weak forces, so it stretches under a force and springs back when the force is removed. **Natural rubber** is **cis-1,4-polyisoprene**, obtained from the latex of the rubber tree *Hevea brasiliensis*. **Synthetic rubbers** are man-made elastomers, mostly addition polymers or copolymers of dienes such as **buta-1,3-diene** and **chloroprene** (neoprene, Buna-S, Buna-N, polybutadiene), plus a few step-growth elastomers such as thiokol and polyurethanes.

<KeyPoint>
Natural rubber = **cis**-polyisoprene (elastic). Gutta-percha = **trans**-polyisoprene (hard, crystalline). Vulcanisation = heating with **sulphur** (373–415 K) to cross-link chains at allylic positions.
</KeyPoint>

## Natural rubber

Isoprene units add **1,4** (head-to-tail), leaving one C=C in each repeat unit:

<Eq ce="n CH2=C(CH3)-CH=CH2 -> -(CH2-C(CH3)=CH-CH2)_{n}-" />

- **Source:** milky latex tapped from the bark of *Hevea brasiliensis*; in the tree the chain is built from isopentenyl pyrophosphate, not free isoprene.
- **cis vs trans:** the cis double bonds bend the chain, so chains coil and cannot crystallise; this gives elasticity. **Gutta-percha** (trans) packs well and is a hard, non-elastic thermoplastic used for golf-ball covers, cable insulation and dental fillings.
- **Drawbacks of raw rubber:** sticky when warm, brittle when cold, swells in non-polar solvents, attacked by oxidising agents.

## Vulcanisation

Heating rubber with **sulphur** (plus an accelerator) between about **373 and 415 K** forms **–S–S– bridges** between chains. The reactive sites are the **allylic C–H bonds** next to each double bond.

| Effect of cross-links | Result |
|---|---|
| Chains can no longer slide past each other | Higher tensile strength, less permanent stretching |
| Fewer free allylic sites | Better resistance to oxidation and solvents |
| Property range widens | Elastic over a wider temperature range, not sticky |

The amount of sulphur sets the stiffness: about 5% gives soft rubber for bands, about 30% gives hard rubber such as battery cases. Charles Goodyear discovered the process in 1839.

## Synthetic rubbers

| Rubber | Monomer(s) | Repeat unit | Key property, uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neoprene (polychloroprene) | Chloroprene, CH₂=C(Cl)–CH=CH₂ | –CH₂–C(Cl)=CH–CH₂– | Resists oil, heat, weather; gaskets, hoses, belts, wetsuits |
| Buna-S (SBR) | Buta-1,3-diene + styrene | –CH₂–CH=CH–CH₂–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)– | Tough, abrasion resistant; car tyres, shoe soles |
| Buna-N (nitrile rubber, NBR) | Buta-1,3-diene + acrylonitrile | –CH₂–CH=CH–CH₂–CH₂–CH(CN)– | Oil and fuel resistant; oil seals, hoses, gaskets, fuel-tank linings |
| Polyisobutylene / butyl rubber | Isobutylene (with a little isoprene in butyl rubber) | –CH₂–C(CH₃)₂– | Very low gas permeability; tyre inner tubes, ball bladders |
| Polybutadiene | Buta-1,3-diene | –CH₂–CH=CH–CH₂– | Stays flexible at low temperature, wear resistant; tyres, golf-ball cores |
| ABS | Acrylonitrile + butadiene + styrene | Rubbery polybutadiene with poly(styrene-acrylonitrile) grafts | Tough, rigid plastic; car body parts, housings, Lego bricks |

<Eq ce="n CH2=C(Cl)-CH=CH2 -> -(CH2-C(Cl)=CH-CH2)_{n}-" />

<Eq ce="n CH2=CH-CH=CH2 + n C6H5CH=CH2 -> -(CH2-CH=CH-CH2-CH2-CH(C6H5))_{n}-" />

<Eq ce="n CH2=CH-CH=CH2 + n CH2=CHCN -> -(CH2-CH=CH-CH2-CH2-CH(CN))_{n}-" />

**Buna** comes from **Bu**tadiene + **Na** (sodium, the catalyst used in the first German process); S = styrene, N = nitrile. Modern SBR is made mainly by free-radical emulsion polymerisation. Exam lists usually give Buna-S as about 3 : 1 butadiene : styrene. ABS proportions vary widely: roughly 15–35% acrylonitrile, 5–30% butadiene and 40–60% styrene.

## Thiokol (polysulphide rubber)

Thiokol is a **condensation polymer** of 1,2-dichloroethane and sodium polysulphide; NaCl is eliminated:

<Eq ce="n ClCH2CH2Cl + n Na2S4 -> -(CH2-CH2-S-S-S-S)_{n}- + 2n NaCl" />

It resists oils and solvents and is used in sealants and, historically, rocket propellant binders.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Older books draw thiokol as –CH₂–CH₂–S(=S)–S(=S)–. The accepted structure is a **linear chain of sulphur atoms**, –CH₂–CH₂–S–S–S–S–, joined to the CH₂ groups.
</ExamTip>

## Polyurethanes

A diol (for example butane-1,4-diol) adds to a diisocyanate (commonly toluene diisocyanate). Each –OH adds across an N=C bond, forming a **urethane (carbamate) link –NH–CO–O–**:

<Eq ce="n HO(CH2)4OH + n OCN-R-NCO -> -(O-(CH2)4-O-CO-NH-R-NH-CO)_{n}-" />

Polyurethanes are **step-growth** polymers, but nothing is eliminated, so strictly they are **polyaddition** products. Many exam lists group them with condensation polymers because they grow step by step. Uses: flexible and rigid **foams** (mattresses, seats, insulation), elastic fibres (spandex), coatings and adhesives.

## Exam tips

- Diene polymers (natural rubber, neoprene, Buna-S, Buna-N, polybutadiene) keep **one C=C per diene unit**, so they can be vulcanised.
- Rubbers are **addition** polymers; thiokol is a **condensation** polymer.
- Natural rubber is not a semisynthetic polymer; **vulcanised rubber** is often classed as semisynthetic.
- Neoprene is the chlorine analogue of natural rubber: Cl replaces the CH₃ of isoprene.
