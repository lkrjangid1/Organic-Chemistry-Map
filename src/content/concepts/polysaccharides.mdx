## What are polysaccharides?

**Polysaccharides** are carbohydrates made of a **very large number of monosaccharide units** joined by glycosidic linkages, with the general formula **(C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ** when the units are glucose. They are not sweet, are mostly insoluble in water and act as **food stores** (starch in plants, glycogen in animals) or **structural material** (cellulose in plant cell walls). All three exam polysaccharides are polymers of **D-glucose**; they differ in the **anomer (α or β)** used and in **branching**.

## Comparison at a glance

| Polysaccharide | Monomer | Main linkage | Branch linkage | Shape | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amylose (15–20% of starch) | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) | none | Unbranched, coils into a helix | Plant food store |
| Amylopectin (80–85% of starch) | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) | α(1→6), roughly every 25–30 units | Branched | Plant food store |
| Glycogen | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) | α(1→6), about every 8–12 units | Highly branched, compact | Animal food store |
| Cellulose | β-D-glucose | β(1→4) | none | Straight, unbranched chains | Plant cell walls |

<KeyPoint>
Starch and glycogen are α-glucose polymers; cellulose is a β-glucose polymer. Glycogen is "like amylopectin, but more branched". Cellulose is the only one of the four with no branching and only β links.
</KeyPoint>

## Starch

Starch is the main **storage polysaccharide of plants** and the most important carbohydrate in the human diet (cereals, potatoes, roots and tubers). It is a mixture of two components:

- **Amylose**, about **15–20%** of starch: a long **unbranched** chain of roughly 200–1000 α-D-glucose units joined by **C1–C4** glycosidic links. It is the water-soluble component and gives the deep **blue** colour with iodine.
- **Amylopectin**, about **80–85%** of starch: chains of α-D-glucose joined by **C1–C4** links, with **branches** attached through **C1–C6** links. It is insoluble in water and gives only a reddish-brown colour with iodine.

The exact ratio depends on the plant; many starches contain 20–25% amylose. Starch as a whole does not dissolve in cold water, and in hot water it swells to a paste.

### Hydrolysis of starch

Starch is broken down stepwise. With dilute acid on heating, long chains are cut into shorter fragments called **dextrins**, then into maltose, and finally into **D-glucose**:

<Eq ce="(C6H10O5)_n (starch) ->[dil. H+][\Delta] dextrins ->[dil. H+][\Delta] C12H22O11 (maltose) ->[dil. H+][\Delta] C6H12O6 (glucose)" />

The enzymatic route is used in brewing and in digestion. **Diastase** (an amylase from malt) converts starch into maltose, and **maltase** converts maltose into glucose:

<Eq ce="(C6H10O5)_n (starch) ->[diastase][H2O] C12H22O11 (maltose) ->[maltase][H2O] 2C6H12O6 (glucose)" />

Complete hydrolysis of starch is how commercial glucose (dextrose) is made.

## Glycogen

Glycogen is the storage carbohydrate of animals and is called **animal starch**. It is found mainly in the **liver and muscles** (also in the brain), and in yeast and fungi. It is a **branched polymer of α-D-glucose**: α(1→4) chains with α(1→6) branch points. Its structure resembles amylopectin but it is **more highly branched**, with only about 8–12 glucose units between branches, which makes the molecule compact and lets enzymes release glucose quickly from many chain ends. A glycogen molecule contains a few thousand to tens of thousands of glucose units.

## Cellulose

Cellulose occurs only in plants and is the **most abundant organic substance in nature**. It is the main constituent of plant cell walls; cotton fibre is about 90% cellulose and wood about 40–50%.

- It is a **straight, unbranched** chain of **β-D-glucose** units joined by **C1–C4 (β)** glycosidic links. Neighbouring chains hydrogen-bond into strong fibres.
- Partial hydrolysis gives the disaccharide **cellobiose**; complete hydrolysis gives glucose.
- **Humans cannot digest cellulose** because they lack an enzyme that hydrolyses β-glycosidic bonds. In cattle and other ruminants, bacteria in the rumen produce **cellulase** and break cellulose down; termites rely on gut microbes in the same way.

### Useful derivatives of cellulose

Each glucose unit of cellulose has three free OH groups that can be esterified:

| Reagent | Product | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | Cellulose nitrate; highly nitrated form (near trinitrate) = **gun cotton** | Explosive, smokeless powder |
| Acetic anhydride (with acetic acid) | Cellulose acetate / **cellulose triacetate** | Films, fibres (acetate rayon) |

<Eq ce="[C6H7O2(OH)3]_n + 3n HNO3 ->[conc. H2SO4] [C6H7O2(ONO2)3]_n + 3n H2O" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Glycogen branches far more often than amylopectin: about every 8–12 glucose units, compared with about every 25–30 in amylopectin. Also remember that cattle do not make cellulase themselves; the microorganisms in their gut do.
</ExamTip>

## Solved examples

**1. Which polysaccharide gives cellobiose on partial hydrolysis?**
Cellulose, because it is a β(1→4) polymer of glucose and cellobiose is its β(1→4) disaccharide unit.

**2. Arrange amylose, amylopectin and glycogen in increasing order of branching.**
Amylose (none) less than amylopectin less than glycogen.

**3. Which component of starch is water-soluble, and what are the linkages in it?**
Amylose; only α(1→4) glycosidic links.
