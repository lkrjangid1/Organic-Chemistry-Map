## What is the inductive effect?

The **inductive effect** is the permanent displacement of σ-bond electrons along a carbon chain caused by an atom or group of different electronegativity. A group that pulls electron density towards itself shows a **−I effect**; a group that pushes electron density into the chain shows a **+I effect**. The polarisation is relayed from bond to bond, becomes weaker at every step and is practically zero after about three bonds.

In chloroethane, the C–Cl bond is polarised so that C-1 carries a partial positive charge (δ+). C-1 then pulls some electron density from the C-1–C-2 bond, so C-2 gets a smaller positive charge (δδ+). The shift is shown by an arrow pointing along the bond towards the electron-attracting atom.

## −I and +I groups

**Rule of thumb:** electron-withdrawing and positively charged groups are −I; electron-donating and negatively charged groups are +I. Hydrogen is the reference (zero).

| Effect | Groups, strongest first |
|---|---|
| −I | −NR₃⁺, −NH₃⁺ > −NO₂ > −SO₂R > −SO₃H > −CN > −CHO > −COR > −COOH > −F > −Cl > −Br > −I > −OR > −OH > −NH₂ > −C₆H₅ > −CH=CH₂ > −H |
| +I | −O⁻ > −COO⁻ > −C(CH₃)₃ > −CH(CH₃)₂ > −CH₂CH₃ > −CH₃ > −H |
| Isotopes (+I) | −T > −D > −H |

<KeyPoint title="Hybridisation and −I effect">
For the same element, electronegativity rises with s-character: **sp > sp² > sp³**. So an sp carbon (alkynyl, −C≡CH) withdraws electrons more strongly than an sp² carbon (vinyl, phenyl), which in turn is more electronegative than an sp³ carbon. This is why −C₆H₅ and −CH=CH₂ appear in the −I list while alkyl groups are +I.
</KeyPoint>

A charged group dominates: −NH₃⁺ and −NR₃⁺ are the strongest −I groups, and −O⁻ and −COO⁻ are the strongest +I groups.

## Distance dependence

Because each bond transmits only part of the polarisation, the inductive effect falls off steeply with the number of intervening bonds:

| Acid | Position of Cl | pKa |
|---|---|---|
| 2-Chlorobutanoic acid | α (next to COOH) | 2.86 |
| 3-Chlorobutanoic acid | β | 4.05 |
| 4-Chlorobutanoic acid | γ | 4.52 (close to butanoic acid) |

<ExamTip>
When asked "in which bond is the inductive effect least?", pick the bond **farthest** from the polarising group. In CH₃CH₂CH₂Br it is the bond at the far end of the chain from bromine.
</ExamTip>

## Effect on acidity

An acid is stronger when its conjugate base is more stable. A −I group spreads the negative charge of a carboxylate or alkoxide and makes the acid stronger; a +I group concentrates the charge and makes the acid weaker.

| Acid | pKa | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| CF₃COOH | about 0.2 (Ka 0.59) | three strongly −I fluorines |
| ClCH₂COOH | 2.82 | −I of Cl |
| HCOOH | 3.74 | no alkyl group |
| HOCH₂COOH (glycolic acid) | 3.83 | −I of O |
| CH₃COOH | 4.76 | +I of CH₃ |

Two general results follow:
- **More −I groups, stronger acid:** Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH.
- **More electronegative halogen, stronger acid:** FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH > ICH₂COOH.

The same logic explains why benzoic acid (pKa 4.20, ring carbon sp²) is stronger than cyclohexanecarboxylic acid (pKa 4.87, sp³ carbon), and why 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol is far more acidic than ethanol. Full orders are collected on the [acidic strength order](/comparisons/acidic-strength-order/) and [acidity of carboxylic acids](/comparisons/acidity-of-carboxylic-acids/) pages.

## Effect on basicity and on intermediates

- **Basicity:** +I groups push electron density onto the nitrogen lone pair, so alkylamines are more basic than ammonia; −I groups (for example in CF₃CH₂NH₂) lower basicity. In water, solvation also matters, which is why the order of methylamines is not simply 3° > 2° > 1° (see [basic strength of amines](/comparisons/basic-strength-of-amines/)).
- **Carbocations:** +I alkyl groups disperse positive charge, so stability is 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺ (together with [hyperconjugation](/concepts/hyperconjugation/)).
- **Carbanions:** +I groups intensify negative charge, so the order reverses: CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°.

More on intermediates: [stability of reaction intermediates](/comparisons/stability-of-reaction-intermediates/).

## Exceptions: strong organic acids

Most organic acids are weak compared with mineral acids such as HCl or H₂SO₄. Two organic acids are unusually strong and are favourite exam exceptions:

| Acid | pKa | Why so strong |
|---|---|---|
| Picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol) | 0.38 | three −I/−M nitro groups delocalise the phenoxide charge |
| Squaric acid (3,4-dihydroxycyclobut-3-ene-1,2-dione) | pKa₁ about 1.5, pKa₂ about 3.4 | the anion is highly resonance-stabilised; the dianion is fully symmetrical |

These cases show that the inductive effect rarely acts alone: [resonance](/concepts/resonance-effect/) often contributes more.

## Inductive effect vs other electronic effects

| Feature | Inductive effect | Resonance (mesomeric) effect | Electromeric effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrons involved | σ-bond electrons | π electrons or lone pairs | π electrons of a multiple bond |
| Nature | permanent | permanent | temporary (needs a reagent) |
| Range | dies out after about 3 bonds | transmitted along the whole conjugated system | only the multiple bond attacked |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not apply the inductive effect alone to halogens on a benzene ring. Halogens are −I but +M; the −I effect wins for reactivity (deactivating), while the +M effect decides ortho/para orientation.
</ExamTip>
