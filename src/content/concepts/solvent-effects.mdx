## What are solvent effects?

**Solvent effects** are the changes in rate, mechanism or equilibrium that a reaction shows when the solvent is changed. For substitution reactions the key question is how well the solvent **solvates** (surrounds and stabilises) the ions involved. **Polar protic** solvents stabilise both cations and anions and favour SN1; **polar aprotic** solvents stabilise only cations, leave anions "naked" and favour SN2.

## Classification of solvents

Solvents are first split by polarity (dipole moment μ and dielectric constant) and then, if polar, by whether they carry an acidic hydrogen on O or N.

| Class | Key feature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-polar | μ ≈ 0, low dielectric constant; cannot dissolve salts | benzene, CCl₄, n-hexane |
| Polar protic | μ ≠ 0 and has O–H or N–H; donates hydrogen bonds | water, methanol, ethanol, acetic acid, formic acid |
| Polar aprotic | μ ≠ 0 but no acidic H; accepts but does not donate hydrogen bonds | acetone, DMSO, DMF, DMA, acetonitrile, THF |

Diethyl ether and THF are aprotic but only weakly polar. Three polar aprotic solvents are asked about by abbreviation:

| Abbreviation | Name | Structure | Dielectric constant |
|---|---|---|---|
| DMSO | dimethyl sulfoxide | (CH₃)₂S=O | about 47 |
| DMF | N,N-dimethylformamide | H–CO–N(CH₃)₂ | about 37 |
| DMA (DMAc) | N,N-dimethylacetamide | CH₃–CO–N(CH₃)₂ | about 38 |

For comparison, water has a dielectric constant of about 80, methanol 33, ethanol 25, acetone 21 and acetic acid only about 6.

## How each solvent treats cations and anions

**In a polar protic solvent such as water**, a cation like Na⁺ is surrounded by water molecules pointing their oxygen lone pairs towards it, and an anion like Cl⁻ is surrounded by water molecules pointing their δ+ hydrogens towards it (hydrogen bonds). Both ions are stabilised.

**In a polar aprotic solvent such as acetone or DMSO**, the negative end of the dipole (the C=O or S=O oxygen) is exposed and binds the cation well. The positive end (the carbonyl carbon or sulphur) is buried inside the molecule behind methyl groups, so an anion cannot get close to it. The anion is left poorly solvated and much more reactive.

<KeyPoint title="Summary">
Polar protic: cation solvated ✓, anion solvated ✓ (by H-bonding). Polar aprotic: cation solvated ✓, anion solvated ✗. A poorly solvated anion is a stronger nucleophile.
</KeyPoint>

## Effect on SN1 reactions

The slow step of SN1 turns a neutral alkyl halide into a carbocation and a halide ion. A solvent that stabilises both ions lowers the energy of this ionisation, so **polar protic solvents speed up SN1**. Relative solvolysis rates of tert-butyl chloride show this clearly:

| Solvent | Dielectric constant | Relative rate |
|---|---|---|
| Acetic acid | 6 | 1 |
| Methanol | 33 | 4 |
| Water | 78 | about 150,000 |

Protic solvents also hydrogen-bond to the departing halide, helping it leave. When the solvent itself is the nucleophile, the reaction is called **solvolysis**.

## Effect on SN2 reactions

In SN2 the nucleophile must attack in the rate-determining step. A protic solvent cages the anion in hydrogen bonds, so the nucleophile must shed part of this shell before it can react. An aprotic solvent removes the cage, so **polar aprotic solvents speed up SN2**. For 1-bromobutane with azide ion:

| Solvent | Type | Relative rate |
|---|---|---|
| Methanol | protic | 1 |
| Water | protic | 7 |
| DMSO | aprotic | 1,300 |
| DMF | aprotic | 2,800 |
| Acetonitrile | aprotic | 5,000 |

The same idea explains why the [Finkelstein reaction](/reactions/finkelstein-reaction/) is run in acetone and why KCN or NaN₃ substitutions are often done in DMSO or DMF.

## Effect on the order of halide nucleophilicity

| Solvent type | Nucleophilicity of halide ions | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Polar protic (water, alcohols) | I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ | small F⁻ is hydrogen-bonded most strongly; large, polarisable I⁻ is held least |
| Polar aprotic (DMSO, DMF, acetone) | F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ | anions are bare, so nucleophilicity follows basicity |

<ExamTip>
In any SN1/SN2 question, read the solvent: water, alcohols or acetic acid point to SN1 (and to I⁻ as the best halide nucleophile); acetone, DMSO, DMF or acetonitrile point to SN2 (and to the basicity order). See [nucleophilicity vs basicity](/comparisons/nucleophilicity-vs-basicity/) for more orders.
</ExamTip>

## Exceptions and finer points

- **Non-polar solvents** such as hexane are useless for ionic substitutions because they cannot dissolve the salt of the nucleophile.
- **Acetone** is slightly protic in practice (keto–enol tautomerism and traces of water) and reacts with strong bases, but is classed as polar aprotic for exams.
- **A general rule (Hughes–Ingold):** a more polar solvent speeds up reactions in which charge is created in the transition state (SN1 of a neutral substrate) and slows down reactions in which charge is spread out or destroyed (SN2 of an anion with a neutral alkyl halide).
