    a: "Addition polymers form by repeated addition of unsaturated monomers (C=C) with no by-product, e.g. polythene and PVC. Condensation polymers form by reaction between bifunctional monomers with loss of a small molecule such as water, e.g. Nylon-6,6 and Terylene."
  - q: "What is the difference between a homopolymer and a copolymer?"
    a: "A homopolymer is made from a single kind of monomer, e.g. polythene from ethene. A copolymer contains two or more different monomers in the same chain, e.g. Buna-S from 1,3-butadiene and styrene, or Nylon-6,6 from a diamine and a diacid."
  - q: "What is the difference between thermoplastic and thermosetting polymers?"
    a: "Thermoplastics are linear or slightly branched chains that soften on heating and harden on cooling again and again, so they can be remoulded (polythene, PVC, polystyrene).   - q: "Which polymers are elastomers and which are fibres?"
    a: "Elastomers have the weakest intermolecular forces plus a few cross-links, so they stretch and spring back: natural rubber, Buna-S, Buna-N, neoprene. Fibres have the strongest forces (hydrogen bonds or strong dipole attractions) and high tensile strength: Nylon-6,6 and Terylene."
  - q: "Is Bakelite an addition or a condensation polymer?"
    a: "Bakelite is a condensation polymer of phenol and formaldehyde (water is lost). It is cross-linked and thermosetting, so it does not soften on reheating."

## What are polymers?

**Polymers** are very large molecules (macromolecules, molar mass roughly 10³–10⁷ u) built by joining many small repeating units called **monomers** through covalent bonds; the process is **polymerisation**. Ethene giving polythene and hexamethylenediamine with adipic acid giving Nylon-6,6 are the two classic examples: the first is an addition polymerisation, the second a condensation polymerisation. Polymers are classified by source, structure, mode of polymerisation, growth mechanism and the intermolecular forces between chains.

## 1. Classification by source

| Class | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | Found in plants and animals | Proteins, cellulose, starch, natural rubber |
| Semi-synthetic | Chemically modified natural polymers | Cellulose acetate (rayon), cellulose nitrate |
| Synthetic | Made entirely in the laboratory or factory | Polythene, Nylon-6,6, Buna-S |

## 2. Classification by structure

| Class | Chain shape | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | Long straight chains | HDPE, PVC |
| Branched chain | Linear chains carrying side branches | LDPE |
| Cross-linked (network) | Chains joined by covalent cross-links; made from bi- and tri-functional monomers | Bakelite, melamine–formaldehyde |

## 3. Classification by mode of polymerisation

**Addition polymers** form by repeated addition of monomers containing double or triple bonds, without loss of any molecule (polythene, PVC, Teflon). **Condensation polymers** form by repeated condensation between two bifunctional (or trifunctional) monomers, with loss of a small molecule such as H₂O, an alcohol or HCl (Nylon-6,6, Terylene, Bakelite).

The same split is also described by **growth mechanism**:

| Addition (chain growth) | Condensation (step growth) |
|---|---|
| Initiator (radical, cation or anion) adds to C=C; the reactive chain end keeps adding monomer | Each bond forms in a separate, independent step between functional groups |
| No by-product | Small molecule lost (H₂O, CH₃OH, HCl) |
| Monomers: alkenes, dienes and their derivatives | Monomers: diacids, diols, diamines, hydroxy- or amino-acids |
| Polythene, PVC, PAN, Teflon, neoprene | Nylons, Terylene, Glyptal, Bakelite |

**Homopolymer vs copolymer:** a homopolymer contains one kind of monomer (polythene, PVC, polystyrene); a copolymer contains two or more (Buna-S, Buna-N, Nylon-6,6). Copolymers can come from chain growth (Buna-S) or step growth (Nylon-6,6), and their properties differ from those of either homopolymer.

<KeyPoint>
Quick test: if the repeating unit contains –CO–NH– or –CO–O– in the main chain, the polymer is almost always a condensation polymer. If the main chain is all carbon (–CH₂–CHX–), it is an addition polymer.
</KeyPoint>

## 4. Classification by molecular forces

| Class | Intermolecular forces | Key property | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastomers | Weakest (van der Waals) plus a few cross-links | Stretch and return to shape | Natural rubber (vulcanised), Buna-S, Buna-N, neoprene |
| Thermoplastics | Intermediate | Soften on heating, harden on cooling, repeatedly; remouldable | Polythene, polystyrene, PVC, PET bottles |
| Fibres | Strongest (hydrogen bonds, strong dipole forces); close packing, crystalline | Thread forming, high tensile strength | Nylon-6,6, Terylene |
| Thermosetting | Heavy cross-linking on heating | Set permanently into an infusible, insoluble mass; cannot be reused | Bakelite, urea–formaldehyde resin |

Order of intermolecular forces: **elastomer less than thermoplastic less than fibre**. Thermosets sit outside this order because their chains are held by covalent cross-links, not just intermolecular forces.

## Solved examples

**Example 1.** Arrange in increasing order of intermolecular forces: Nylon-6,6, Buna-S, polythene.
Buna-S is an elastomer, polythene a thermoplastic, Nylon-6,6 a fibre. Order: **Buna-S less than polythene less than Nylon-6,6**.

**Example 2.** Arrange in increasing order of intermolecular forces: Nylon-6, neoprene, PVC.
Neoprene (elastomer) less than PVC (thermoplastic) less than Nylon-6 (fibre).

**Example 3.** Classify Terylene, Bakelite, PVC and polythene as addition or condensation polymers.
Addition: PVC, polythene. Condensation: Terylene, Bakelite.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Teflon, PAN and PVC are **homopolymers**, not copolymers, even though their monomers contain atoms other than C and H. "Copolymer" refers to the number of different monomers, not to the elements present.
</ExamTip>

## Exam tips

- Natural rubber is cis-1,4-polyisoprene, a natural addition polymer; vulcanisation adds sulphur cross-links.
- LDPE = branched, HDPE = linear, Bakelite = cross-linked: a favourite one-line question.
- PET is a thermoplastic in bottle form but behaves as a fibre (Terylene) when drawn; classification depends on how the material is used.
