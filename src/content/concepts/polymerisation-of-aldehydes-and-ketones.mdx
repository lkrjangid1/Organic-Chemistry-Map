## What is polymerisation of aldehydes and ketones?

**Polymerisation of aldehydes** is the joining of several aldehyde molecules through **C–O–C (acetal) links**, giving either **cyclic oligomers** (trioxane, paraldehyde, metaldehyde) or **long chains** (paraformaldehyde, polyoxymethylene). It is closely related to acetal formation and is usually catalysed by acid. **Ketones do not form stable polymers** of this kind; instead, acid makes acetone **condense** with loss of water to mesityl oxide, phorone and mesitylene. Formaldehyde also gives two special products: **urotropine** with ammonia and **formose sugars** with lime water.

<KeyPoint>
Trimer of HCHO = 1,3,5-trioxane (metaformaldehyde). Linear HCHO chain with about 8–100 units = paraformaldehyde; over about 100 units = polyoxymethylene. Trimer of CH₃CHO = **paraldehyde**; tetramer of CH₃CHO = **metaldehyde**.
</KeyPoint>

## Polymers of formaldehyde

| Product | How it forms | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| 1,3,5-Trioxane (metaformaldehyde, trioxymethylene) | Acid-catalysed trimerisation of concentrated aqueous HCHO, or heating paraformaldehyde with dilute acid | Six-membered ring, three CH₂ alternating with three O |
| Paraformaldehyde | Separates slowly as a white solid from concentrated formalin, especially in the cold | HO(CH₂O)ₙH, n about 8–100 |
| Polyoxymethylene (POM, acetal resin) | Anhydrous HCHO (or trioxane) polymerised with a catalyst, chain ends capped | –(CH₂–O)ₙ–, n much greater than 100 |

<Eq ce="3HCHO ->[H+] (CH2O)3" />

<Eq ce="nHCHO + H2O -> HO(CH2O)_{n}H" />

Paraformaldehyde and trioxane both **revert to formaldehyde** when heated (with or without a little acid), so chemists use them as solid, water-free sources of HCHO. Uncapped polyoxymethylene chains unzip on heating; capping the end groups as esters or acetals gives the tough engineering plastic sold as Delrin.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The cyclic trimer of formaldehyde is **trioxane (metaformaldehyde)**, not paraformaldehyde. Paraformaldehyde is the open-chain polymer HO(CH₂O)ₙH. Because it depolymerises easily, paraformaldehyde behaves as a source of HCHO in reactions.
</ExamTip>

## Formaldehyde and ammonia: urotropine

Formaldehyde and ammonia do not stop at a simple imine. Six HCHO and four NH₃ combine, losing six water molecules, to give **hexamethylenetetramine (urotropine, hexamine, methenamine)**:

<Eq ce="6HCHO + 4NH3 -> (CH2)6N4 + 6H2O" />

Its four N atoms and six CH₂ groups form a rigid, symmetrical **cage like adamantane**, which explains its very high melting point. It was the first organic compound whose structure was solved by X-ray diffraction. Uses: urinary antiseptic (it releases formaldehyde in acidic urine), a hardener for phenol–formaldehyde resins, and the starting material for the explosive RDX (by nitration).

## Formose reaction

With a base such as **Ca(OH)₂ or Ba(OH)₂**, formaldehyde condenses with itself (Butlerov, 1861). A trace of glycolaldehyde starts a self-accelerating chain of aldol additions and aldose–ketose isomerisations, building up trioses, tetroses, pentoses and hexoses. In exam answers this is written as:

<Eq ce="6HCHO ->[Ca(OH)2] C6H12O6" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The product, **formose**, is a **mixture of racemic sugars** (including hexoses), not pure glucose or pure fructose. Write C₆H₁₂O₆ for the hexose part, but do not call it D-glucose.
</ExamTip>

## Polymers of acetaldehyde

Acetaldehyde gives cyclic acetal oligomers with a little concentrated H₂SO₄. **Temperature decides the ring size:**

| Product | Ring | Conditions | Physical state, use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paraldehyde** (2,4,6-trimethyl-1,3,5-trioxane) | Trimer, six-membered | Conc. H₂SO₄, room temperature | Liquid; old sedative (hypnotic) |
| **Metaldehyde** (tetramer) | Tetramer, eight-membered | Chilled mineral acid, about 0 °C or below | Solid; slug and snail poison |

<Eq ce="3CH3CHO ->[conc. H2SO4][room temp] (CH3CHO)3" />

<Eq ce="4CH3CHO ->[conc. H2SO4][below 0 °C] (CH3CHO)4" />

Both are acetals, so they have **no free –CHO group**; heating with a trace of acid splits them back into acetaldehyde. Paraldehyde is therefore a convenient, less volatile stand-in for acetaldehyde.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Paraldehyde is the **trimer** and metaldehyde is the **tetramer**, not the other way round. Link "para" with room temperature and the liquid trimer.
</ExamTip>

## Acetone: condensation instead of polymerisation

Acetone has no tendency to form C–O–C polymers. With acid it undergoes **aldol-type condensation** followed by dehydration. In exam questions the acid is written as dry HCl gas (for the open-chain products) or conc. H₂SO₄ (for the aromatic product):

| Product | Acetone units | Reagent | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mesityl oxide (4-methylpent-3-en-2-one) | 2 | Dry HCl | (CH₃)₂C=CH–CO–CH₃ |
| Phorone (2,6-dimethylhepta-2,5-dien-4-one) | 3 | Dry HCl | (CH₃)₂C=CH–CO–CH=C(CH₃)₂ |
| Mesitylene (1,3,5-trimethylbenzene) | 3 | Conc. H₂SO₄, heat | Benzene ring with three CH₃ at 1,3,5 |

<Eq ce="2CH3COCH3 ->[dry HCl] (CH3)2C=CHCOCH3 + H2O" />

<Eq ce="3CH3COCH3 ->[dry HCl] (CH3)2C=CHCOCH=C(CH3)2 + 2H2O" />

<Eq ce="3CH3COCH3 ->[conc. H2SO4][\Delta] C6H3(CH3)3 + 3H2O" />

Mesityl oxide forms through diacetone alcohol, which dehydrates easily; a further aldol step on mesityl oxide gives phorone, which is why phorone needs three acetone units. Mesitylene was first made in 1837 by heating acetone with concentrated sulphuric acid. Mesitylene can also be obtained by trimerising **propyne** (in exam questions, by passing it through a red-hot metal tube, as for ethyne to benzene); in practice this gives a mixture of 1,3,5- and 1,2,4-trimethylbenzene.

## Exam tips

- Count carbons: 3 HCHO gives C₃ trioxane; 3 CH₃CHO gives C₆ paraldehyde; 3 acetone gives C₉ phorone or mesitylene.
- Acetal oligomers (trioxane, paraldehyde, metaldehyde) do not give Tollens' or Fehling's test until acid splits them back to the aldehyde.
- Urotropine contains no oxygen: all six oxygens of HCHO leave as water.
- Base with HCHO gives either formose sugars (Ca(OH)₂) or the Cannizzaro reaction (concentrated NaOH); read the reagent carefully.
