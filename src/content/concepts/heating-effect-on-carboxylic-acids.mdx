## What is the effect of heat on carboxylic acids?

Simple monocarboxylic acids are stable to moderate heating, but acids carrying a **second functional group** (another COOH, an OH or an NH₂) react with themselves when heated. What forms depends almost entirely on **how far apart the two groups are**: groups that can close a strain-free **five- or six-membered ring** do so; groups too close lose CO₂ instead; groups too far apart join **between molecules**, giving polymers.

## Dicarboxylic acids

| Acid (HOOC–(CH₂)ₙ–COOH) | Common name | Product on heating | What is lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,2- (n = 0) | Oxalic acid | HCOOH + CO₂ | CO₂ |
| 1,3- (n = 1) | Malonic acid | CH₃COOH + CO₂ | CO₂ |
| 1,4- (n = 2) | Succinic acid | Succinic anhydride (5-ring) | H₂O |
| 1,5- (n = 3) | Glutaric acid | Glutaric anhydride (6-ring) | H₂O |
| 1,6- (n = 4) | Adipic acid | Cyclopentanone | CO₂ + H₂O |
| 1,7- (n = 5) | Pimelic acid | Cyclohexanone | CO₂ + H₂O |
| 1,8- and longer | Suberic acid onwards | Polymeric (linear) anhydride | H₂O |
| benzene-1,2- | Phthalic acid | Phthalic anhydride (above about 210 °C) | H₂O |

<KeyPoint title="Blanc's rule (exam form)">
1,4- and 1,5-dicarboxylic acids form **cyclic anhydrides** (5- and 6-membered rings). 1,6- and 1,7-dicarboxylic acids form **cyclic ketones** with one carbon fewer (cyclopentanone and cyclohexanone), because the alternative anhydride rings would be 7- and 8-membered. 1,2- and 1,3-acids are too short to cyclise and simply lose CO₂.
</KeyPoint>

**Short chains: decarboxylation.** Malonic acid loses CO₂ easily because the second C=O group lets a cyclic six-membered transition state form; the enol of acetic acid is produced and tautomerises.

<Eq ce="HOOC-CH2-COOH ->[\Delta] CH3COOH + CO2" />

Oxalic acid vapour decomposes at about 125–175 °C into carbon dioxide and formic acid. With **concentrated H₂SO₄**, which removes water, oxalic acid instead gives **CO + CO₂ + H₂O**; this is a standard laboratory source of carbon monoxide.

<Eq ce="HOOC-COOH ->[\Delta] HCOOH + CO2" />
<Eq ce="HOOC-COOH ->[conc. H2SO4] CO + CO2 + H2O" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not write CO + CO₂ + H₂O for oxalic acid on simple heating. Heat alone gives HCOOH + CO₂; the CO-forming dehydration needs conc. H₂SO₄ (and formic acid itself gives CO + H₂O with conc. H₂SO₄).
</ExamTip>

**Medium chains: cyclic anhydrides.** Succinic, glutaric and phthalic acids lose one molecule of water intramolecularly.

<Eq ce="HOOC-CH2CH2-COOH ->[\Delta] (CH2CO)2O + H2O" />

**Longer chains: cyclic ketones.** Adipic and pimelic acids lose both water and carbon dioxide (a ketonic decarboxylation) and close a five- or six-membered carbocyclic ring. In practice this works best with a base such as Ba(OH)₂.

<Eq ce="HOOC-(CH2)4-COOH ->[\Delta] C5H8O + CO2 + H2O" />

Here C₅H₈O is cyclopentanone; pimelic acid, HOOC–(CH₂)₅–COOH, gives cyclohexanone, C₆H₁₀O. From suberic acid (1,8-) onwards, cyclic products would need large rings, so in exam answers the molecules link end to end through anhydride bonds, giving a **polymeric anhydride**, –[O–CO–(CH₂)ₙ–CO]ₓ– with n of 6 or more.

### Phthalic acid with ammonia

Phthalic acid (or its anhydride) with ammonia gives the ammonium salt, then **phthalamide**, and on strong heating the cyclic imide **phthalimide**, the starting material for the [Gabriel phthalimide synthesis](/reactions/gabriel-phthalimide-synthesis/). Succinic anhydride behaves the same way, giving succinimide.

## Hydroxy acids

| Position of OH | Example | Product on heating | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| α (2-hydroxy) | Lactic acid, glycolic acid | Lactide (6-membered cyclic diester of two molecules) | intermolecular esterification, ×2 |
| β (3-hydroxy) | 3-Hydroxybutanoic acid | But-2-enoic acid (α,β-unsaturated acid) | dehydration |
| γ (4-hydroxy) | 4-Hydroxybutanoic acid | γ-Butyrolactone (5-ring) | intramolecular esterification |
| δ (5-hydroxy) | 5-Hydroxypentanoic acid | δ-Valerolactone (6-ring) | intramolecular esterification |
| further away (ω) | 6-Hydroxyhexanoic acid | Polyester –[O–(CH₂)₅–CO]ₙ– | intermolecular esterification |

<Eq ce="2 CH3CH(OH)COOH ->[\Delta] C6H8O4 + 2 H2O" />
<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)CH2COOH ->[\Delta] CH3CH=CHCOOH + H2O" />
<Eq ce="HO-CH2CH2CH2-COOH ->[\Delta] C4H6O2 + H2O" />

Here C₆H₈O₄ is lactide (3,6-dimethyl-1,4-dioxane-2,5-dione) and C₄H₆O₂ is γ-butyrolactone.

γ-Lactones are so stable that 4-hydroxy acids cyclise **spontaneously**, even on standing in acid. For longer hydroxy acids, lactone formation competes with **polymerisation**, so polyesters result. The lactide of lactic acid is the monomer for **polylactic acid**, a [biodegradable polymer](/concepts/biodegradable-polymers/).

## Amino acids

Amino acids follow the same pattern, with amide bonds in place of ester bonds:

| Position of NH₂ | Product on heating | Lost |
|---|---|---|
| α | 2,5-Diketopiperazine (cyclic diamide of two molecules) | 2 H₂O |
| β | α,β-Unsaturated acid | NH₃ |
| γ, δ | γ- or δ-Lactam (cyclic amide) | H₂O |

<Eq ce="2 H2N-CH2-COOH ->[\Delta] C4H6N2O2 + 2 H2O" />

<ExamTip>
The product of heating two glycine molecules is **2,5-diketopiperazine** (piperazine-2,5-dione), a cyclic dipeptide. Lactam formation from a single amino-acid molecule is most efficient when it gives a five-membered γ-lactam (pyrrolidin-2-one).
</ExamTip>

## Quick revision

- Count the atoms in the ring that would form, including both carbonyl carbons and the bridging O, N or C.
- **5 or 6 atoms:** ring forms (anhydride, lactone, lactam, cyclic ketone, lactide, diketopiperazine).
- **Too short:** decarboxylation (1,1- and 1,2-diacids, β-keto acids) or dehydration (β-hydroxy acids).
- **Too long:** polymer (polyanhydride, polyester, polyamide).
