## What is hyperconjugation?

**Hyperconjugation** is the delocalisation of the σ electrons of a C–H bond on an α-carbon (the carbon directly attached to an unsaturated or electron-deficient atom) into a neighbouring **empty p orbital, π bond or half-filled p orbital**. It is a permanent, stabilising interaction. Because one way of drawing it shows the C–H bond broken (H⁺ and a new C=C), it is also called **no-bond resonance** or **σ-conjugation**.

It operates in three situations:

| System | Overlap | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Carbocation | σ(C–H) → empty p orbital | (CH₃)₃C⁺ |
| Free radical | σ(C–H) → half-filled p orbital | (CH₃)₃C· |
| Alkene / alkylarene | σ(C–H) → π* of C=C | propene, toluene |

## The α-hydrogen rule

Only C–H bonds on the α-carbon can align with the adjacent p orbital, so:

<KeyPoint title="Counting rule">
**Number of hyperconjugative structures = number of α-hydrogens.** The more α-hydrogens, the greater the hyperconjugation and the more stable the carbocation, radical or alkene.
</KeyPoint>

In the ethyl cation, one C–H bond of the methyl group lies in the plane of the empty p orbital and its electrons spread into that orbital, dispersing the positive charge. Only C–H bonds roughly parallel to the p orbital contribute effectively; a C–H bond perpendicular to it cannot.

## Stability of carbocations and free radicals

| Carbocation | α-H | Hyperconjugative structures | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| (CH₃)₃C⁺ (3°) | 9 | 9 | most stable |
| (CH₃)₂CH⁺ (2°) | 6 | 6 | |
| CH₃CH₂⁺ (1°) | 3 | 3 | |
| CH₃⁺ | 0 | 0 | least stable |

In CH₃⁺ the empty p orbital is perpendicular to the plane of the three C–H bonds, so they cannot overlap with it at all. The [inductive effect](/concepts/inductive-effect/) of alkyl groups adds to the same order. Free radicals follow the same sequence: 3° > 2° > 1° > ·CH₃. For allylic and benzylic intermediates, [resonance](/concepts/resonance-effect/) is usually more important than hyperconjugation. See [carbocations](/concepts/carbocations/) and [stability of reaction intermediates](/comparisons/stability-of-reaction-intermediates/).

## Stability of alkenes

The σ(C–H)→π* interaction is weaker than σ(C–H)→p in cations, but it still makes **more substituted alkenes more stable**. Heats of hydrogenation show this clearly:

| Alkene | Substitution | α-H | ΔH hydrogenation (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH₂ | none | 0 | −136 |
| CH₃CH=CH₂ | mono | 3 | −125 |
| cis-CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | di | 6 | −119 |
| trans-CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | di | 6 | −115 |
| (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ | tri | 9 | −112 |
| (CH₃)₂C=C(CH₃)₂ | tetra | 12 | −110 |

For isomeric alkenes, a **smaller heat of hydrogenation means a more stable alkene**. The trans/cis difference has a steric origin (two methyl groups crowd each other on the same side), not a hyperconjugative one. This order underlies **Saytzeff (Zaitsev) products** in [E1 reactions](/reactions/e1-reaction/). More data: [alkene stability and heat of hydrogenation](/comparisons/alkene-stability-heat-of-hydrogenation/).

## Effect on bond length

Typical bond lengths are C–C 1.54 Å, C=C 1.34 Å and C≡C 1.20 Å. Hyperconjugation gives the C–C single bond next to a π system some double-bond character, so it becomes **shorter** than 1.54 Å; in propyne the CH₃–C bond is about 1.46 Å. Correspondingly, the C=C or C≡C bond gains slight single-bond character.

## Baker–Nathan effect

In 1935, Baker and Nathan measured how fast pyridine reacts with para-alkylbenzyl bromides. The inductive effect predicts tert-butyl > isopropyl > ethyl > methyl, but they found the reverse:

| Para substituent | α-H | Electron release by hyperconjugation |
|---|---|---|
| −CH₃ | 3 | highest |
| −CH₂CH₃ | 2 | |
| −CH(CH₃)₂ | 1 | |
| −C(CH₃)₃ | 0 | lowest |

This order, **CH₃ > CH₂CH₃ > CH(CH₃)₂ > C(CH₃)₃**, is the classic hyperconjugation order. The differences are small and partly due to solvation, but exams use it as the standard example.

<ExamTip>
Do not confuse the two alkyl orders. **Inductive (+I):** tert-butyl > isopropyl > ethyl > methyl. **Hyperconjugation:** methyl > ethyl > isopropyl > tert-butyl (depends on the number of α-H). For carbocation stability both effects point the same way, because a 3° cation has both more alkyl groups and more α-H.
</ExamTip>

## Hyperconjugation on a benzene ring

In toluene, the C–H bonds of the methyl group release electron density into the ring, raising it at the ortho and para positions; this partly explains why toluene undergoes nitration faster than benzene and is ortho/para directing. Like resonance, hyperconjugation **does not place charge on the meta position**, so a meta alkyl group acts only through its inductive effect.

## Quick revision

- σ(C–H) electrons of α-carbon delocalise into adjacent p, π or radical orbital.
- Structures = number of α-H; more α-H, more stable.
- Carbocations and radicals: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl.
- Alkenes: tetra > tri > di > mono > ethene (trans > cis).
- Hyperconjugation shortens the adjacent C–C single bond.
- It is permanent, unlike the electromeric effect.
