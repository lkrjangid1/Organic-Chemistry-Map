## What are addition polymers?

**Addition polymers** (chain-growth polymers) form when many molecules of an unsaturated monomer, usually an alkene CH₂=CHX, join through their C=C bonds **without losing any small molecule**. The polymer therefore has the same empirical formula as the monomer. Polythene, PVC, Teflon, polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) are the five examples JEE and NEET ask about most; all are **homopolymers** made from a single monomer.

<KeyPoint>
General pattern: n CH₂=CHX → –(CH₂–CHX)ₙ–. Read the repeating unit, put the double bond back between the two main-chain carbons, and you have the monomer.
</KeyPoint>

## Master table

| Polymer (other name) | Monomer | Repeating unit | Initiator / catalyst | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LDPE (low density polythene) | Ethene, CH₂=CH₂ | –CH₂–CH₂– | Trace O₂ or peroxide, very high pressure | Insulation of electric wires, squeeze bottles, toys, flexible pipes, plastic bags |
| HDPE (high density polythene) | Ethene | –CH₂–CH₂– | Ziegler–Natta (Et₃Al + TiCl₄), low pressure | Buckets, dustbins, bottles, pipes |
| PVC (polyvinyl chloride) | Vinyl chloride, CH₂=CHCl | –CH₂–CHCl– | Free radical | Water pipes, raincoats, handbags, vinyl flooring |
| Teflon (PTFE) | Tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂ | –CF₂–CF₂– | Free radical or persulphate, high pressure | Non-stick coating on utensils, oil seals, gaskets |
| PAN (Orlon, Acrilan) | Acrylonitrile, CH₂=CH–CN | –CH₂–CH(CN)– | Peroxide (free radical) | Wool substitute in fibres, sweaters; precursor of carbon fibre |
| PMMA (Perspex, Plexiglas, Lucite) | Methyl methacrylate, CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ | –CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)– | Free radical (also anionic) | Lenses (including intraocular lenses), skylights, signboards, aircraft windows |

## Polythene: LDPE vs HDPE

Both grades come from ethene. What differs is the **mechanism**, which decides whether the chains branch.

<Eq ce="nCH2=CH2 ->[traces of O2 or peroxide][1000-2000 atm, 350-570 K] (-CH2-CH2-)_{n} (LDPE)" />

<Eq ce="nCH2=CH2 ->[Et3Al + TiCl4][6-7 atm, 333-343 K] (-CH2-CH2-)_{n} (HDPE)" />

| Feature | LDPE | HDPE |
|---|---|---|
| Conditions (NCERT figures) | 1000–2000 atm, 350–570 K, trace O₂/peroxide | 6–7 atm, 333–343 K, hydrocarbon solvent, Ziegler–Natta catalyst |
| Mechanism | Free-radical addition (with H-atom abstraction) | Coordination polymerisation on the Ti centre |
| Chain shape | Highly branched | Linear, very little branching |
| Density | About 0.91–0.94 g/cm³ | About 0.94–0.97 g/cm³ |
| Melting point | About 105–115 °C | About 120–130 °C |
| Properties | Soft, flexible, tough, more transparent | Harder, stiffer, stronger, more opaque |
| Type of polymer | Branched-chain polymer | Linear polymer |

Both are chemically inert and poor conductors of electricity. Branches keep LDPE chains apart, so packing is poor and density, crystallinity and tensile strength are lower. HDPE chains lie close together, so the intermolecular forces are stronger.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The NCERT temperatures are in **kelvin**: 350–570 K for LDPE and 333–343 K for HDPE (about 77–297 °C and 60–70 °C). Writing them as °C is wrong. Also, HDPE is not formed by an anionic mechanism: the Ziegler–Natta catalyst works by **coordination** of ethene to titanium followed by insertion into the Ti–C bond.
</ExamTip>

Ziegler and Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for these titanium–aluminium catalysts, which also give stereoregular (isotactic) polypropene.

## Free-radical mechanism (exam answer)

Using benzoyl peroxide as initiator for ethene:

1. **Initiation:** the peroxide splits on heating; the phenyl radical adds to CH₂=CH₂, giving a new carbon radical.
2. **Propagation:** this radical adds to another ethene, and the chain grows by one CH₂–CH₂ unit each time.
3. **Termination:** two growing radicals combine (or disproportionate), ending the chain.

<Eq ce="C6H5^. + CH2=CH2 -> C6H5-CH2-CH2^." />

In LDPE, a growing radical can pull an H atom from the middle of another chain; the new radical site then grows a side branch. That is why high-pressure radical polythene is branched.

## Teflon (PTFE)

<Eq ce="nCF2=CF2 ->[persulphate or free-radical initiator][high pressure] (-CF2-CF2-)_{n}" />

Very strong C–F bonds make Teflon **chemically inert**, resistant to corrosive reagents and extremely slippery (one of the lowest friction coefficients of any solid). Coatings decompose above about 300 °C, so non-stick pans should not be overheated.

## Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)

<Eq ce="nCH2=CH-CN ->[peroxide] (-CH2-CH(CN)-)_{n}" />

PAN fibres (Orlon, Acrilan) replace **wool**. Acrylic fibres resist stains, chemicals, insects and fungi. Heating PAN fibres above 1000 °C in an inert atmosphere converts them into carbon fibre.

## PMMA (acrylic glass)

<Eq ce="nCH2=C(CH3)COOCH3 -> (-CH2-C(CH3)(COOCH3)-)_{n}" />

PMMA is an amorphous, transparent thermoplastic sold as **Perspex, Plexiglas and Lucite**. It replaces glass where breakage is a risk: lenses of vehicle lamps and spectacles, skylights, display boards, aircraft windows. Being compatible with body tissue, it is used for rigid **intraocular lenses** implanted after cataract surgery.

## PVC

<Eq ce="nCH2=CHCl -> (-CH2-CHCl-)_{n}" />

Pure PVC is rigid and used for **pipes**, doors and window frames; adding plasticisers makes it soft enough for raincoats, handbags, flooring and medical drip bags. Further free-radical chlorination of PVC gives **chlorinated PVC (CPVC)**, which tolerates higher temperatures and is used for hot-water pipes.

## Exam tips

- All five are addition homopolymers; none releases water or HCl when it forms.
- Teflon and PAN are prepared with free-radical/peroxide initiators; HDPE is the one that needs Ziegler–Natta.
- "Substitute for wool" means PAN; "non-stick" means Teflon; "Perspex" means PMMA.
- LDPE is a branched-chain polymer, HDPE and PVC are linear polymers.
