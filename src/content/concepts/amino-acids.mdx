## What are amino acids?

**Amino acids** are compounds that contain both an **amino group (–NH₂)** and a **carboxyl group (–COOH)**. Those obtained by hydrolysis of proteins are all **α-amino acids**, with both groups on the same carbon: **R–CH(NH₂)–COOH**, where R is the side chain. About twenty of them build all proteins; they differ only in R. Amino acids join through **peptide bonds** to form peptides and proteins.

## Classification

### By the number of acidic and basic groups

| Type | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral | Equal numbers of NH₂ and COOH | Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, serine |
| Acidic | More COOH than NH₂ | Aspartic acid, glutamic acid |
| Basic | More basic (NH₂-type) groups than COOH | Lysine, arginine, histidine |

### Essential and non-essential

- **Essential amino acids** cannot be synthesised by the body and must come from the diet.
- **Non-essential amino acids** can be synthesised in the body.

| Essential (NCERT list of 10) | Non-essential (10) |
|---|---|
| Valine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Phenylalanine, Methionine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Histidine, Arginine | Cysteine, Alanine, Aspartic acid, Asparagine, Tyrosine, Serine, Glutamic acid, Glutamine, Glycine, Proline |

<Trick>
**V I L L P M T T H A** for the essential ones and **C A A A T S G G G P** for the non-essential ones. These are the first letters of the names, not the one-letter codes (Lys is K, Phe is F, Trp is W, Arg is R, Tyr is Y).
</Trick>

Biochemistry texts often list **nine** essential amino acids for adults and treat **arginine** as conditionally (semi-) essential, because adults make some of it but growing children need it in food. For NCERT-based exams, the ten-member list is the one to learn.

<KeyPoint>
**Aromatic** amino acids: phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan. **Heterocyclic**: histidine, tryptophan, proline. **Sulphur-containing**: cysteine, methionine. **Proline** is the only standard amino acid whose nitrogen is a secondary amine, which is why exam books call it an imino acid.
</KeyPoint>

## Zwitterion and properties

In the solid state and in neutral water, the acidic COOH gives its proton to the basic NH₂ of the same molecule, forming a **dipolar ion (zwitterion)**:

<Eq ce="H2N-CH(R)-COOH <=> H3N+-CH(R)-COO-" />

- The zwitterion has a positive and a negative charge but **no net charge**.
- This explains why amino acids are **colourless, crystalline, water-soluble solids with high melting points** (they decompose above about 200 °C), behaving like salts rather than like amines or carboxylic acids.
- They are **amphoteric**: in acid, –COO⁻ takes up H⁺ (cation forms); in base, –NH₃⁺ loses H⁺ (anion forms).
- The pH at which the amino acid exists mainly as the zwitterion and does not move in an electric field is its **isoelectric point (pI)**.

### Optical activity

Except **glycine**, every standard α-amino acid has a stereocentre at the α-carbon and is optically active. Those in proteins have the **L-configuration**: in the Fischer projection with COOH at the top, the –NH₂ group is on the **left**.

## Peptide bond

A **peptide bond** is an amide link **–CO–NH–** formed when the carboxyl group of one amino acid condenses with the amino group of another, with loss of water. Glycine (COOH end) and alanine (NH₂ end) give the dipeptide **glycylalanine (Gly-Ala)**:

<Eq ce="H2N-CH2-COOH + H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH -> H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-COOH + H2O" />

Three amino acids give a tripeptide (two peptide bonds), and so on. Chains of more than ten units are **polypeptides**; a polypeptide with more than about a hundred residues (molecular mass above about 10,000 u) is called a **protein**. Peptide bonds are detected by the [biuret test](/tests/biuret-test/).

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
One-letter codes are not always the first letter of the name. Learn the exceptions: Lys K, Phe F, Trp W, Tyr Y, Arg R, Asp D, Glu E, Asn N, Gln Q. T belongs to threonine, not tyrosine.
</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>
A one-line fact often paired with this chapter: vitamin B₁₂ (cobalamin) is a cobalt complex, and its deficiency causes pernicious anaemia.
</ExamTip>

## Solved examples

**1. How many peptide bonds are present in a tetrapeptide?** Three (n amino acids give n − 1 peptide bonds).

**2. Classify lysine and glutamic acid.** Lysine is basic (two NH₂ groups, one COOH); glutamic acid is acidic (two COOH groups, one NH₂).

**3. Which is the simplest optically active amino acid?** Alanine, CH₃–CH(NH₂)–COOH.
