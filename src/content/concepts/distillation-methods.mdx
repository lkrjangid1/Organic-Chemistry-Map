## What is distillation?

**Distillation** separates a liquid from non-volatile impurities, or separates liquids from one another, by **boiling the mixture and condensing the vapour**. The component with the lower boiling point vaporises first, is cooled in a condenser and is collected separately. Four variants are used depending on the boiling points and stability of the liquids: **simple distillation, fractional distillation, distillation under reduced pressure and steam distillation**.

<KeyPoint>
Large b.p. difference → simple. Close b.p. → fractional (fractionating column). High b.p. or decomposes on boiling → reduced pressure. Steam volatile and immiscible with water → steam distillation (boils below 373 K).
</KeyPoint>

| Method | Use it when | Classic example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple distillation | Volatile liquid + non-volatile impurity, or liquids with a large b.p. difference | Chloroform (334 K) from aniline (457 K) |
| Fractional distillation | Boiling points close together | Crude oil into petroleum fractions |
| Distillation under reduced pressure | Very high b.p., or decomposes at or below its b.p. | Glycerol from spent lye (soap industry) |
| Steam distillation | Steam volatile and immiscible with water | Aniline from aniline–water; o-nitrophenol from p-nitrophenol |

## Simple distillation

The liquid mixture is heated in a round-bottom flask fitted with a condenser.

1. On boiling, the vapour of the **lower-boiling** component forms first.
2. The vapour is cooled in the condenser and the liquid is collected in a receiver.
3. The higher-boiling component vaporises later and is collected in a separate receiver (or left in the flask).

It works well only when the boiling points differ widely. **Example:** chloroform (b.p. 334 K) and aniline (b.p. 457 K) are easily separated.

## Fractional distillation

When boiling points are close, both liquids vaporise in the same temperature range and simple distillation fails. A **fractionating column** is fitted between the flask and the condenser.

- The column offers a large surface on which ascending vapour meets descending condensed liquid.
- The higher-boiling component condenses and runs back; part of the liquid re-vaporises using heat from the rising vapour.
- Each condensation and re-vaporisation step is one **theoretical plate**. After many plates the vapour at the top is almost pure lower-boiling component.

**Example:** refineries use fractional distillation to separate crude oil into fractions of different boiling ranges. Lighter, lower-boiling fractions leave from the top of the column, heavier ones lower down.

## Distillation under reduced pressure

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the **external pressure**. Lowering the pressure above the liquid with a water pump or vacuum pump lowers its boiling point. The method is used for liquids with **very high boiling points** and those that **decompose at or below their normal boiling point**.

**Example:** glycerol is recovered from **spent lye** in the soap industry by this method, since it would decompose if boiled at atmospheric pressure.

## Steam distillation

Used for substances that are **steam volatile and immiscible with water**. Steam from a generator is passed through the heated flask containing the mixture; the steam and the organic vapour condense together, and the organic liquid is later separated from the water with a separating funnel.

**Principle:** the mixture boils when the **sum of the vapour pressures** of the organic liquid (p₁) and of water (p₂) equals atmospheric pressure (p):

p = p₁ + p₂

Because p₁ is less than p, the organic liquid distils at a temperature **below its own boiling point**, and the mixture boils close to but **below 373 K**. Heat-sensitive compounds can therefore be distilled without decomposing.

**Examples:** aniline from an aniline–water mixture; essential oils from plant material.

### Separating o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol

Nitration of phenol with dilute HNO₃ gives a mixture of o- and p-nitrophenol, which is separated by steam distillation.

| Isomer | Hydrogen bonding | Behaviour with steam |
|---|---|---|
| o-Nitrophenol | **Intramolecular** (–OH to the neighbouring –NO₂, six-membered ring) | Molecules stay separate, so it is **steam volatile** and distils over |
| p-Nitrophenol | **Intermolecular** (–OH of one molecule to –NO₂ of another) | Molecules associate, so it is far less volatile (b.p. 279 °C) and **stays in the flask** |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
It is the **ortho** isomer that comes over with steam, not the para isomer. The intramolecular hydrogen bond "uses up" the –OH, so o-nitrophenol molecules do not hydrogen-bond to their neighbours. The para isomer is held together by intermolecular hydrogen bonds and remains behind.
</ExamTip>

## Solved examples: choose the method

1. **Separate glycerol from spent lye.** Glycerol decomposes near its boiling point: distillation under reduced pressure.
2. **Separate the o- and p-isomers obtained by nitrating phenol.** Only the ortho isomer is steam volatile: steam distillation.
3. **Separate chloroform from aniline.** Boiling points differ by more than 100 K: simple distillation.
4. **Separate crude oil into its fractions.** Many components with close boiling points: fractional distillation.
