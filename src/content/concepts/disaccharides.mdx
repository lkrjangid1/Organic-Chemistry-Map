## What are disaccharides?

**Disaccharides** are carbohydrates that give **two monosaccharide molecules** on hydrolysis. The two units are joined by a **glycosidic linkage**, an acetal C–O–C bond formed between the anomeric carbon of one sugar and an OH group of the other, with loss of water. The four disaccharides asked in exams, **sucrose, maltose, lactose and cellobiose**, all have the molecular formula **C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁**. Whether a disaccharide is **reducing** depends on whether one anomeric carbon is left free.

<Eq ce="C12H22O11 + H2O ->[H+ or enzyme] C6H12O6 + C6H12O6" />

## Reducing or non-reducing: the one rule

A sugar reduces Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent only if it can open to a **free aldehyde (or α-hydroxy ketone) group**. That needs an anomeric carbon present as a **hemiacetal**, not tied up in the glycosidic bond.

- If the linkage joins **anomeric carbon to anomeric carbon** (C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose in sucrose), nothing is left free: **non-reducing**.
- If the linkage joins C1 of one unit to **C4** of the other, the second unit keeps its anomeric C1 free: **reducing**.

A free anomeric carbon also explains **mutarotation** (α ⇌ β interconversion in water) and **osazone formation** with phenylhydrazine. Reducing disaccharides show both; sucrose shows neither.

<KeyPoint>
Only sucrose is non-reducing among these four, because its glycosidic bond is a "head-to-head" link between the two anomeric carbons. Maltose, lactose and cellobiose are "head-to-tail" (1→4) and keep one hemiacetal carbon free.
</KeyPoint>

## Sucrose

Sucrose is ordinary **cane sugar**, obtained from sugar cane and sugar beet. A pyranose ring of **α-D-glucose** is joined through its C1 to C2 of a furanose ring of **β-D-fructose**.

- **Dextrorotatory**, specific rotation about **+66.5°**.
- **Non-reducing**: no reaction with Fehling's solution or Tollens' reagent, no osazone, no mutarotation.

### Invert sugar

Hydrolysis by dilute acid or the enzyme **invertase** gives an **equimolar mixture of D-(+)-glucose and D-(−)-fructose**.

<Eq ce="C12H22O11 (sucrose) + H2O ->[H+ or invertase] C6H12O6 (D-glucose) + C6H12O6 (D-fructose)" />

Glucose rotates light by about +52.5° but fructose by about −92.4°. Because the laevorotation of fructose is larger, the 1:1 mixture has a net rotation of roughly −20°. The sign changes from (+) to (−) during hydrolysis, so the product is called **invert sugar** and the process **inversion**.

### Action of heat and concentrated H₂SO₄

- When heated strongly (around its melting point, about 189 °C), sucrose decomposes and turns into brown **caramel**.
- **Concentrated H₂SO₄** acts as a dehydrating agent and removes the elements of water, leaving a black mass of carbon (the "sugar charring" experiment):

<Eq ce="C12H22O11 ->[conc. H2SO4] 12C + 11H2O" />

## Maltose

Maltose (**malt sugar**) is formed when starch is broken down by the enzyme **diastase** (an amylase present in malt, i.e. germinating barley). It is made of **two α-D-glucose units**, with C1 of the first unit linked to C4 of the second.

<Eq ce="(C6H10O5)_n (starch) ->[diastase][H2O] C12H22O11 (maltose)" />

<Eq ce="C12H22O11 (maltose) + H2O ->[maltase] 2C6H12O6 (D-glucose)" />

- **Dextrorotatory** and a **reducing sugar**: C1 of the second glucose unit is free, so it gives Fehling's and Tollens' tests, forms an osazone and shows mutarotation.

## Lactose

Lactose (**milk sugar**) is the sugar of milk. It consists of **β-D-galactose** and **D-glucose**, with C1 of galactose linked to C4 of glucose.

- The galactose unit has its anomeric carbon tied up (non-reducing part); the **glucose unit has a free C1** (reducing part), so lactose is a **reducing sugar**.
- It is dextrorotatory. Hydrolysis by dilute acid or the enzyme lactase gives D-galactose and D-glucose.

## Cellobiose

Cellobiose is the repeating disaccharide of cellulose and is obtained by partial (acid or enzymatic) hydrolysis of cellulose. It contains **two D-glucose units joined by a β(1→4) linkage**. Like maltose, its second glucose keeps a free anomeric carbon, so cellobiose is a **reducing sugar**. Maltose and cellobiose differ only in the configuration of the glycosidic bond (α versus β).

<ExamTip>
Questions often ask which sugar is NOT reducing, or which does NOT show mutarotation. The answer is sucrose (and trehalose, glucose α(1→1) glucose, if it appears as an option). For "products of hydrolysis of lactose", write D-galactose and D-glucose, not two glucose molecules.
</ExamTip>

## Solved examples

**1. Which of these gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent: sucrose, maltose?**
Maltose. Its second glucose unit has a free hemiacetal carbon. Sucrose has none.

**2. A sugar solution is dextrorotatory. After warming with dilute HCl it becomes laevorotatory. Identify the sugar.**
Sucrose. Its hydrolysis gives invert sugar, in which the strong laevorotation of fructose outweighs the dextrorotation of glucose.

**3. Name the disaccharide formed by the action of diastase on starch and the enzyme that hydrolyses it.**
Maltose; it is hydrolysed to glucose by maltase.
