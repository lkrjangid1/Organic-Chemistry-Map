## What are biodegradable polymers?

**Biodegradable polymers** are polymers that micro-organisms, enzymes or water can break down into small harmless molecules (finally CO₂, water and biomass) within a reasonable time. Most of them are **aliphatic polyesters** or **polyamides** whose backbone links (–CO–O– or –CO–NH–) are open to hydrolysis. The exam set is **PHBV, nylon-2-nylon-6, PLA, PCL and PGA**; all are condensation (step-growth) polymers of hydroxy acids or amino acids.

<KeyPoint>
PHBV = 3-hydroxybutanoic acid + 3-hydroxypentanoic acid. Nylon-2-nylon-6 = glycine + 6-aminohexanoic acid. PLA = lactic acid. PGA = glycolic acid. PCL = ε-caprolactone. Nylon-6, nylon-6,6, polythene and glyptal are **not** biodegradable.
</KeyPoint>

## Why they degrade

Ordinary plastics such as polythene have only C–C and C–H bonds, which water and enzymes cannot attack, so they last for centuries. Biodegradable polymers contain **functional groups in the main chain** that mimic natural polymers:

- **Ester links** (PHBV, PLA, PCL, PGA) are hydrolysed to the original hydroxy acids.
- **Amide links next to glycine units** (nylon-2-nylon-6) resemble peptide bonds, which microbes can cleave.
- The hydrolysis products (lactic acid, glycolic acid, 3-hydroxybutanoic acid) are normal metabolites, which is why these polymers are safe for implants and sutures.

## The five exam polymers

| Polymer | Monomer(s) | Link | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| PHBV | 3-Hydroxybutanoic acid + 3-hydroxypentanoic acid | Ester | Orthopaedic devices, controlled drug release, speciality packaging |
| Nylon-2-nylon-6 | Glycine + 6-aminohexanoic acid | Amide | Biodegradable polyamide |
| PLA (polylactic acid) | Lactic acid (or its cyclic dimer, lactide) | Ester | Absorbable sutures, screws and pins, packaging |
| PCL (polycaprolactone) | ε-Caprolactone | Ester | Long-term implants, capsules for controlled drug release |
| PGA (polyglycolic acid) | Glycolic acid (or glycolide) | Ester | First synthetic absorbable suture (Dexon), bone-fixation parts |

## PHBV

PHBV (poly-β-hydroxybutyrate-co-β-hydroxyvalerate) is a copolymer of two β-hydroxy acids. Bacteria make it naturally as a storage material; in exam answers it is written as a condensation of the two acids:

<Eq ce="n CH3CH(OH)CH2COOH + n CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2COOH -> -(O-CH(CH3)-CH2-CO-O-CH(C2H5)-CH2-CO)_{n}- + 2n H2O" />

The ratio of the two units controls how brittle or flexible it is. It is degraded by bacteria in the environment.

## Nylon-2-nylon-6

An alternating polyamide of a C₂ amino acid (glycine) and a C₆ amino acid (6-aminohexanoic acid):

<Eq ce="n H2N-CH2-COOH + n H2N-(CH2)5-COOH -> -(NH-CH2-CO-NH-(CH2)5-CO)_{n}- + 2n H2O" />

The name follows the nylon rule: the numbers give the carbon count of each amino-acid unit (2 for glycine, 6 for aminocaproic acid).

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Glycine is H₂N–**CH₂**–COOH, not H₂N–CH₃–COOH: the middle carbon carries two H atoms and bonds to both N and the COOH group.
</ExamTip>

## Polylactic acid (PLA)

Lactic acid (2-hydroxypropanoic acid) condenses head-to-tail, losing water at each ester link:

<Eq ce="n CH3CH(OH)COOH -> -(O-CH(CH3)-CO)_{n}- + n H2O" />

Industrially, high-molecular-mass PLA is made by **ring-opening polymerisation of lactide**, the cyclic dimer of lactic acid, because direct condensation is reversible and water must be removed continuously. Lactic acid is obtained by fermenting starch from corn, potato waste or sugar. Inside the body PLA hydrolyses back to lactic acid over months, so it is used for dissolvable stitches, anchors, screws and pins.

## Polycaprolactone (PCL)

ε-Caprolactone is a seven-membered cyclic ester. PCL is made by **ring-opening polymerisation** with a catalyst:

<Eq ce="n C6H10O2 ->[catalyst, heat] -(O-(CH2)5-CO)_{n}-" />

The repeat unit is the same as that of 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid, which forms when the lactone is hydrolysed by acid:

<Eq ce="C6H10O2 + H2O ->[H3O+] HO-(CH2)5-COOH" />

PCL degrades more slowly than PLA, so it suits long-term implants and slow, controlled drug-release capsules.

## Polyglycolic acid (PGA)

The simplest aliphatic polyester, from glycolic acid (hydroxyethanoic acid):

<Eq ce="n HOCH2COOH -> -(O-CH2-CO)_{n}- + n H2O" />

PGA sutures lose about half their strength in two weeks and are fully absorbed in a few months. Copolymers with PLA are widely used for absorbable sutures.

## Exam tips

- "Which monomers give a biodegradable polymer?" Answer pairs: 3-hydroxybutanoic + 3-hydroxypentanoic acid; glycine + aminocaproic acid. Ethylene glycol + phthalic acid (glyptal) and caprolactam (nylon-6) are the wrong options.
- Biopolymers (starch, cellulose, proteins) are natural; biodegradable polymers can be synthetic. Do not mix the terms.
- All five are condensation-type polymers with an ester or amide link in the main chain.
