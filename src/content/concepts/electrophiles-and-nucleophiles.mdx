## What are electrophiles and nucleophiles?

An **electrophile** ("electron loving") is an electron-deficient species that **accepts an electron pair** to form a new bond; every electrophile is a Lewis acid. A **nucleophile** ("nucleus loving") is an electron-rich species that **donates an electron pair** to an electron-poor atom; every nucleophile is a Lewis base. In a polar organic reaction, the nucleophile always attacks the electrophile, and the curved arrow is drawn from the electron pair of the nucleophile to the electrophilic atom.

## Substrate and reagent

In most organic reactions one partner is called the **substrate** and the other the **reagent**. By the NCERT convention, the substrate is the molecule whose carbon forms the new bond, and the reagent is the species that attacks it.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> BrCH2-CH2Br" />

Here ethene is the substrate and bromine is the reagent. When a C–C bond forms between two organic molecules, the choice is arbitrary. Reagents are then classified by what they seek: **electrophiles** attack electron-rich sites (lone pairs, π bonds), and **nucleophiles** attack electron-poor sites (a δ+ carbon, a carbocation).

<KeyPoint title="One-line test">
Electrophile: has a vacant orbital or an incomplete octet, or an atom with a strong δ+ charge. Nucleophile: has a lone pair, a negative charge, a polarised C–metal bond, or loosely held π electrons.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples of electrophiles

| Type | Examples | Why it is electron-seeking |
|---|---|---|
| Cations | H⁺, Br⁺, Cl⁺, NO₂⁺, NO⁺, R₃C⁺ (carbocation) | positive charge with an empty orbital |
| Neutral, incomplete octet | BF₃, AlCl₃, BH₃ | boron or aluminium has only six valence electrons |
| Neutral, electron-deficient carbon | carbenes (:CCl₂), free radicals (R•), nitrenes (R–N:) | sextet or septet; seeks electrons to complete the octet |
| Polarised neutral molecules | R–X (δ+ C), R₂C=O (δ+ C), CO₂, SO₃, Br₂ when polarised | strongly δ+ central atom |
| Metal cations | Ag⁺, Hg²⁺ | accept electron pairs from lone pairs or π bonds |

Some of these appear repeatedly in named reactions: **dichlorocarbene** is the electrophile in the [Reimer–Tiemann reaction](/reactions/reimer-tiemann-reaction/), and **CO₂** is attacked by phenoxide in the [Kolbe–Schmitt reaction](/reactions/kolbe-schmitt-reaction/). In exam answers PCl₅ is also listed as an electrophile and the explanation given is its vacant d-orbitals on phosphorus.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Positive charge does not automatically mean electrophile. In **NH₄⁺** the nitrogen has a complete octet and no vacant orbital, so it cannot accept an electron pair. Likewise, metal species that act as electrophiles are **cations** such as Ag⁺ and Hg²⁺, not metal anions.
</ExamTip>

## Examples of nucleophiles

| Type | Examples | Donating site |
|---|---|---|
| Anions | OH⁻, RO⁻, I⁻, Br⁻, CN⁻, NO₂⁻, HS⁻, N₃⁻ | lone pair on the negative atom |
| Neutral, lone pairs | H₂O, ROH, NH₃, RNH₂, R₃N | lone pair on O or N |
| Carbon nucleophiles | RMgX, R₂CuLi, RZnX, RLi, acetylide ions, enolates | carbon is δ− in the polarised C–metal bond |
| Hydride donors | LiAlH₄, NaBH₄ | delivers H⁻ (hydrogen has oxidation state −1) |
| π nucleophiles | alkenes, alkynes, benzene | π electron cloud |

Water has two lone pairs on oxygen and acts as a nucleophile in hydrolysis and hydration reactions. Organometallic reagents and hydride reagents attack δ+ carbon, for example the carbonyl carbon in [Grignard additions](/reactions/grignard-reagent/) and [LiAlH₄ reductions](/reagents/lithium-aluminium-hydride/). Alkenes and arenes act as nucleophiles towards electrophiles in [electrophilic addition](/mechanisms/electrophilic-addition/) and [electrophilic aromatic substitution](/mechanisms/electrophilic-aromatic-substitution/).

## Species with both an electrophilic and a nucleophilic centre

Molecules with a **polar multiple bond** carry a δ+ atom and a δ− atom side by side. In a carbonyl compound, the carbon is δ+ and is attacked by nucleophiles, while the oxygen is δ− with lone pairs and is the site that is protonated or bound by a Lewis acid.

| Molecule | Electrophilic centre | Nucleophilic centre |
|---|---|---|
| Acetone, CH₃COCH₃ | carbonyl carbon | carbonyl oxygen |
| Acetonitrile, CH₃C≡N | nitrile carbon | nitrogen lone pair |

Such molecules are often called **ambiphiles** in exam preparation. The label is useful, but remember that a simple alkene (non-polar C=C) normally acts only as a nucleophile.

## Ambident nucleophiles

An **ambident nucleophile** has two (or more) **different** donor atoms, so it can bond through either one and give two different products.

| Ambident nucleophile | Attack through | Product with R–X |
|---|---|---|
| Cyanide, CN⁻ | C | R–C≡N (alkyl cyanide, with KCN) |
| | N | R–N≡C (isocyanide, with AgCN) |
| Nitrite, NO₂⁻ | O | R–O–N=O (alkyl nitrite, with KNO₂) |
| | N | R–NO₂ (nitroalkane, with AgNO₂) |
| Thiocyanate, SCN⁻ | S or N | R–SCN or R–NCS |
| Enolate ions | C or O | C-alkylated or O-alkylated product |

<Eq ce="R-X + KCN -> R-CN + KX" />
<Eq ce="R-X + AgCN -> R-NC + AgX" />

Ionic KCN and KNO₂ give free anions that attack through the atom carrying more negative charge (C of CN⁻, O of NO₂⁻). In AgCN and AgNO₂ the carbon or nitrogen is bonded to silver, so the other atom's lone pair attacks.

<KeyPoint title="Not ambident">
Carbonate (CO₃²⁻) is **not** ambident: all three donor atoms are equivalent oxygens, so attack through any of them gives the same product.
</KeyPoint>

## Exam tips

- To classify a species, look for a vacant orbital or sextet (electrophile) versus a lone pair, negative charge or π bond (nucleophile).
- Many species can be both: a carbonyl compound is electrophilic at carbon and nucleophilic at oxygen.
- NCERT asks directly: "What are ambident nucleophiles? Explain with an example." Answer with CN⁻ or NO₂⁻ and the KCN/AgCN or KNO₂/AgNO₂ pair.
- How strongly a nucleophile attacks depends on charge, size, steric bulk and solvent; see [nucleophilicity vs basicity](/comparisons/nucleophilicity-vs-basicity/) and [solvent effects](/concepts/solvent-effects/).
