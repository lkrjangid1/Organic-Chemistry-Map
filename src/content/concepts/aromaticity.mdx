  - q: "What is Hückel's rule of aromaticity?"
    a: "A cyclic, planar molecule or ion in which every ring atom is part of one continuous conjugated system is aromatic when it holds (4n + 2) π electrons, where n = 0, 1, 2 and so on. So 2, 6, 10, 14 or 18 π electrons give aromatic stability."
  - q: "Is cyclooctatetraene aromatic?"
    a: "No. Cyclooctatetraene (COT) has 8 π electrons and would be antiaromatic if flat, so it adopts a tub shape in which the double bonds are not conjugated. It is therefore non-aromatic and reacts like an ordinary polyene. Adding two electrons (for example with potassium) gives the planar, aromatic 10π COT dianion."
  - q: "What is the difference between antiaromatic and non-aromatic compounds?"
    a: "An antiaromatic compound is cyclic, planar and fully conjugated but has 4n π electrons, which makes it less stable than an open-chain analogue (cyclobutadiene). A non-aromatic compound fails one of the structural conditions, for example it is not planar or has an sp³ carbon breaking the conjugation (COT, cyclohexa-1,3-diene)."
  - q: "Why is the cyclopentadienyl anion aromatic but the cyclopentadienyl cation antiaromatic?"
    a: "Removing H⁺ from cyclopentadiene leaves a planar ring with 6 π electrons, a Hückel number, so the anion is aromatic and cyclopentadiene is unusually acidic (pKa about 16). Removing H⁻ instead leaves only 4 π electrons, so the cation is antiaromatic."

## What is aromaticity?

**Aromaticity** is the extra stability of a cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system that holds **(4n + 2) π electrons** (Hückel's rule). Benzene, with 6 π electrons, is the standard example. If the same flat, conjugated ring holds **4n** π electrons it is **antiaromatic** (destabilised), and if it fails any structural condition it is simply **non-aromatic**.

<KeyPoint title="Hückel's conditions">
1. The ring is **cyclic**. 2. It is **planar**. 3. Every ring atom has a p orbital, so conjugation runs **all the way round** (no sp³ carbon in the ring). 4. It has **(4n + 2) π electrons**: 2, 6, 10, 14, 18…
</KeyPoint>

## Aromatic, antiaromatic or non-aromatic?

| Type | Conditions | π electrons | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aromatic | cyclic, planar, fully conjugated | 4n + 2 | benzene, tropylium cation |
| Antiaromatic | cyclic, planar, fully conjugated | 4n | cyclobutadiene, cyclopentadienyl cation |
| Non-aromatic | a structural condition fails | any | cyclooctatetraene (tub), cyclohexa-1,3-diene |

Stability order for exam questions: **aromatic > non-aromatic > antiaromatic**. A molecule that would be antiaromatic when flat will twist out of plane if it can, becoming non-aromatic instead.

## Annulenes and cyclooctatetraene

| Annulene | π electrons | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| [4] cyclobutadiene | 4 | antiaromatic | forced to be planar |
| [6] benzene | 6 | aromatic | planar, 4n + 2 |
| [8] cyclooctatetraene | 8 | non-aromatic | tub shape breaks conjugation |
| [10] annulene | 10 | non-aromatic | cannot be flat: inner H atoms clash or angles are strained |
| [14] annulene | 14 | weakly aromatic | crowding of inner H |
| [18] annulene | 18 | aromatic | big enough to hold inner H atoms while flat |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not assume that every ring from 8 to 18 atoms is non-aromatic. [8] and [10]annulene are non-aromatic because they cannot be planar, but [18]annulene is planar and aromatic, and [14]annulene is weakly aromatic. Check planarity and the electron count for each ring.
</ExamTip>

**COT chemistry.** Cyclooctatetraene reacts with potassium to give K₂C₈H₈; the dianion is **planar and aromatic (10 π electrons)**. COT also interconverts with its valence isomer bicyclo[4.2.0]octa-2,4,7-triene by a thermal 6-electron electrocyclic process, and heating the bicyclic isomer opens it to COT.

<Eq ce="C8H8 (COT) + 2K -> 2K+ + C8H8^2- (planar, 10 pi)" />

## Aromatic and antiaromatic ions

| Ion | π electrons | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclopropenyl cation, C₃H₃⁺ | 2 (n = 0) | aromatic |
| Cyclopropenyl anion | 4 | antiaromatic |
| Cyclopentadienyl anion, C₅H₅⁻ | 6 | aromatic |
| Cyclopentadienyl cation | 4 | antiaromatic |
| Tropylium cation, C₇H₇⁺ | 6 | aromatic |
| COT dianion, C₈H₈²⁻ | 10 | aromatic |

How they are made:

<Eq ce="C3H3Cl (3-chlorocyclopropene) + SbCl5 -> C3H3+ SbCl6-" />

Antimony pentachloride is a very strong Lewis acid; AlCl₃ or a silver salt (AgBF₄) also pulls off chloride. The hexachloroantimonate salt of the parent cation is stable at −20 °C.

<Eq ce="C5H6 + NaH -> Na+ C5H5- + H2 ^" />

Cyclopentadiene (pKa about 16) is acidic because its conjugate base is aromatic. NaH is a much stronger base than NaOH and removes the proton irreversibly, releasing H₂. The tropylium cation is formed from cycloheptatriene with bromine or PCl₅ and gives stable salts such as tropylium perchlorate.

**Cyclobutadienyl dianion.** On paper, adding two electrons to cyclobutadiene (for example from 2 Na) gives a 6π ring, which satisfies Hückel's rule; exam questions treat this dianion as aromatic.

## Cyclobutadiene: the antiaromatic case

Cyclobutadiene (4 π electrons) is rectangular, with localised single and double bonds, and is extremely reactive. It can only be observed trapped at very low temperature (below about 35 K); otherwise one molecule acts as a diene and another as a dienophile, and it **dimerises by a Diels–Alder reaction** to a tricyclic dimer with three fused four-membered rings.

## Tropone and benzyne

- **Tropone** (cyclohepta-2,4,6-trien-1-one) has a strongly polarised C=O. In the dipolar form O⁻ carries the negative charge and the ring becomes a tropylium-like 6π cation, so tropone has a larger dipole moment (4.17 D) than cycloheptanone (3.04 D) and is unusually basic: it forms salts with strong acids such as HClO₄, giving a hydroxytropylium ion.
- **Benzyne** keeps the aromatic π sextet of benzene. Its extra "triple" bond comes from weak side-on overlap of two **in-plane** orbitals, which is why benzyne is highly strained and reactive.

## Inorganic benzene and sandwich compounds

- **Borazine, B₃N₃H₆** ("inorganic benzene", borazole) has alternating BH and NH units and is isoelectronic with benzene: each N lone pair is shared with the empty p orbital of a neighbouring B. The polar B–N bonds make its delocalisation weaker than benzene's, so it is far more reactive. **Hexagonal boron nitride (BN)ₙ** is the layered "inorganic graphite".
- **Ferrocene, Fe(C₅H₅)₂**: Fe²⁺ between two aromatic C₅H₅⁻ rings (6 π electrons each). EAN = 24 + 6 + 6 = **36**.
- **Bis(benzene)chromium, Cr(C₆H₆)₂**: Cr(0) between two neutral benzene rings. EAN = 24 + 6 + 6 = **36**.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Each cyclopentadienyl ring in ferrocene carries a −1 charge (two rings balance Fe²⁺), not −2. And Cr(C₆H₆)₂ is bis(benzene)chromium (dibenzenechromium); the name chromocene belongs to Cr(C₅H₅)₂.
</ExamTip>
