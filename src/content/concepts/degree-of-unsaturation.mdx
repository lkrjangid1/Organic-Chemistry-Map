## What is degree of unsaturation?

The **degree of unsaturation (DU)**, also called the **double bond equivalent (DBE)** or index of hydrogen deficiency, is the total number of **rings plus π bonds** in a molecule. It is worked out from the molecular formula alone: every ring or double bond removes two hydrogens from the saturated open-chain formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, so DU counts how many pairs of hydrogens are missing.

<KeyPoint title="Formula">
**DU = [(2C + 2) − H]/2** for a hydrocarbon CₓHᵧ.

With other elements: **DU = [(2C + 2) + N − H − X]/2**, or equivalently **C − H/2 − X/2 + N/2 + 1**.
</KeyPoint>

## How to treat other atoms

| Atom in the formula | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Halogen (F, Cl, Br, I) | count each one as an H | a halogen replaces a hydrogen (one bond) |
| Oxygen, sulphur | ignore | two bonds: fits into a chain without changing the H count |
| Nitrogen | subtract one H for each N | three bonds: a saturated amine has one extra H |

Example of the logic: chloroethane C₂H₅Cl behaves like C₂H₆, ethanol C₂H₆O like C₂H₆, and propylamine C₃H₉N like C₃H₈. All three are saturated (DU = 0).

## What each unit means

| Structural feature | Contribution to DU |
|---|---|
| Ring | 1 |
| C=C, C=O, C=N double bond | 1 |
| C≡C or C≡N triple bond | 2 |
| Benzene ring (one ring + three C=C) | 4 |

DU tells you the total, not the type. C₄H₆ (DU = 2) could be an alkyne, a diene, a cycloalkene or a bicyclic alkane. Use other clues (reaction with H₂, Br₂ water, ozonolysis) to decide.

## Worked examples

| Formula | Treat as | DU | One possible structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| C₆H₁₀ | C₆H₁₀ | (14 − 10)/2 = 2 | cyclohexene |
| C₆H₆ | C₆H₆ | (14 − 6)/2 = 4 | benzene |
| C₃H₆O | C₃H₆ | (8 − 6)/2 = 1 | acetone (one C=O) |
| C₂H₃N | C₂H₂ | (6 − 2)/2 = 2 | acetonitrile (C≡N) |
| C₄H₆Br₂ | C₄H₈ | (10 − 8)/2 = 1 | a dibromobutene |
| C₆H₅NO₂ | C₆H₄ | (14 − 4)/2 = 5 | nitrobenzene (ring 4 + N=O 1) |
| C₇H₆O₂ | C₇H₆ | (16 − 6)/2 = 5 | benzoic acid (ring 4 + C=O 1) |
| C₁₀H₈ | C₁₀H₈ | (22 − 8)/2 = 7 | naphthalene (2 rings + 5 C=C) |
| C₁₆H₁₃ClN₂O | C₁₆H₁₂ | (34 − 12)/2 = 11 | diazepam (3 rings + 8 double bonds) |

**Step-by-step for C₉H₉NO₄:** drop the four O atoms, then subtract one H for the N, giving C₉H₈. DU = (20 − 8)/2 = 6.

<Trick>
X → +H ; O, S → हटा दे ; N → −H. (Add a hydrogen for every halogen, drop O and S, take one hydrogen off for every nitrogen.)
</Trick>

## Charged species

For an ion, first turn it into the **neutral molecule it came from**, then use the formula. This follows from the general rule that each atom contributes (number of bonds − 2)/2, so what matters is how many bonds the charged atom actually forms.

| Ion | Neutral parent | DU |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃⁺ (carbocation, lost H⁻) | CH₄: add one H | 0 |
| CH₃O⁻ (alkoxide, lost H⁺) | CH₃OH: add one H | 0 |
| C₂H₅NH₃⁺ (ammonium, gained H⁺) | C₂H₅NH₂: remove one H | 0 |
| C₅H₆N⁺ (pyridinium, gained H⁺) | C₅H₅N: remove one H | 4 |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
There is no single rule of the type "positive charge: add H, negative charge: remove H". It works for a carbocation, but it gives wrong answers for alkoxide ions, carbanions and protonated amines. Always go back to the neutral parent.
</ExamTip>

## Exam tips

- For an ordinary neutral molecule the answer is always a whole number. A fraction means an arithmetic slip, a wrong formula or a radical.
- In exam problems, a DU of 4 or more in a formula with six or more carbons is a strong hint of a benzene ring.
- Catalytic hydrogenation adds H₂ across C=C and C≡C bonds but does not open ordinary rings. A C₆H₁₀ compound (DU = 2) that takes up only one mole of H₂ therefore has one ring and one C=C, as in cyclohexene.
