## What are the reactions of carboxylic acids?

Carboxylic acids, R–COOH, react at **four sites**: the acidic **O–H** bond, the **C–OH** bond (replaced in all the acid derivatives), the **–COOH group as a whole** (reduction, loss of CO₂), and the **hydrocarbon part**, especially the α-carbon. Their derivatives (acyl chlorides, anhydrides, esters, amides) all react by **nucleophilic acyl substitution**: a nucleophile adds to the C=O to form a tetrahedral intermediate, which then expels the leaving group.

## Acidity first

Carboxylic acids are much stronger acids than alcohols and most phenols because the negative charge of the carboxylate ion is shared equally by **two electronegative oxygen atoms**. Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity and electron-donating groups decrease it. See [acidity of carboxylic acids](/comparisons/acidity-of-carboxylic-acids/) for the full order. This acidity matters in synthesis: a Grignard reagent or organolithium is first destroyed by the COOH proton.

## The four reaction sites

| Site | Reaction | Reagent | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| O–H | salt formation | Na, NaOH, NaHCO₃ | RCOO⁻ Na⁺ (+ H₂, H₂O or CO₂) |
| C–OH | acid chloride | PCl₅, PCl₃ or SOCl₂ | RCOCl |
| C–OH | anhydride | P₂O₅ or heat with mineral acid | (RCO)₂O |
| C–OH | esterification | R′OH, conc. H₂SO₄ | RCOOR′ |
| C–OH | amide | NH₃, then strong heat | RCONH₂ |
| –COOH | reduction | LiAlH₄ or B₂H₆ | RCH₂OH |
| –COOH | decarboxylation | NaOH/CaO (soda lime), heat | RH |
| –COOH | Kolbe electrolysis | electrolysis of RCOOK | R–R |
| α-C–H | [HVZ reaction](/reactions/hell-volhard-zelinsky-reaction/) | X₂, red P | RCHXCOOH |

## Interconversion of derivatives

Reactivity towards nucleophiles is **acyl chloride &gt; anhydride &gt; ester &gt; amide**, which follows leaving-group ability (Cl⁻ is the weakest base, NH₂⁻ the strongest). An acyl chloride can be turned into any of the others; an amide cannot be turned directly into an acyl chloride. Ester cleavage is treated in detail in [ester hydrolysis mechanisms](/reactions/ester-hydrolysis/) (AAC2, BAC2, AAL1 and the others).

## Dehydrating agents and carboxylic acids

- **P₂O₅** dehydrates two molecules of a monocarboxylic acid to the anhydride.
- **Formic acid** with conc. H₂SO₄ gives **CO + H₂O** instead, and **oxalic acid** gives **CO + CO₂ + H₂O**; both are laboratory sources of carbon monoxide.
- **Malonic acid** does not form a normal anhydride: warming it with P₄O₁₀ removes two molecules of water to give **carbon suboxide**, O=C=C=C=O, which behaves like a "malonic anhydride" and reforms malonates.

What dicarboxylic, hydroxy and amino acids do on simple heating (decarboxylation, cyclic anhydrides, cyclic ketones, lactides, lactones, lactams) is covered on [effect of heat on carboxylic acids](/concepts/heating-effect-on-carboxylic-acids/).

## Ketene: an acylating agent

Ketene, CH₂=C=O, is made industrially by thermal dehydration of acetic acid and is the intermediate of the Wolff rearrangement in the [Arndt–Eistert synthesis](/reactions/arndt-eistert-synthesis/). The central carbon is strongly electrophilic, so nucleophiles add there and the CH₂ end picks up a proton:

| Ketene + | Product |
|---|---|
| H₂O | CH₃COOH |
| R′OH | CH₃COOR′ |
| NH₃ / R′NH₂ | CH₃CONH₂ / CH₃CONHR′ |
| R′COOH | mixed anhydride |

<Eq ce="CH2=C=O + H2O -> CH3COOH" />

In a labelling question, if H₂¹⁸O adds to ketene whose central carbon is ¹⁴C, the ¹⁴C becomes the carboxyl carbon and the ¹⁸O ends up in the –OH group of acetic acid.

## Condensation with urea

Urea's two NH₂ groups can condense with both carboxyl groups of a diacid, closing a ring with loss of two water molecules:

<Eq ce="CH2(COOH)2 + CO(NH2)2 ->[POCl3] C4H4N2O3 + 2 H2O" />

The product, C₄H₄N₂O₃, is **barbituric acid** (pyrimidine-2,4,6-trione), the parent of the barbiturate drugs. Grimaux's 1879 synthesis used malonic acid, urea and POCl₃; diethyl malonate is used today because the free acid's acidity interferes. In exam answers oxalic acid and urea are condensed in the same way to the five-membered **parabanic acid** (oxalylurea, imidazolidine-2,4,5-trione).

## Imides and NBS

Phthalic and succinic anhydrides react with ammonia, and the products cyclise on strong heating to the imides **phthalimide** (used in the [Gabriel synthesis](/reactions/gabriel-phthalimide-synthesis/)) and **succinimide**. Adding NaOH and Br₂ to cold aqueous succinimide precipitates **N-bromosuccinimide (NBS)**, the reagent for [allylic bromination](/reactions/nbs-allylic-bromination/). The N–H of an imide is acidic, so OH⁻ forms the imide anion, which takes Br from Br₂.

A longer sequence from phthalic acid: anhydride → phthaloyl chloride (PCl₅ or SOCl₂) → benzene-1,2-dimethanol (LiAlH₄) → phthalaldehyde (PCC) → 2-(hydroxymethyl)benzoate (conc. OH⁻, treated in exam answers as an intramolecular [Cannizzaro reaction](/reactions/cannizzaro-reaction/)) → acid → **phthalide**, the benzo-lactone obtained from 2-(hydroxymethyl)benzoic acid.

## Other reactions linked to this chapter

- **Organolithium and acids:** the first RLi removes the acidic proton; a second RLi adds to the carboxylate to give a dianion that hydrolyses (via the gem-diol) to a **ketone**, R–CO–R′. Organolithiums do this better than Grignard reagents.
- **Ipso substitution:** in electrophilic aromatic substitution, –COOH (lost as CO₂ + H⁺), –SO₃H, –SiR₃ and tert-butyl can leave instead of H⁺. So salicylic acid with bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol with loss of CO₂.
- **PCl₅ with ketones and aldehydes** converts C=O into CCl₂ (a gem-dichloride) with POCl₃ as by-product; with acids it gives the acyl chloride.
- **Acid-catalysed α-halogenation of ketones** goes through the enol and usually stops at **one** halogen, for example acetone with Cl₂/H⁺ gives chloroacetone. In base all α-hydrogens are replaced (see [haloform reaction](/reactions/haloform-reaction/)).
- **Strecker sulfite alkylation:** an alkyl halide with Na₂SO₃ gives a sodium **alkanesulfonate**, R–SO₃⁻Na⁺, with a **C–S bond** (sulfite attacks through sulphur); long-chain (paraffin) sulfonates are detergents.
- **Alcohol + conc. H₂SO₄ by temperature:** below about 140 °C ethanol gives ethyl hydrogen sulfate; at 140 °C (413 K) with excess alcohol the ether forms by SN2; above about 170 °C (443 K) the alkene forms. Propan-1-ol behaves the same way (propyl hydrogen sulfate, dipropyl ether, propene). See [dehydration of alcohols](/reactions/dehydration-of-alcohols/).

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
R–X + Na₂SO₃ does not give R–O–SO₂Na. The sulfite ion attacks through sulphur, so the product is the sulfonate R–SO₃Na, with carbon bonded directly to sulphur.
</ExamTip>

## Acetic acid as a reaction map

| Reagents on CH₃COOH | Product | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Br₂, red P | BrCH₂COOH | [HVZ](/reactions/hell-volhard-zelinsky-reaction/) |
| SOCl₂; CH₂N₂; Ag₂O, H₂O | CH₃CH₂COOH | [Arndt–Eistert](/reactions/arndt-eistert-synthesis/) |
| Ag salt, then Br₂/CCl₄, heat | CH₃Br | [Hunsdiecker](/reactions/hunsdiecker-reaction/) |
| HN₃, conc. H₂SO₄ | CH₃NH₂ | [Schmidt](/reactions/schmidt-reaction/) |
| NH₃, heat; then Br₂ + NaOH | CH₃NH₂ | [Hofmann bromamide](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/) |
| PCl₅; NaN₃, heat; H₂O | CH₃NH₂ | [Curtius](/reactions/curtius-rearrangement/) |
| KOH, then electrolysis | CH₃–CH₃ | [Kolbe electrolysis](/reactions/kolbe-electrolysis/) |
| sodium salt + soda lime, heat | CH₄ | [Decarboxylation](/reactions/decarboxylation/) |
| HI, red P (forcing, exam shorthand) | CH₃–CH₃ | [HI and red P](/reagents/hi-and-red-phosphorus/) |

<KeyPoint>
Step-up (one carbon more): Arndt–Eistert. Step-down to an amine: Hofmann, Curtius, Schmidt. Step-down to a halide: Hunsdiecker. Loss of CO₂ to an alkane: soda-lime decarboxylation; doubling the alkyl group: Kolbe electrolysis.
</KeyPoint>
