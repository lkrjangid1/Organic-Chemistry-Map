## What is nucleophilic substitution?

In a **nucleophilic substitution**, an electron-rich species (the **nucleophile**, Nu⁻ or Nu:) replaces a **leaving group** (Lg) on an electron-poor carbon. Alkyl halides are the classic substrates: the C–X bond is polarised, so carbon carries δ+ and the halide leaves as X⁻.

<Eq ce="Nu- + R-X -> R-Nu + X-" />

Most exam questions ask one thing: **which mechanism operates**, because that decides the rate law, the stereochemistry and whether the skeleton rearranges.

## SN1 vs SN2

| Feature | [SN2](/reactions/sn2-reaction/) | [SN1](/reactions/sn1-reaction/) |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | One, concerted | Two (or more), via carbocation |
| Rate law | k[RX][Nu⁻] | k[RX] |
| Substrate order | CH₃ > 1° > 2° > 3° | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃ (allyl, benzyl fast) |
| Nucleophile | Strong, usually anionic | Weak, often neutral (solvent) |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic (acetone, DMF, DMSO) | Polar protic (water, alcohols, acetic acid) |
| Stereochemistry | Inversion (Walden) | Racemisation, with some excess inversion |
| Rearrangement | Never | Possible (hydride or alkyl shift) |

<KeyPoint>
SN2 reactivity ∝ stability of the transition state ∝ 1/steric hindrance. SN1 reactivity ∝ stability of the carbocation. Both need a good leaving group: leaving-group ability ∝ 1/basicity ∝ stability of the anion.
</KeyPoint>

## Factors that decide the pathway

- **Substrate.** Steric crowding blocks SN2; carbocation stability drives SN1. Tertiary, aryl, vinyl and bridgehead halides give no SN2.
- **Nucleophile.** Negative charge and lower electronegativity raise nucleophilicity (OH⁻ is better than H₂O). Bulky bases such as tert-butoxide favour elimination instead.
- **Solvent.** Protic solvents hydrogen-bond to anions and slow SN2 but stabilise carbocations for SN1.
- **Leaving group.** The weaker its conjugate base character, the better it leaves: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻; tosylate, –N₂⁺ and protonated –OH are excellent.

## Substitution with retention

Two special mechanisms give **retention** instead of inversion:

- [SNi mechanism](/reactions/sni-mechanism/): SOCl₂ converts an alcohol to an alkyl chloride through a chlorosulfite and a tight ion pair; chlorine is delivered from the front. With pyridine the path becomes SN2 (inversion).
- [Neighbouring group participation](/reactions/neighbouring-group-participation/): a lone pair or π bond inside the molecule displaces the leaving group first, then the external nucleophile opens the bridged ring. Two inversions give retention, and the rate is often much higher.

## Substitution on aromatic rings

- [Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr / ArSN2)](/reactions/nucleophilic-aromatic-substitution/): addition–elimination through a Meisenheimer carbanion; needs –NO₂ or similar groups at ortho/para positions, or 623 K and 300 atm for chlorobenzene. ArSN1 is the aryl-cation path of diazonium salts.
- [Benzyne mechanism](/reactions/benzyne-mechanism/): elimination–addition with NaNH₂ in liquid NH₃, giving direct and cine products.

## Substitution at a carbonyl carbon (SN2AE)

Acid derivatives R–CO–G (G = –X, –OCOR, –OR, –NH₂, –OH, but not H or R) react by **addition–elimination**, written SN2AE or SN2th (via a tetrahedral intermediate) in exam answers:

1. Nu⁻ adds to the sp² carbonyl carbon, giving a **tetrahedral sp³ intermediate** R–C(O⁻)(Nu)–G.
2. The C=O re-forms and G⁻ is expelled, giving R–CO–Nu.

<Eq ce="R-CO-G + Nu- <=> R-C(O^-)(Nu)-G -> R-CO-Nu + G-" />

Both steps are reversible. Reactivity follows the leaving ability of G and the loss of resonance on forming the intermediate: **acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide**. Aldehydes and ketones do not substitute, because H⁻ and R⁻ are not leaving groups; they stop at addition. In exam answers, SN2th at an acyl carbon is said to be faster than SN2 at an sp³ carbon, because the planar, strongly δ+ carbonyl carbon is open to attack (reactivity ∝ positive charge on the sp² carbon ∝ 1/steric hindrance).

## Common substitution reactions of alkyl halides

| Reagent | Product | Page |
|---|---|---|
| NaI in acetone | Alkyl iodide | [Finkelstein reaction](/reactions/finkelstein-reaction/) |
| AgF, Hg₂F₂, SbF₃ | Alkyl fluoride | [Swarts reaction](/reactions/swarts-reaction/) |
| NaOR′ | Ether | [Williamson ether synthesis](/reactions/williamson-ether-synthesis/) |
| KCN / AgCN | Nitrile / isocyanide | [SN2 reaction](/reactions/sn2-reaction/) |
| KNO₂ / AgNO₂ | Alkyl nitrite / nitroalkane | [SN2 reaction](/reactions/sn2-reaction/) |
| NH₃ (excess RX) | 1°, 2°, 3° amines, then R₄N⁺X⁻ | this page |

**Ammonolysis.** NH₃ attacks R–X to give a primary amine. The amine is itself a nucleophile, so it reacts with more R–X to give secondary and tertiary amines and finally a **quaternary ammonium salt**. A large excess of NH₃ favours the primary amine; excess alkyl halide drives the reaction to R₄N⁺X⁻.

<Eq ce="RX ->[NH3] RNH2 ->[RX] R2NH ->[RX] R3N ->[RX] R4N+X-" />

**Hydrolysis of gem-dihalides.** Aqueous KOH replaces one halogen of R–CHX₂ by OH; the oxygen lone pair then pushes out the second halide, so the product is the aldehyde R–CHO (a gem-dihalide on an internal carbon gives a ketone).

Reactions of alcohols and ethers that follow the same mechanisms are covered on the [Lucas test](/tests/lucas-test/) (SN1 of 3° alcohols with HCl/ZnCl₂) and [ether cleavage with HX](/reactions/ether-cleavage-with-hx/) pages.

<ExamTip>
Read the question in this order: substrate (1°/2°/3°, allylic, aryl) → nucleophile strength and bulk → solvent → temperature. Hot conditions and strong bulky bases switch substitution to elimination; see [elimination reactions](/mechanisms/elimination-reactions/).
</ExamTip>
