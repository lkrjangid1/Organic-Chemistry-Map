## What are diazonium salts?

An **arenediazonium salt**, ArN₂⁺X⁻, contains the –N≡N⁺ group bonded to an aromatic ring. The best-known example is **benzenediazonium chloride (BDC)**, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻, made from aniline by [diazotization](/reactions/diazotization-reaction/) with NaNO₂ and HCl at **273–278 K (0–5 °C)**. Because N₂ is an excellent leaving group, the diazonium group can be swapped for many other atoms or groups, which makes these salts the most versatile intermediates in aromatic chemistry.

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl ->[273-278 K] C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + NaCl + 2H2O" />

<KeyPoint title="Stability">
Benzenediazonium chloride is a colourless solid that is stable only in cold solution and can explode when dry, so it is used straight away. Benzenediazonium tetrafluoroborate is insoluble in water and stable at room temperature. Aliphatic diazonium ions lose N₂ immediately.
</KeyPoint>

## How amines react with nitrous acid

| Amine | Product with HNO₂ (cold, acidic) | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| 1° aliphatic, RNH₂ | R⁺ → ROH (major), plus ROR, RONO, RCl, alkene; rearrangement possible | Brisk evolution of N₂ |
| 1° aromatic, ArNH₂ | ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ (stable at 0–5 °C) | No N₂ in the cold; couples with phenol to give a dye |
| 2°, R₂NH | R₂N–N=O (N-nitrosamine) | Yellow oil separates |
| 3° aliphatic, R₃N | R₃NH⁺ (acid–base reaction only) | Clear solution |
| 3° aromatic, C₆H₅NR₂ | p-ON–C₆H₄–NR₂ (ring nitrosation by NO⁺) | Substitution on the ring, not at N |

In every case the electrophile is the **nitrosonium ion, NO⁺**, formed from protonated nitrous acid. For N,N-dimethylaniline there can be a further step: HNO₃, acting as an oxidising agent, converts the p-nitroso group into a p-nitro group.

## Reactions with loss of nitrogen

The replacement reactions of benzenediazonium chloride at a glance:

| Reagent | Product | Name / comment |
|---|---|---|
| Warm H₂O (about 283 K) | C₆H₅OH | Hydrolysis to phenol |
| CuCl/HCl, CuBr/HBr | C₆H₅Cl, C₆H₅Br | [Sandmeyer reaction](/reactions/sandmeyer-reaction/) |
| Cu powder + HCl/HBr | C₆H₅Cl, C₆H₅Br | [Gattermann reaction](/reactions/gattermann-reaction/) |
| CuCN/KCN | C₆H₅CN | Sandmeyer cyanation |
| KI | C₆H₅I | No copper needed |
| HBF₄, then heat | C₆H₅F + N₂ + BF₃ | [Balz–Schiemann reaction](/reactions/balz-schiemann-reaction/) |
| ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ + NaNO₂, Cu, heat | C₆H₅NO₂ | Nitro replacement |
| H₃PO₂ or C₂H₅OH | C₆H₆ | Reduction (deamination) |
| Thiolate (e.g. KSH) | C₆H₅SH | Sandmeyer-type, no catalyst |

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + H3PO2 + H2O -> C6H6 + N2 + H3PO3 + HCl" />

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + CH3CH2OH -> C6H6 + N2 + CH3CHO + HCl" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing KCN alone for the cyanide replacement, or AgNO₂ for the nitro replacement. The cyanide comes from **CuCN** (a Sandmeyer reaction), and the nitro replacement from the fluoroborate uses **aqueous NaNO₂ with copper** on heating (NCERT).
</ExamTip>

## Mechanisms compared

A common shortcut is to draw one general mechanism for all of these: ArN₂⁺ loses N₂ in a slow step to give an aryl cation, which is then captured by the nucleophile. In fact the reactions fall into two groups:

- **Aryl cation (SN1-like):** hydrolysis to phenol and the thermal Balz–Schiemann reaction. No metal is needed and no rearrangement occurs.
- **Aryl radical (electron transfer):** the copper-catalysed Sandmeyer and Gattermann reactions, the KI reaction (iodide is itself a good enough reducing agent) and reduction by H₃PO₂. Biaryl by-products support the radical route.

## Reactions with retention of nitrogen: azo coupling

The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile. With very electron-rich rings it attacks the para position and keeps both nitrogen atoms, giving coloured **azo compounds** in which two rings are joined by –N=N–: phenol gives p-hydroxyazobenzene and aniline gives p-aminoazobenzene. The extended conjugation makes these compounds coloured, and they are used as dyes. This is an electrophilic substitution on the phenol or aniline ring.

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + C6H5OH -> C6H5-N=N-C6H4OH + HCl" />

## Factors that control the outcome

- **Temperature:** below 278 K the salt survives; at about 283 K it hydrolyses to phenol.
- **Counter-ion:** Cl⁻ salts are used in solution; BF₄⁻ salts can be isolated and are needed for fluorination.
- **Catalyst:** Cu(I) or Cu powder is required for Cl, Br and CN, but not for I, F, OH or H.
- **Ring substituents:** electron-donating groups make the diazonium ion less reactive as an electrophile.
