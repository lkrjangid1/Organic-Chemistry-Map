## What is a free radical reaction?

A **free radical reaction** is one that goes through intermediates carrying an **unpaired electron**. Radicals form when a covalent bond breaks **homolytically**, each atom taking one electron of the shared pair. Weak bonds break most easily, which is why **light (hν)** or **heat** acting on Cl–Cl, Br–Br or the O–O bond of a peroxide is the usual starting point.

<Eq ce="R-O-O-R ->[light] R-O^{.} + R-O^{.}" />

Radical reactions are typical of **non-polar conditions**: gas phase or non-polar solvents such as CCl₄, light or peroxide initiators, and no strong acids or bases. Oxygen is a radical trap and slows them down.

## Stability of free radicals

<KeyPoint>
Allyl ≈ benzyl (resonance) greater than 3° greater than 2° greater than 1° greater than CH₃• (hyperconjugation). Reactivity ∝ stability of the radical formed: the hydrogen whose removal gives the most stable radical is replaced first.
</KeyPoint>

## The general pattern: a chain reaction

1. **Initiation:** a radical is created, e.g. Cl₂ → 2Cl• under light.
2. **Propagation:** a radical reacts with a molecule and produces a new radical, e.g. Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl, then CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•. One photon can start thousands of cycles.
3. **Termination:** radicals are destroyed in pairs.
   - **Combination:** R• + R• → R–R.
   - **Disproportionation:** one radical gives a β-H to the other, forming an alkene and an alkane (2CH₃CH₂• → CH₂=CH₂ + CH₃CH₃).

Both termination routes are exothermic and need little or no activation energy. The share of disproportionation grows with the number of β-hydrogens, so tertiary radicals disproportionate more readily than primary ones.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Linking disproportionation to high temperature and a sizeable activation energy. Like combination, it is nearly barrier-free.
</ExamTip>

## Factors that control radical reactions

- **Bond strength:** initiation breaks the weakest bond present (Br–Br weaker than Cl–Cl; O–O of peroxides is very weak).
- **Radical stability:** decides *where* substitution or addition occurs (allylic/benzylic and 3° positions first).
- **Reactivity vs selectivity:** a less reactive radical is more selective. Per-hydrogen rates (1° : 2° : 3°) are 1 : 3.8 : 5 for chlorination but roughly 1 : 80 : 1600 for bromination, because Br• is far less reactive than Cl•.
- **Thermodynamics of each step:** a chain runs only if every propagation step is favourable. This is why HBr alone shows the peroxide effect and why direct iodination of alkanes fails while fluorination is explosive.
- **Concentration:** NBS keeps Br₂ low so that substitution beats addition.

## Other alkane reactions

**Nitration (vapour phase).** Propane with nitric acid at about 350–450 °C gives a mixture of **1-nitropropane, 2-nitropropane, nitroethane and nitromethane**. Alkoxy radicals formed during the reaction can break C–C bonds, which is why the smaller nitroalkanes appear.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH3 ->[conc. HNO3][vapour phase] CH3CH2CH2NO2 + CH3CH(NO2)CH3 + CH3CH2NO2 + CH3NO2" />

**Sulphonation.** In exam answers, heating propane with concentrated H₂SO₄ is written as giving propane-1- and propane-2-sulphonic acids. Treat this as a classroom statement: the references checked do not describe it, and the standard route for putting a sulphur group on an alkane is the Reed reaction (SO₂ and Cl₂ under light, giving sulphonyl chlorides).

**Isomerisation.** n-Alkanes heated with **anhydrous AlCl₃ and HCl gas** rearrange to branched isomers (n-butane → isobutane; n-hexane → 2- and 3-methylpentane). This is an ionic (carbocation) process, not a radical one. Industrially, platinum catalysts are also used, and branching raises the octane number.

**Aromatisation (reforming).** n-Alkanes with six or more carbons, heated to **773 K at 10–20 atm** over **oxides of vanadium, molybdenum or chromium supported on alumina**, are dehydrogenated and cyclised: n-hexane gives benzene. **Common mistake:** listing manganese as a catalyst ("V, Cr, Mn"); the NCERT catalyst list has molybdenum, not manganese.

<Eq ce="C6H14 ->[V, Mo or Cr oxide on Al2O3][773 K, 10-20 atm] C6H6 + 4H2" />

**Pyrolysis (cracking).** Heating higher alkanes strongly breaks them into smaller alkanes, alkenes and hydrogen; NCERT describes pyrolysis as a **free radical reaction**. For example, propane above 500 °C gives propene, ethene, methane, ethane, ethyne, propyne, propadiene and H₂.

**Benzene hexachloride.** Under UV light benzene **adds** three Cl₂ molecules to give C₆H₆Cl₆ (hexachlorocyclohexane, "666", BHC). The γ-isomer, **lindane (gammexane)**, is the most insecticidal (कीटनाशी, insecticide).

<Eq ce="C6H6 + 3Cl2 ->[UV light] C6H6Cl6" />

<KeyPoint>
**Boiling point ∝ surface area ∝ molecular mass ∝ 1/branching.** n-Butane boils at 0 °C but isobutane at −12 °C. **Octane number** is the scale for fuel quality (ईंधन की Quality का पता लगाने का पैमाना): n-heptane = 0, iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane) = 100.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip>
Identify a radical question by its conditions: hν, peroxide, NBS, high temperature or vapour phase, CCl₄ as solvent. Then ask which radical is most stable; that decides the major product. Unlike carbocations, simple alkyl radicals generally do not undergo hydride or methyl shifts, so expect no rearranged products.
</ExamTip>
