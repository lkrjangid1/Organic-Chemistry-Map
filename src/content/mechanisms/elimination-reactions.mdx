## What is an elimination reaction?

In an **elimination reaction** two atoms or groups are removed from a molecule and a **multiple bond** (or a carbene) is formed. Usually a hydrogen and a leaving group (halide, water from a protonated alcohol, an ammonium group) are lost from **adjacent carbons**, and the product is an **alkene**. Elimination is the reverse of addition and the main competitor of nucleophilic substitution.

## α-Elimination and β-elimination

- **β-Elimination (1,2-elimination):** the groups leave from neighbouring carbons (Cα carries the leaving group, Cβ the hydrogen) and a C=C bond forms. This is by far the most common type.
- **α-Elimination (1,1-elimination):** both groups leave from the **same carbon**, giving a **carbene**. The classic case is chloroform with a base: H⁺ and Cl⁻ are both lost from one carbon to give dichlorocarbene, the intermediate in the Reimer–Tiemann and carbylamine reactions.

<Eq ce="CHCl3 + OH- -> :CCl2 + H2O + Cl-" />

## Common β-eliminations

| Type | Group lost | Reagent | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dehydration | H₂O | Conc. H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄, Al₂O₃, heat | Ethanol to ethene at 443 K |
| Dehydrohalogenation | HX | Alcoholic KOH, NaOEt, KOtBu | 2-Bromopentane to pent-2-ene |
| Dehalogenation | X₂ | Zn dust or NaI in acetone, heat | 2,3-Dibromobutane to but-2-ene |
| Hofmann elimination | R₃N + H₂O | CH₃I, Ag₂O, heat | Amine to least substituted alkene |
| Cope elimination | R₂NOH | Heat on an amine N-oxide | Amine oxide to alkene |

<Eq ce="CH3CHBr-CHBrCH3 ->[Zn][\Delta] CH3CH=CHCH3 + ZnBr2" />

A related dehydration: a β-hydroxy (or β-amino) acid loses water (or ammonia) to give the conjugated unsaturated acid, because the α-H next to the C=O is acidic.

<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)CH2COOH -> CH3CH=CHCOOH + H2O" />

## The four mechanisms compared

| Feature | E1 | E2 | E1cB | Ei |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steps | Two | One | Two | One |
| Intermediate | Carbocation | None | Carbanion | None (cyclic TS) |
| Rate law | k[RX] | k[RX][B⁻] | Depends on slow step | k[substrate] |
| Base | Weak or none | Strong | Moderate to strong | None (internal) |
| Leaving group | Good | Good | Poor (OH, OR, F) | Built into the molecule |
| Stereochemistry | Not stereospecific | Anti-periplanar | Not required | Syn |
| Rearrangement | Possible | No | No | No |
| Typical substrate | 3° halides, 2°/3° alcohols in acid | 1°/2°/3° halides with strong base | β-Hydroxy carbonyls | Amine oxides, esters, xanthates |

## Saytzeff or Hofmann alkene?

When there is more than one kind of β-hydrogen, two alkenes can form. **Saytzeff's (Zaitsev's) rule:** the **more substituted, more stable alkene** is normally major. There are three cases in which the **Hofmann (less substituted) alkene** is major instead:

1. **The leaving group is poor or positively charged**: F, –NR₃⁺, –SR₂⁺. Here the C–H bond breaks well ahead of the C–X bond (carbanion-like transition state), so the most acidic, least hindered hydrogen is removed.
2. **The base is bulky**, for example potassium tert-butoxide: 2-chlorobutane gives mainly but-1-ene.
3. **The substrate is crowded** near the more substituted β-carbon: 2-chloro-4,4-dimethylpentane gives mainly 4,4-dimethylpent-1-ene.

<KeyPoint>
Small base and halide leaving group: Saytzeff. Bulky base, onium or fluoride leaving group, or crowded substrate: Hofmann. Ei reactions (Cope, ester pyrolysis) also usually give the Hofmann alkene.
</KeyPoint>

## Elimination versus substitution

Elimination and substitution compete (E2 with SN2, E1 with SN1). Elimination is favoured by:

- more substitution at the α-carbon (3° greater than 2° greater than 1°),
- a stronger, bulkier, less nucleophilic base,
- higher temperature,
- an alcoholic rather than aqueous medium.

<ExamTip>
Aqueous KOH on chloroethane gives ethanol (substitution); alcoholic KOH gives ethene (elimination). With conc. H₂SO₄, ethanol gives diethyl ether at 413 K but ethene at 443 K. Questions often test exactly this switch.
</ExamTip>
