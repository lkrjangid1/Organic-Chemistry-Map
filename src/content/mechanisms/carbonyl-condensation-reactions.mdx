  - q: "What is a carbonyl condensation reaction?"
    a: "It is a reaction between two carbonyl partners in which one, after losing an α-hydrogen, acts as a carbon nucleophile (an enolate or enol) and the other acts as the electrophile. A new C–C bond forms at the α-carbon of the nucleophile, often with loss of water or an alcohol."
  - q: "Why are α-hydrogens of carbonyl compounds acidic?"
    a: "When an α-hydrogen is removed, the negative charge of the enolate ion is delocalised onto the electronegative carbonyl oxygen. Aldehydes have α-H pKa values near 17, ketones about 19–20 and esters about 25; a CH₂ between two carbonyl groups is far more acidic (pKa 9–13)."

  - q: "What is an active methylene compound?"
    a: "A compound with a CH₂ group between two electron-withdrawing groups, such as diethyl malonate, ethyl acetoacetate or acetylacetone. Its hydrogens are acidic enough to be removed by alkoxides or even weak amine bases, as used in the Knoevenagel condensation."
  - q: "Which carbonyl condensation gives a 1,5-dicarbonyl compound?"
    a: "The Michael addition of an enolate (or an enamine in the Stork reaction) to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound. An intramolecular aldol condensation of the 1,5-diketone then gives a cyclohexenone in the Robinson annulation."

## What is a carbonyl condensation reaction?

A **carbonyl condensation** joins two molecules through a new **C–C bond at the α-carbon** of a carbonyl (or similar) compound. One partner loses an α-hydrogen to a base and becomes a carbon nucleophile, an **enolate** (or a neutral enol or enamine). The other partner supplies an **electrophilic carbon**, usually a C=O. Many of these reactions then lose a small molecule such as water or an alcohol, which is why they are called condensations.

## Where the nucleophile comes from

A hydrogen on the carbon next to C=O is acidic because the negative charge of the enolate is shared with the electronegative oxygen. The more electron-withdrawing groups on the carbon, the easier the deprotonation, and the weaker the base needed.

| Compound type | Example | Approx. pKa of α-H | Base usually used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ester | CH₃COOEt | 25 | NaOEt (Claisen) |
| Nitrile | CH₃C≡N | 25 | strong base (Thorpe) |
| Ketone | CH₃COCH₃ | 19–20 | dil. NaOH or Ba(OH)₂ (aldol) |
| Aldehyde | CH₃CHO | 17 | dil. NaOH (aldol) |
| 1,3-Diester (malonic ester) | CH₂(COOEt)₂ | 13 | NaOEt or an amine |
| β-Keto ester | CH₃COCH₂COOEt | 11 | NaOEt or an amine |
| 1,3-Diketone, nitroalkane | CH₃COCH₂COCH₃, CH₃NO₂ | 9 | weak base (amine, OH⁻) |

<KeyPoint title="Active methylene compounds">
A CH₂ flanked by two electron-withdrawing groups (Z–CH₂–Z′) is an **active methylene** group. Malonic ester, acetoacetic ester and acetylacetone are more acidic than water, so their enolates form with alkoxides or even amines. These are the preferred donors in Knoevenagel and Michael reactions.
</KeyPoint>

## Three ways the carbanion can react

1. **1,2-Addition to an aldehyde or ketone C=O.** The alkoxide is protonated to a β-hydroxy compound, which may lose water to an α,β-unsaturated product (aldol, Henry, Knoevenagel, Perkin).
2. **Addition–elimination at an ester C=O.** The tetrahedral intermediate expels an alkoxide, giving a β-keto ester (Claisen, Dieckmann). The β-keto ester is deprotonated by the base, which drives the equilibrium, so a full equivalent of base is needed.
3. **1,4-Addition to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl.** The carbanion adds to the β-carbon, giving a 1,5-dicarbonyl product (Michael, Stork, first step of Robinson).

## The condensations compared

| Reaction | Nucleophile (donor) | Electrophile | Base / conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Aldol](/reactions/aldol-condensation/) | Aldehyde or ketone with α-H | Same molecule | dil. NaOH; Δ for dehydration | β-Hydroxy carbonyl, then enal/enone |
| [Cross aldol / Claisen–Schmidt](/reactions/cross-aldol-condensation/) | Ketone or aldehyde with α-H | Aldehyde, often without α-H | aq. NaOH | α,β-Unsaturated carbonyl |
| [Claisen](/reactions/claisen-condensation/) | Ester with α-H | Second ester | NaOEt (1 equiv.) | β-Keto ester |
| [Dieckmann](/reactions/dieckmann-condensation/) | Diester (intramolecular) | Same molecule | NaOEt | Cyclic β-keto ester |
| [Perkin](/reactions/perkin-reaction/) | Acid anhydride | Aromatic aldehyde | Sodium salt of the acid, Δ | α,β-Unsaturated aromatic acid |
| [Knoevenagel](/reactions/knoevenagel-condensation/) | Active methylene compound | Aldehyde or ketone | Piperidine or pyridine | C=C(Z)Z′ product |
| [Henry (nitroaldol)](/reactions/henry-reaction/) | Nitroalkane | Aldehyde or ketone | Catalytic base | β-Nitro alcohol |
| [Thorpe](/reactions/thorpe-reaction/) | Nitrile with α-H | Second nitrile (C≡N) | Strong base | β-Enaminonitrile |
| [Michael](/reactions/michael-addition/) | Stabilised enolate (malonate etc.) | α,β-Unsaturated carbonyl | NaOEt | 1,5-Dicarbonyl |
| [Stork enamine](/reactions/stork-enamine-reaction/) | Enamine of a ketone | Michael acceptor, R–X, RCOCl | R₂NH, then H₃O⁺ | α-Substituted ketone |
| [Robinson annulation](/reactions/robinson-annulation/) | Enolate or enamine | Methyl vinyl ketone | Base, then Δ | Cyclohexenone |

<ExamTip>
In every condensation, find the **acidic α-carbon** first: it is the one that becomes bonded to the electrophilic carbon. Then decide whether that electrophile is a C=O of an aldehyde or ketone (alcohol, possibly dehydrated), an ester (β-keto ester), or the β-carbon of an enone (1,5-dicarbonyl).
</ExamTip>

## Factors that decide the outcome

- **Which partner has α-hydrogens.** Aldehydes without α-H (HCHO, C₆H₅CHO) can only be electrophiles, which keeps crossed reactions clean (Claisen–Schmidt, Perkin). With strong bases they may undergo the [Cannizzaro reaction](/reactions/cannizzaro-reaction/) instead.
- **Base strength.** Match the base to the acidity of the donor. A base that is too strong deprotonates the carbonyl partner too and causes self-condensation, which is why Knoevenagel uses amines.
- **Reversibility and dehydration.** Aldol and Henry additions are reversible. Heating removes water to give the conjugated product and pulls the equilibrium forward.
- **Ring size.** Intramolecular versions (intramolecular aldol, Dieckmann, Thorpe–Ziegler, Robinson) form five- and six-membered rings most easily.
- **Hard versus soft nucleophiles.** Stabilised enolates and enamines add 1,4 to α,β-unsaturated carbonyls; strongly basic organometallics prefer 1,2-addition.

## A related α-carbon reaction: Tilden's reagent

Nitrosyl chloride, NOCl, is known as **Tilden's reagent** after William A. Tilden, who first prepared it pure. It is a strong electrophile. Enolisable aldehydes and ketones react with it through the enol: NOCl attacks the nucleophilic α-carbon, and the product is a **vicinal keto- or aldo-oxime**. It also adds to alkenes in a Markovnikov sense to give α-chloro oximes, and Tilden used it to make crystalline derivatives that helped identify terpenes.
