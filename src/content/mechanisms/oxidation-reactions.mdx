## What is an oxidation reaction?

In organic chemistry, **oxidation** is any change that **adds oxygen**, **removes hydrogen** or **removes electrons**, so that the **oxidation number of carbon increases**. Reduction is the opposite. Turning an alcohol into an aldehyde is an oxidation because two hydrogens are lost; NCERT points out that alcohol oxidations are therefore also called **dehydrogenation** reactions.

<KeyPoint>
Oxidation = gain of O, loss of H, loss of e⁻, rise in oxidation number. One oxidation step usually means one more C–O bond or one fewer C–H bond on the carbon that changes.
</KeyPoint>

## The oxidation-state ladder of carbon

Counting C–H bonds as −1 and C–O bonds as +1 for each carbon gives a ladder that every oxidation question climbs:

| Compound | Functional carbon | Oxidation number of that C |
|---|---|---|
| CH₄ | alkane | −4 |
| CH₃OH | alcohol | −2 |
| HCHO | aldehyde | 0 |
| HCOOH | carboxylic acid | +2 |
| CO₂ | carbon dioxide | +4 |

The same pattern holds for larger molecules: alkane → alcohol → aldehyde or ketone → carboxylic acid → CO₂. A **ketone cannot be oxidised further without breaking a C–C bond**, which is why ketones resist mild oxidants.

## Oxidation of alcohols

- **1° alcohol** → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (two steps; the second needs water, which converts the aldehyde to its hydrate).
- **2° alcohol** → ketone, and normally stops there.
- **3° alcohol** → no oxidation under normal conditions, because there is no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon. Strong oxidants (hot KMnO₄) at high temperature break C–C bonds and give a mixture of acids with fewer carbon atoms.

In exam answers the ease of oxidation is written as **1° greater than 2° greater than 3°** (3° alcohols do not oxidise, ऑक्सीकरण नहीं होता है). With Cr(VI) reagents the chromate ester breaks down by loss of the α-hydrogen, and that C–H cleavage is the slow step; a tertiary alcohol forms the ester but has no α-hydrogen to lose.

## Copper at 573 K: dehydrogenation vs dehydration

When alcohol vapours are passed over **heated copper at 573 K (300 °C)**, primary and secondary alcohols lose H₂, while tertiary alcohols lose H₂O instead.

| Alcohol | Product over Cu, 573 K | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanol (1°) | acetaldehyde | dehydrogenation |
| Propan-2-ol (2°) | acetone | dehydrogenation |
| 2-Methylpropan-2-ol (3°) | 2-methylpropene | dehydration |

<Eq ce="CH3CH2OH ->[Cu][573 K] CH3CHO + H2" />

<Eq ce="(CH3)2CHOH ->[Cu][573 K] (CH3)2C=O + H2" />

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-OH ->[Cu][573 K] (CH3)2C=CH2 + H2O" />

<ExamTip>
Cu at 573 K is the classic test of alcohol class without a solution reagent: aldehyde means 1°, ketone means 2°, alkene means 3°.
</ExamTip>

The rule behind the table: **water present means over-oxidation of aldehydes**, because the aldehyde hydrate, RCH(OH)₂, is oxidised again. Anhydrous reagents (PCC, PDC or Collins in CH₂Cl₂) stop at the aldehyde.

## Oxidation of other functional groups

- **Alkenes.** Cold dilute KMnO₄ gives a syn-diol; peracids give epoxides ([Prilezhaev epoxidation](/reactions/prilezhaev-epoxidation/)); [ozonolysis](/reactions/ozonolysis/) and hot KMnO₄ cleave the C=C; PdCl₂/CuCl₂/O₂ gives a carbonyl compound without cleavage ([Wacker oxidation](/reactions/wacker-oxidation/)). [Hydroboration–oxidation](/reactions/hydroboration-oxidation/) uses H₂O₂ to give the anti-Markovnikov alcohol.
- **Vicinal diols.** HIO₄ cuts the C–C bond between the two OH groups ([Malaprade oxidation](/reactions/malaprade-oxidation/)).
- **Methylarenes.** Chromyl chloride stops at the aldehyde ([Étard reaction](/reactions/etard-reaction/)); KMnO₄ takes any benzylic side chain to benzoic acid.
- **Ketones.** Peracids insert an oxygen next to the C=O ([Baeyer–Villiger oxidation](/reactions/baeyer-villiger-oxidation/)).
- **Phenols.** Persulphate introduces a second OH ([Elbs persulphate oxidation](/reactions/elbs-persulphate-oxidation/)).
- **Aldehydes.** Mild oxidants such as Tollens' and Fehling's reagents oxidise aldehydes but not ketones, the basis of [Tollens' test](/tests/tollens-test/) and [Fehling's test](/tests/fehling-test/). Methyl ketones are cleaved by halogen in alkali ([haloform reaction](/reactions/haloform-reaction/)).

## Air oxidation of chloroform to phosgene

Chloroform is slowly oxidised by **air in the presence of light** to **phosgene** (carbonyl chloride, COCl₂), an extremely poisonous gas, with HCl as the other product.

<Eq ce="2CHCl3 + O2 ->[light] 2COCl2 + 2HCl" />

Here the carbon climbs from +2 in CHCl₃ to +4 in COCl₂. To prevent this, chloroform is stored in **closed, dark-coloured bottles filled to the top** so that light and air are excluded, and commercial chloroform contains a little **ethanol**, which converts any phosgene into harmless diethyl carbonate.

<Eq ce="COCl2 + 2C2H5OH -> (C2H5O)2CO + 2HCl" />
