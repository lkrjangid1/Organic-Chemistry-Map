## What is electrophilic aromatic substitution?

In **electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS, sometimes written ESR)** an **electrophile, E⁺, replaces a hydrogen atom** on an aromatic ring. Benzene's π electrons are held tightly in an aromatic sextet, so it prefers substitution, which keeps the ring aromatic, over the addition reactions typical of alkenes. Every EAS reaction follows the same pattern; the only thing that changes is how the electrophile is made.

<Eq ce="C6H6 + E+ -> C6H5E + H+" />

## General mechanism

1. **Generation of the electrophile**, usually with a strong acid or a Lewis acid.
2. **Attack by the ring.** The π electrons attack E⁺, giving a resonance-stabilised carbocation called the **arenium ion**, **σ-complex** or **Wheland intermediate**. Aromaticity is lost here, so this is the slow, rate-determining step.
3. **Loss of H⁺.** A weak base removes the proton from the sp³ carbon and aromaticity is restored.

## How each electrophile is made

**(a) Nitration.** A mixture of concentrated nitric and sulphuric acids is used. Sulphuric acid protonates nitric acid, which loses water to give the **nitronium ion, NO₂⁺**.

<Eq ce="HNO3 + H2SO4 -> NO2+ + HSO4- + H2O" />

**(b) Sulphonation.** The electrophile is **SO₃** (or HSO₃⁺, depending on conditions), giving benzenesulphonic acid. Most exam questions write conc. H₂SO₄; textbooks and lab practice use **fuming sulphuric acid (oleum, H₂SO₄ + SO₃)**. Sulphonation is **reversible**: strong acid favours it, while hot dilute aqueous acid removes the –SO₃H group.

<Eq ce="C6H6 + SO3 ->[conc. H2SO4] C6H5SO3H" />

**(c) Halogenation.** Chlorine or bromine alone does not attack benzene. A Lewis acid such as FeCl₃, FeBr₃ or AlCl₃ polarises the X–X bond so that one halogen behaves like **X⁺**. Exam answers simply write X⁺ as the electrophile; SbCl₅ may also be given as the Lewis acid. Iodine needs an oxidant (H₂O₂ or a copper(II) salt) to become electrophilic.

<Eq ce="Cl2 + FeCl3 -> Cl+ + FeCl4-" />

**(d) Friedel–Crafts alkylation.** The electrophile is a **carbocation, R⁺**, from an alkyl halide with AlCl₃ or from an alkene with acid. See [Friedel–Crafts alkylation](/reactions/friedel-crafts-alkylation/).

**(e) Friedel–Crafts acylation.** The electrophile is the resonance-stabilised **acylium ion, R–C≡O⁺**, from an acyl chloride with AlCl₃. See [Friedel–Crafts acylation](/reactions/friedel-crafts-acylation/).

| Reaction | Reagents | Electrophile | Product from benzene |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitration | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | NO₂⁺ | nitrobenzene |
| Sulphonation | oleum (or conc. H₂SO₄, heat) | SO₃ / HSO₃⁺ | benzenesulphonic acid |
| Halogenation | X₂ + FeX₃ or AlCl₃ | X⁺ (polarised X–X) | chlorobenzene, bromobenzene |
| FC alkylation | R–X + anhyd. AlCl₃ | R⁺ | alkylbenzene |
| FC acylation | RCOCl + anhyd. AlCl₃ | RCO⁺ | aryl ketone |

## Directing effects of substituents

A group already on the ring decides both **how fast** the next substitution happens and **where** it goes. The effects can be summarised as follows:

| Effect of the group | Directs to |
|---|---|
| +M (lone pair donated by resonance: –OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHCOCH₃) | ortho, para |
| –M (–NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –SO₃H) | meta |
| +H (hyperconjugation: alkyl groups) | ortho, para |
| –H (reverse hyperconjugation, e.g. –CCl₃, –CF₃) | meta |
| +I (alkyl groups) | ortho, para |
| –I (when it is the only effect, e.g. –N⁺(CH₃)₃, –CF₃) | meta |

<KeyPoint title="The halogen exception">
Halogens are **–I and +M** at the same time. The –I effect is stronger, so halogens **deactivate** the ring, but their lone pairs still stabilise the ortho and para intermediates, so they are **ortho/para directors**. Nitration of chlorobenzene gives about 35% ortho, 64% para and only 1% meta product. The simple rule "–I directs meta" applies only to groups that have no lone pair to donate.
</KeyPoint>

Useful general rules from the textbook data:

- **All activating groups are ortho/para directors.** There are no meta-directing activators.
- **All deactivating groups except the halogens are meta directors.**
- Strength matters: an –OH group makes the ring roughly a thousand times more reactive than benzene towards nitration, while an –NO₂ group makes it more than ten million times less reactive.
- Nitration of toluene gives 63% ortho, 34% para and 3% meta; nitration of nitrobenzene gives 91% meta.

<ExamTip>
To predict the major product, first decide whether the group has a lone pair on the atom attached to the ring (ortho/para) or a δ+ atom attached to the ring (meta). With two groups on the ring, the stronger activator controls the position; steric hindrance usually makes para the major ortho/para product for bulky groups.
</ExamTip>

## Why the position changes

For ortho and para attack, one resonance form of the arenium ion puts the positive charge on the carbon carrying the substituent. A lone-pair donor (–OH, –NH₂, halogen) can then share that charge, which stabilises those intermediates. An electron-withdrawing group with a δ+ atom next to the ring makes those same resonance forms especially unfavourable, so meta attack, which never places the charge on that carbon, becomes the least bad option.

## Ipso substitution: when a group other than H leaves

Usually the proton is the group lost from the arenium ion. If the electrophile instead attacks a ring carbon that already carries a substituent (the **ipso** position), that substituent can leave in place of H⁺, provided it departs easily without its bonding pair. Groups that behave this way include **–SiR₃** (as R₃Si⁺), **–SO₃H** (as SO₃ + H⁺), **–COOH** (as CO₂ + H⁺) and **tertiary alkyl groups** such as tert-butyl (as a stable carbocation).

The exam case is **bromine water on a phenol or aniline** that carries one of these groups at an ortho or para position. The –OH or –NH₂ group activates all three ortho/para positions so strongly that bromine goes into all of them, displacing the group that was there:

<Eq ce="HOC6H4COOH + 3 Br2 ->[H2O] Br3C6H2OH v + CO2 + 3 HBr" />

| Starting compound | Group replaced | Product with Br₂/H₂O |
|---|---|---|
| Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) | –COOH | 2,4,6-tribromophenol |
| 4-Hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid | –SO₃H | 2,4,6-tribromophenol |
| Sulphanilic acid (4-aminobenzenesulphonic acid) | –SO₃H | 2,4,6-tribromoaniline |
| 4-tert-Butylphenol, 2-(trimethylsilyl)phenol | –C(CH₃)₃, –Si(CH₃)₃ | 2,4,6-tribromophenol |

<KeyPoint>
Protodesulphonation (the reverse of sulphonation in hot dilute acid) is ipso substitution with H⁺ as the electrophile. The same idea lets –SO₃H act as a removable blocking group.
</KeyPoint>

## Limits of Friedel–Crafts on substituted rings

Both Friedel–Crafts reactions fail on rings carrying strong meta-directing deactivators such as –NO₂, and on anilines, because the basic nitrogen binds AlCl₃. Nitration, sulphonation and halogenation still work on such rings, only more slowly.
