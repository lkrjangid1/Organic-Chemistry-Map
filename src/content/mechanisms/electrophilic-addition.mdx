## What is electrophilic addition?

In an **electrophilic addition**, the π bond of an alkene or alkyne is broken and two new σ bonds are formed. The electron-rich π bond attacks an **electrophile (E⁺)**, giving a positively charged intermediate (a carbocation or a bridged ion); a **nucleophile (Nu⁻)** then bonds to that intermediate. The overall result is E and Nu on adjacent carbons:

<Eq ce="R2C=CR2 + E-Nu -> R2C(E)-C(Nu)R2" />

## General mechanism (HX as the model)

1. **Protonation.** H⁺ adds to one alkene carbon, leaving a carbocation on the other. In exam answers this step is the **rate-determining step**.
2. **Nucleophile attack.** X⁻ bonds to the carbocation quickly.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH2 + HX -> CH3-CH2+ + X- -> CH3-CH2X" />

**Markovnikov orientation.** For an unsymmetrical alkene, H⁺ adds so that the **more stable (more substituted) carbocation** forms, and the nucleophile ends up on the more substituted carbon. This is [Markovnikov's rule](/reactions/markovnikov-rule/). HBr with peroxide reverses it through a radical chain: see the [peroxide effect](/reactions/peroxide-effect/).

## E–Nu reagents with propene

Every reagent below is split into an electrophilic part and a nucleophilic part. The electrophilic part goes to the CH₂ end, the nucleophilic part to C-2.

| Reagent | E⁺ / Nu⁻ | Product from CH₃–CH=CH₂ |
|---|---|---|
| HCl | H⁺ / Cl⁻ | 2-Chloropropane |
| H₂O, H⁺ | H⁺ / OH | Propan-2-ol |
| CH₃OH, H⁺ | H⁺ / OCH₃ | 2-Methoxypropane |
| CH₃COOH, H⁺ | H⁺ / OCOCH₃ | Isopropyl acetate |
| Cl₂ / CCl₄ | Cl⁺ / Cl⁻ | 1,2-Dichloropropane |
| X₂ / H₂O (HOX) | X⁺ / OH⁻ | 1-Halopropan-2-ol (halohydrin) |
| ICl | I⁺ / Cl⁻ | 2-Chloro-1-iodopropane |
| IN₃ | I⁺ / N₃⁻ | 2-Azido-1-iodopropane |
| NOCl | NO⁺ / Cl⁻ | 2-Chloro-1-nitrosopropane |

<KeyPoint>
Halogens and HOX add through a bridged **halonium ion**, so they add **anti** (trans). In a halohydrin the OH goes to the more substituted carbon and X to the less substituted one.
</KeyPoint>

## Carbocation rearrangements

Because a free carbocation forms, it can rearrange to a more stable one before the nucleophile arrives. A classic example is isopropenylcyclobutane + HBr: protonation gives a 3° cation next to the four-membered ring; a ring bond migrates, expanding the ring to a cyclopentyl cation (relieving ring strain); a methyl shift then gives a 3° cation, and Br⁻ attack gives **1-bromo-1,2-dimethylcyclopentane**. The energy diagram has one peak for each step (TS₁ to TS₄) and a valley for each cation.

Methods that avoid a free carbocation give no rearrangement: [oxymercuration–demercuration](/reactions/oxymercuration-demercuration/) (Markovnikov) and [hydroboration–oxidation](/reactions/hydroboration-oxidation/) (anti-Markovnikov, syn).

## Kinetic vs thermodynamic control: HBr + buta-1,3-diene

Protonation at C-1 gives an allylic cation with positive charge shared by C-2 and C-4.

- **1,2-addition → 3-bromobut-1-ene**: the **kinetically controlled product (KCP)**. It forms first, has the lower activation energy, is less stable, and dominates at **low temperature**.
- **1,4-addition → 1-bromobut-2-ene**: the **thermodynamically controlled product (TCP)**. It is more stable (more substituted C=C), has the higher activation energy, is more exothermic, and dominates at **higher temperature**.

<Trick>
KCP: "Rxn में सबसे पहले बनने वाला", "Low temp per banta h" (formed first; formed at low temperature). TCP: "ज्यादा स्थिर", "High temp per banta h" (more stable; formed at high temperature).
</Trick>

## Syn and anti dihydroxylation

| Product | Reagents | Stereochemistry |
|---|---|---|
| Syn diol | Baeyer's reagent (cold, dilute, alkaline KMnO₄, 1–2%) or OsO₄ then NaHSO₃ | Both OH on the same face |
| Anti diol | Peroxy acid (e.g. mCPBA) to the epoxide, then H₃O⁺ | OH groups on opposite faces |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing H₂O₂/NaOH for anti-hydroxylation. The anti route is **epoxidation with a peroxy acid followed by acid-catalysed hydrolysis**: water opens the epoxide from the side opposite the oxygen. Use that sequence in answers. See [Prilezhaev epoxidation](/reactions/prilezhaev-epoxidation/).
</ExamTip>

## Other additions across C=C: a quick map

Several common alkene reactions are not simple E⁺/Nu⁻ additions, but they are learnt alongside them. What matters in exam answers is the orientation and the stereochemistry:

| Reagent | Product | Orientation / stereochemistry |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ with Ni, Pt or Pd | Alkane | Syn addition on the metal surface ([catalytic hydrogenation](/reactions/catalytic-hydrogenation/)) |
| HN=NH (diimide, from N₂H₄ + H₂O₂) | Alkane | Syn delivery of both H from one face ([diimide reduction](/reactions/diimide-reduction/)) |
| Hg(OAc)₂, H₂O; then NaBH₄ | Alcohol | Markovnikov; anti oxymercuration; no carbocation, so no rearrangement |
| BH₃·THF (B₂H₆); then H₂O₂/NaOH | Alcohol | Anti-Markovnikov; syn addition of H and OH |
| Peroxy acid (mCPBA, MMPP) | Epoxide | Syn, stereospecific (cis alkene gives cis epoxide) |
| O₂ over a silver catalyst | Ethylene oxide | Industrial route, used for ethene |
| Singlet :CH₂ (e.g. from CH₂N₂ + light) | Cyclopropane | Concerted, stereospecific: cis-but-2-ene gives cis-1,2-dimethylcyclopropane |
| Triplet :CH₂ | Cyclopropane | Stepwise diradical addition; stereochemistry is scrambled |
| O₃; then Zn/H₂O or (CH₃)₂S | Aldehydes and/or ketones | C=C cleaved ([ozonolysis](/reactions/ozonolysis/), reductive work-up) |
| O₃; then H₂O₂ | Carboxylic acids (and ketones) | Oxidative work-up |

For carbene chemistry in more depth, see the [carbenes hub](/mechanisms/carbenes/) and the [Simmons–Smith reaction](/reactions/simmons-smith-reaction/).

## Worked example: cyclohexene

Cyclohexene is the standard ring alkene in road-map questions, because the ring makes anti and syn addition easy to see.

- **HBr** gives bromocyclohexane (both carbons are equivalent, so orientation does not arise).
- **Br₂ in CCl₄** gives **trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane**. Attack of Br⁻ on the bromonium ion from the back face makes the addition anti; the product is formed as a racemic pair of enantiomers.
- **Br₂ in water** gives **trans-2-bromocyclohexan-1-ol** (a bromohydrin), again by anti addition.
- **Base (NaOH or KOH) on the bromohydrin** closes the ring by an internal SN2 reaction: the alkoxide displaces bromide from the back side, giving **cyclohexene oxide** (7-oxabicyclo[4.1.0]heptane).
- **Cold, dilute alkaline KMnO₄ or OsO₄** gives cis-cyclohexane-1,2-diol; **peroxy acid then H₃O⁺** gives the trans-diol.

<Eq ce="C6H10 ->[Br2, H2O] C6H10(OH)Br ->[NaOH] C6H10O + NaBr + H2O" />

## Additions to alkynes

Alkynes add the same reagents, usually in two stages. Water needs Hg²⁺ catalysis and gives a ketone through an enol: the [Kucherov reaction](/reactions/kucherov-reaction/). Hydrogen can be stopped at the alkene stage with the [Lindlar catalyst](/reagents/lindlar-catalyst/) (palladium on CaCO₃, poisoned with lead salts or quinoline), which adds H₂ syn and gives the **cis-alkene**.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing nickel for the Lindlar catalyst. Lindlar's catalyst is poisoned **palladium**; Pd on BaSO₄ poisoned with sulphur compounds is the Rosenmund catalyst used for acid chlorides.
</ExamTip>

**Positional isomerisation (often studied alongside E2).** NaNH₂ on heating moves an internal triple bond to the chain end (but-2-yne → but-1-yne), because the terminal alkyne is trapped as its sodium salt. Alcoholic KOH on heating does the reverse, since the terminal alkyne salt is not held and the more stable internal alkyne forms via an allene.

## Useful points

- **gem-Diols, gem-amino alcohols and gem-halohydrins** usually collapse to a C=O group (losing H₂O, NH₃ or HX). That is why enols and hydrates appear only as intermediates.
- **vic-Diiodides** easily lose I₂ to re-form the alkene, which is why iodine adds poorly to alkenes.
