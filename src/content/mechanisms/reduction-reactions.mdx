## What is a reduction reaction?

In organic chemistry, **reduction** means **adding hydrogen to a molecule or removing oxygen (or another electronegative atom) from it**. In electron terms, the carbon atom gains electrons and its oxidation state falls. An aldehyde becoming a primary alcohol, a ketone becoming an alkane, an alkyne becoming an alkene and a nitro compound becoming an amine are all reductions. The exam skill is choosing the reagent that reduces the group you want and nothing else.

## Reagent selection table

What each common reagent does to each functional group (— means no reaction under normal conditions):

| Group | LiAlH₄ | NaBH₄ | H₂ / Ni, Pd, Pt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde, RCHO | 1° alcohol | 1° alcohol | 1° alcohol |
| Ketone, R₂CO | 2° alcohol | 2° alcohol | 2° alcohol |
| Acyl chloride, RCOCl | 1° alcohol | 1° alcohol | aldehyde (only with Pd/BaSO₄) |
| Ester, RCOOR′ | 1° alcohol | slow and inefficient | — |
| Carboxylic acid, RCOOH | 1° alcohol | — | — |
| Amide, RCONH₂ | amine RCH₂NH₂ | — | — |
| Nitrile, RC≡N | amine RCH₂NH₂ | — | amine RCH₂NH₂ |
| Nitro, RNO₂ | amine | — | amine RNH₂ |
| Alkyl halide, RX | alkane RH | — | — |
| Isolated C=C | — | — | alkane |

DIBAL-H is the reagent for partial reduction: it takes esters and nitriles only as far as the aldehyde (see below).

<KeyPoint title="Strength order of hydride reagents">
LiAlH₄ is much stronger than NaBH₄ because the Al–H bond is weaker than the B–H bond. LiAlH₄ is used in dry ether; NaBH₄ is used in methanol or ethanol, which it needs for activation. Neither reduces an isolated C=C bond. With conjugated (α,β-unsaturated) ketones NaBH₄ can also add 1,4; adding CeCl₃ (Luche reduction) makes it reduce only the C=O.
</KeyPoint>

## Turning C=O into CH₂

| Method | Reagents | Medium | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Clemmensen](/reactions/clemmensen-reduction/) | Zn-Hg, conc. HCl | strongly acidic | acid-stable substrates, aryl ketones |
| [Wolff–Kishner](/reactions/wolff-kishner-reduction/) | NH₂NH₂, KOH, ethylene glycol, about 200 °C | strongly basic | base-stable substrates |
| [Mozingo](/reactions/mozingo-reduction/) | HSCH₂CH₂SH / H⁺, then Raney Ni | nearly neutral | substrates sensitive to both |
| [HI / red P](/reagents/hi-and-red-phosphorus/) | conc. HI, red P, prolonged heat | strongly acidic | complete deoxygenation (glucose to n-hexane) |

NCERT names Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner as the two standard ways of reducing aldehydes and ketones to hydrocarbons. Hydride reagents cannot do this job: they stop at the alcohol.

## Stopping at the aldehyde

Partial reduction of acid derivatives needs a reagent that cannot attack the aldehyde formed:

<Eq ce="R-COCl ->[H2, Pd/BaSO4] R-CHO" />
<Eq ce="R-C#N ->[1. SnCl2, HCl][2. H3O+] R-CHO" />
<Eq ce="R-COOR' ->[1. DIBAL-H, low temp.][2. H2O] R-CHO" />

These are the [Rosenmund reduction](/reactions/rosenmund-reduction/) (poisoned catalyst), the [Stephen reduction](/reactions/stephen-reduction/) (the imine salt precipitates) and [DIBAL-H](/reagents/dibal-h/) reduction (a stable intermediate forms at low temperature and is hydrolysed only at workup).

## Dissolving-metal reductions

Alkali metals give electrons one at a time, so these reactions go through **radical anions**:

- **Birch reduction:** Na or Li in liquid NH₃ with an alcohol reduces benzene to cyclohexa-1,4-diene, not to cyclohexane.
- **Bouveault–Blanc reduction:** Na in absolute ethanol reduces esters to primary alcohols.
- **Mendius reduction:** Na in ethanol reduces nitriles to primary amines.
- **Metal and acid:** Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl reduce nitro compounds to amines; iron scrap and HCl is preferred industrially because the FeCl₂ formed is hydrolysed and releases HCl again. See [Reduction of nitrobenzene](/reactions/reduction-of-nitrobenzene/) and [Zinin reduction](/reactions/zinin-reduction/) for selective reduction of one nitro group.

## Triphenyltin hydride (TTH)

**Triphenyltin hydride, Ph₃SnH**, is a hydrogen-atom (H•) donor rather than a hydride (H⁻) donor. It transfers hydrogen through a **radical chain**: a tin radical removes the halogen from an alkyl halide, and the alkyl radical then takes H• from another Ph₃SnH, regenerating the tin radical. Organotin hydrides such as tributyltin hydride convert organic halides into the corresponding hydrocarbons, with AIBN or light as initiator.

## Choosing the reagent: exam tips

<ExamTip>
Ask two questions: (1) Which group must change, and how far (alcohol, aldehyde, CH₂, amine)? (2) Which other groups must survive? Then pick the mildest reagent that does the job: NaBH₄ for an aldehyde next to an ester; Lindlar for alkyne to cis-alkene; Pd/BaSO₄ for acyl chloride to aldehyde; Wolff–Kishner when acid would destroy something.
</ExamTip>

- Only LiAlH₄ (among common hydrides) reduces **carboxylic acids** and **amides**.
- **MPV reduction** (aluminium isopropoxide in isopropanol) reduces aldehydes and ketones to alcohols and leaves C=C and C≡C untouched; its reverse is the Oppenauer oxidation.
- Amines can also be made by [reductive amination](/reactions/reductive-amination/) of aldehydes and ketones.
