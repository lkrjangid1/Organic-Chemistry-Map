## What is nucleophilic addition?

The carbonyl group is polar: oxygen pulls the π electrons towards itself, so the carbonyl carbon is **electron-poor (δ+)**. In **nucleophilic addition**, a nucleophile attacks this carbon, the carbon changes from **sp² (trigonal) to sp³ (tetrahedral)**, and the π electrons move onto oxygen as an **alkoxide**. Protonation of the oxygen then gives a neutral product in which **Nu and H have added across C=O**. This is the characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones, just as electrophilic addition is the characteristic reaction of alkenes.

<Eq ce="R2C=O + Nu- -> R2C(O-)Nu ->[H+] R2C(OH)Nu" />

## Mechanism: base-catalysed and acid-catalysed

Weak nucleophiles such as water, alcohols and HCN add only slowly to a neutral carbonyl group. A catalyst speeds things up in one of two ways.

| | Base-catalysed (strong Nu⁻) | Acid-catalysed (weak, neutral Nu) |
|---|---|---|
| Step 1 | Nu⁻ (OH⁻, CN⁻, H⁻, R⁻) attacks the carbonyl carbon | H⁺ protonates the carbonyl oxygen |
| Step 2 | Alkoxide intermediate forms | Carbon becomes much more electrophilic (C=OH⁺) |
| Step 3 | Alkoxide picks up H⁺ from water or HNu | Neutral Nu (H₂O, ROH, NH₂Z) attacks, then loses H⁺ |
| How it helps | makes a better nucleophile | makes a better electrophile |
| Examples | HCN/CN⁻, RMgX, NaBH₄ | acetals, imines and oximes |

The nucleophile approaches the C=O carbon from above or below the plane of the carbonyl group. A planar aldehyde can be attacked from either face, so a new stereocentre forms as a racemic mixture unless something chiral is present.

After the tetrahedral intermediate forms, two outcomes are possible: protonation gives an **alcohol-type adduct** (cyanohydrins, hydrates, alcohols), or the OH is protonated and lost as water to give a **C=Nu double bond** (imines, oximes, hydrazones). The second is called **addition–elimination**.

## Reactivity order

Aldehydes react faster than ketones for steric reasons (one bulky group instead of two) and electronic reasons (one electron-releasing alkyl group instead of two). Aromatic carbonyl compounds are slower because the ring donates electrons by resonance.

**HCHO > CH₃CHO > RCHO > ArCHO > CH₃COCH₃ > bulkier ketones > C₆H₅COCH₃ > C₆H₅COC₆H₅**

Full explanation and solved ordering problems: [Reactivity of aldehydes and ketones](/comparisons/reactivity-of-carbonyl-compounds/).

## Key nucleophilic additions

| Reagent | Product from R₂C=O | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HCN (trace base) | cyanohydrin R₂C(OH)CN | CN⁻ is the real nucleophile; hydrolysis gives α-hydroxy acids |
| NaHSO₃ | bisulphite adduct R₂C(OH)SO₃Na | crystalline; acid or alkali regenerates the carbonyl; used to purify aldehydes |
| H₂O | gem-diol R₂C(OH)₂ | favoured only for HCHO and C=O carrying strong –I groups (chloral) |
| ROH, dry HCl | hemiacetal, then acetal R₂C(OR)₂ | ketones + ethylene glycol give cyclic ketals; aqueous acid reverses |
| RMgX, then H₃O⁺ | alcohol | HCHO → 1°, RCHO → 2°, ketone → 3° |
| NaBH₄ / LiAlH₄ | alcohol | aldehyde → 1°, ketone → 2° |
| NH₂–Z, pH 4–5 | R₂C=N–Z | addition–elimination; see table below |

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + HCN ->[OH-] CH3CH(OH)CN ->[H3O+] CH3CH(OH)COOH" />

The second step turns acetaldehyde cyanohydrin into **lactic acid** (2-hydroxypropanoic acid), a favourite conversion question.

## Ammonia derivatives (H₂N–Z)

The amine nitrogen adds to C=O to give a **carbinolamine**, which is protonated on oxygen and loses water. The rate is highest in weakly acidic solution (about pH 4.5): at high pH the OH is not protonated, and at low pH the amine itself is protonated and stops being a nucleophile.

| H₂N–Z | Reagent | Product |
|---|---|---|
| H₂N–R | primary amine | substituted imine (Schiff's base) |
| H₂N–OH | hydroxylamine | oxime |
| H₂N–NH₂ | hydrazine | hydrazone |
| H₂N–NHC₆H₅ | phenylhydrazine | phenylhydrazone |
| H₂N–NH–C₆H₃(NO₂)₂ | 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine | 2,4-DNP derivative (yellow, orange or red solid) |
| H₂N–NHCONH₂ | semicarbazide | semicarbazone |

The 2,4-DNP derivative is the basis of the [2,4-DNP test](/tests/2-4-dnp-test/) for any aldehyde or ketone.

## Hydrolysis: running the additions backwards

**Imines** are hydrolysed by water (acid helps) back to the carbonyl compound and the amine or ammonia. Water adds to the C=N carbon, proton transfer within the adduct puts the positive charge on nitrogen, and the C=O reforms as NH₃ leaves.

<Eq ce="R2C=NH + H2O ->[H3O+] R2C=O + NH3" />

**Nitriles** add water across C≡N to give a hydroxy imine, R–C(OH)=NH, which tautomerises to an **amide**. Further hydrolysis gives the **carboxylic acid and ammonia** (or a carboxylate in base).

<Eq ce="RC#N ->[H2O] RCONH2 ->[H2O, H+] RCOOH + NH4+" />

**Isocyanides** are stable to base but are hydrolysed by aqueous acid, first to an N-alkylformamide and then to a **primary amine and formic acid**. This is how foul-smelling isocyanide residues are destroyed.

<Eq ce="RN#C + 2 H2O ->[H3O+] RNH2 + HCOOH" />

<ExamTip>
Nitrile hydrolysis gives an **acid** (carbon stays attached to R); isocyanide hydrolysis gives an **amine** plus formic acid (the carbon leaves as HCOOH). Use this to tell R–C≡N and R–N≡C apart in structure problems.
</ExamTip>

## Two special cases

**Formaldehyde + ammonia → urotropine.** Instead of a simple imine, six molecules of formaldehyde and four of ammonia condense into the cage compound **hexamethylenetetramine** (hexamine, urotropine). It is used to harden phenol–formaldehyde resins and as a urinary antiseptic (methenamine).

<Eq ce="6 HCHO + 4 NH3 -> (CH2)6N4 + 6 H2O" />

**Chloral + NaOH → chloroform + formate.** Hydroxide adds to the very electrophilic carbonyl carbon of chloral. When C=O reforms, the trichloromethyl anion is expelled, because three chlorine atoms stabilise its negative charge; it then takes a proton to become chloroform. The same strong –I effect is why chloral exists as the stable solid **chloral hydrate**.

<Eq ce="Cl3C-CHO + NaOH -> CHCl3 + HCOONa" />
