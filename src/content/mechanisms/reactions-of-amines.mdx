faq:
  - q: "Why is the –NH₂ group of aniline acetylated before bromination or nitration?"
    a: "The –NH₂ group activates the ring so strongly that bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromoaniline at once, and nitric acid oxidises aniline and forms the meta-directing anilinium ion. Acetylation to –NHCOCH₃ reduces the activation, so a single para substituent can be introduced; acid or base hydrolysis then removes the acetyl group."
  - q: "Why does aniline not undergo Friedel–Crafts reaction?"
    a: "Aniline is a Lewis base and forms a salt with the AlCl₃ catalyst. The nitrogen then carries a positive charge and strongly deactivates the ring, so neither alkylation nor acylation occurs."
  - q: "What is the product of oxidation of aniline with KMnO₄?"
    a: "It depends on the medium: neutral KMnO₄ gives nitrobenzene, alkaline KMnO₄ gives azobenzene (with ammonia and oxalic acid), and acidic KMnO₄ gives the dye aniline black. Chromic acid gives p-benzoquinone and Caro's acid gives nitrosobenzene."
  - q: "What happens when urea is heated?"
    a: "Above about 160 °C urea decomposes to ammonia and isocyanic acid (HN=C=O). The isocyanic acid adds to more urea to give biuret, and at around 175 °C three molecules combine into cyanuric acid with loss of ammonia."
  - q: "How do nitriles and isocyanides differ?"
    a: "They are isomers: R–C≡N is bonded through carbon, R–N≡C through nitrogen. Nitriles are pleasant-smelling and hydrolyse to carboxylic acids; isocyanides have a vile smell, hydrolyse in aqueous acid to formamides (R–NH–CHO) and rearrange to the more stable nitrile on heating."

## What are the reactions of amines?

Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia, and nearly all their chemistry comes from the **lone pair on nitrogen**. It makes amines **bases**, **nucleophiles** (towards alkyl halides, acyl chlorides and NO⁺) and, in aniline, strong **activators of the ring** towards electrophiles. This hub also covers nitrogen compounds taught alongside amines: nitro compounds, nitriles and isocyanides, and urea.

## Basicity

Alkyl groups (+I) make alkylamines stronger bases than ammonia; in aniline the lone pair is delocalised into the ring, so arylamines are much weaker. NCERT's order is **(C₂H₅)₂NH > C₂H₅NH₂ > NH₃ > C₆H₅NH₂**; the gas-phase order 3° > 2° > 1° changes in water because of solvation. See [basic strength of amines](/comparisons/basic-strength-of-amines/).

Acylation needs a base stronger than the amine (pyridine) to remove the HCl formed. For aryl diazonium chemistry, see [diazotization](/reactions/diazotization-reaction/) and the [diazonium salt hub](/mechanisms/diazonium-salt-reactions/).

In exam answers, an **α-amino acid** treated with NaNO₂/HCl gives the α-hydroxy acid with **retention** of configuration. This is explained by [neighbouring group participation](/reactions/neighbouring-group-participation/) of the carboxyl group (two inversions).

## Electrophilic substitution in aniline

–NH₂ is a powerful ortho/para director, which causes three problems:

- **Bromination:** bromine water at room temperature gives a white precipitate of **2,4,6-tribromoaniline** straight away.
- **Nitration:** direct nitration gives tarry oxidation products. In the strongly acidic medium aniline is also protonated to the meta-directing anilinium ion, so a large amount of **m-nitroaniline** forms alongside the o- and p-isomers.
- **Friedel–Crafts:** fails, because aniline forms a salt with AlCl₃.

**Protection by acetylation** solves this: in acetanilide the nitrogen lone pair is shared with C=O, so –NHCOCH₃ is a milder activator. Substitute, then hydrolyse the amide.

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 ->[(CH3CO)2O] C6H5NHCOCH3 ->[Br2, CH3COOH] p-BrC6H4NHCOCH3 ->[H3O+] p-BrC6H4NH2" />

To make **o-bromoaniline**, the para position is blocked first. Sulphonation is reversible: the –SO₃H group goes on in concentrated acid and comes off in hot dilute acid, so it can act as a temporary blocking group. Bromination then goes ortho, and hydrolysis removes both the acetyl and the sulphonic acid groups.

**Sulphonation:** aniline and conc. H₂SO₄ give anilinium hydrogensulphate, which on heating at 453–473 K gives **sulphanilic acid** (p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid). It exists as a zwitterion, H₃N⁺–C₆H₄–SO₃⁻, which explains its high melting point.

## Oxidation of aniline and amines

| Oxidising agent | Product from aniline |
|---|---|
| Peroxy acids (e.g. CF₃CO₃H) | Nitrobenzene |
| Caro's acid (H₂SO₅) | Nitrosobenzene |
| Chromic acid (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺) | p-Benzoquinone |
| KMnO₄, neutral | Nitrobenzene |
| KMnO₄, alkaline | Azobenzene (+ NH₃, oxalic acid) |
| KMnO₄, acidic | Aniline black |
| Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) or NaOCl | Intense violet-blue colour, a test for aniline |
| Conc. HNO₃ | Tarry oxidation products |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Swapping the KMnO₄ media. Acidic permanganate gives the black dye (aniline black); alkaline permanganate gives azobenzene.
</ExamTip>

**Aliphatic amines:** tertiary amines are oxidised by H₂O₂, peroxy acids (mCPBA) or Caro's acid to **amine oxides**, R₃N⁺–O⁻, which undergo the [Cope elimination](/reactions/cope-elimination/) on heating. Primary amines can be oxidised to nitro compounds. In exam answers, KMnO₄ converts a 1° amine on a tertiary carbon (R₃C–NH₂) into R₃C–NO₂, and gives aldehydes or ketones via imines from RCH₂NH₂ and R₂CHNH₂.

## Nitro compounds

- **Reduction:** acid → amine, neutral → hydroxylamine, alkaline → azoxy/azo/hydrazo compounds; see [reduction of nitrobenzene](/reactions/reduction-of-nitrobenzene/). One nitro group of a dinitro compound is reduced by the [Zinin reduction](/reactions/zinin-reduction/). The [Mulliken–Barker test](/tests/mulliken-barker-test/) uses Zn/NH₄Cl for this.
- **Further nitration:** nitrobenzene with mixed acid gives **m-dinitrobenzene** (about 93% meta). 1,3,5-Trinitrobenzene needs far harsher conditions, such as long heating with HNO₃ in fuming H₂SO₄.
- **Acid hydrolysis of nitroalkanes:** a nitroalkane with an α-H tautomerises to the aci form. Primary nitroalkanes in strong acid give a **carboxylic acid + hydroxylamine**. The salt of a secondary nitroalkane gives a **ketone + N₂O** in acid (Nef reaction). Tertiary nitroalkanes have no α-H and do not react.
- **Decomposition:** nitro compounds release a lot of energy on decomposition, which is why many are explosives. For nitromethane: 2CH₃NO₂ → 2CO + 2H₂O + H₂ + N₂ (monopropellant).

## Nitriles and isocyanides

| Property | Nitrile R–C≡N | Isocyanide R–N≡C |
|---|---|---|
| Smell | Faint, ethereal | Vile |
| Water solubility | Higher (CH₃CN is miscible; N lone pair H-bonds with water) | Lower |
| Acid hydrolysis | Carboxylic acid | Formamide R–NH–CHO |
| Stability | More stable | Rearranges to the nitrile on heating |

Isocyanides add Cl₂ to give isocyanide dichlorides, R–N=CCl₂. In exam answers, oxidation (HgO) gives isocyanates R–N=C=O and sulphur gives isothiocyanates R–N=C=S.

## Urea and related compounds

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Carbonic acid | HO–CO–OH |
| Carbamic acid | H₂N–CO–OH |
| Urea (carbamide) | H₂N–CO–NH₂ |
| Cyanic / isocyanic acid | H–O–C≡N / H–N=C=O (isocyanic acid is the main tautomer) |
| Cyanogen | N≡C–C≡N |
| Carbolic acid / carbinol | Phenol / methanol |

- **Hydrolysis:** CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂.
- **Heating:** above about 160 °C urea gives NH₃ + HNCO. HNCO plus urea gives biuret, and near 175 °C cyanuric acid, (HNCO)₃, forms.
- **With NaOCl:** (NH₂)₂CO + NaOCl + 2NaOH → N₂H₄ + NaCl + Na₂CO₃ + H₂O. Ammonia is oxidised in the same way (Raschig process), and hydrazine with H₂O₂ gives diimide, HN=NH. In exam answers, urea with HNO₂ gives N₂ + CO₂ + H₂O.
- **From calcium carbide:** CaC₂ + N₂ → CaCN₂ + C at high temperature. This mixture is the fertiliser **nitrolim**. Calcium cyanamide is hydrolysed to cyanamide, H₂N–C≡N, which adds water to give urea. Carbides also differ on hydrolysis: CaC₂ gives acetylene, Al₄C₃ gives methane, and Mg₂C₃ gives propyne (with some allene).

## Other exam facts

- **Carbaryl (Sevin)**, a carbamate insecticide, is made from 1-naphthol and methyl isocyanate (CH₃–N=C=O), the gas released at Bhopal.
- **Anthranilic acid** is made from naphthalene → phthalic anhydride (V₂O₅, air) → phthalimide (NH₃) → Hofmann degradation with NaOBr/NaOH. See [Hofmann bromamide degradation](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/).
- **Methylene blue** forms from N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine with H₂S and Fe³⁺, which is also a test for sulphide.
- **Sodium in liquid NH₃** gives a blue, conducting solution of ammoniated electrons, which turns bronze when concentrated. With an iron catalyst it forms **sodamide**: 2Na + 2NH₃ → 2NaNH₂ + H₂. NaNH₂ is a white solid and a strong base.
