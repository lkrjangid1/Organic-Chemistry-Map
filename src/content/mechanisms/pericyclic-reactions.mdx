## What is a pericyclic reaction?

A **pericyclic reaction** is a reaction in which all bond-making and bond-breaking happen **together, in one step**, through a **cyclic transition state**. The orbitals that take part overlap in a continuous ring at the transition state. There is no carbocation, carbanion or radical intermediate, which is why these reactions were once jokingly called "no-mechanism" reactions. Their outcome, including stereochemistry, is controlled by **orbital symmetry**, summarised in the **Woodward–Hoffmann rules**.

Pericyclic reactions are usually equilibria, so every type has a "retro" version that runs backwards when the reverse direction is favoured, for example the retro-Diels–Alder reaction at high temperature.

## Main classes compared

| Class | What happens | σ bonds | π bonds | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cycloaddition | Two π systems join into a ring | +2 | −2 | Diels–Alder ([4+2]) |
| Electrocyclic | A conjugated polyene closes into a ring (or opens) | +1 | −1 | Hexatriene → cyclohexadiene |
| Sigmatropic | A σ bond moves along a π system | 0 | 0 | Claisen and Cope [3,3] shifts |
| Ene | An alkene with an allylic H adds to a second π bond | +1 | −1 | Ene reaction of an alkene with an enophile |
| Cheletropic | Both new bonds form to one atom | +2 | −1 | SO₂ adding to a diene |

<KeyPoint>
Cycloaddition, electrocyclic and sigmatropic are the three classes that matter most. Exam questions on pericyclic reactions at JEE level are dominated by the **Diels–Alder reaction**.
</KeyPoint>

## Thermal or photochemical? The selection rules

Pericyclic reactions run either on heating (ground state, Δ) or under UV light (excited state, hν), and the electron count decides which is allowed.

**Cycloadditions (both components suprafacial):**

| Total π electrons | Thermal (Δ) | Photochemical (hν) |
|---|---|---|
| 4n + 2 (e.g. [4+2], 6 e⁻) | Allowed | Forbidden |
| 4n (e.g. [2+2], 4 e⁻) | Forbidden | Allowed |

So the Diels–Alder reaction is a heat reaction, and making cyclobutanes from two alkenes needs light.

**Electrocyclic reactions:**

| π electrons | Thermal | Photochemical |
|---|---|---|
| 4n (e.g. butadiene, 4 e⁻) | Conrotatory | Disrotatory |
| 4n + 2 (e.g. hexatriene, 6 e⁻) | Disrotatory | Conrotatory |

In a **conrotatory** process both terminal groups rotate the same way; in a **disrotatory** process they rotate in opposite directions. This decides whether the substituents end up cis or trans in the ring.

<ExamTip>
Remember one row and flip the rest: **4n + 2 electrons + heat = allowed suprafacial (Diels–Alder) and disrotatory**. Changing either the electron count to 4n or the conditions to light reverses the answer; changing both brings it back.
</ExamTip>

## The Diels–Alder reaction (member page)

The [Diels–Alder reaction](/reactions/diels-alder-reaction/) is a [4+2] cycloaddition of a conjugated diene with a dienophile to give a cyclohexene. It needs an **s-cis** diene, is **thermally allowed**, keeps the geometry of the dienophile, and with cyclic dienes favours the **endo** product under kinetic control. Electron-withdrawing groups on the dienophile (CHO, COOR, CN, anhydride) speed it up. Furan adducts revert easily (retro-Diels–Alder) because furan regains aromaticity.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing **hν** over the arrow for buta-1,3-diene + ethene → cyclohexene. The [4+2] cycloaddition is a **thermal** reaction, so write **Δ** in answers.
</ExamTip>

## Factors that control pericyclic reactions

- **Electron count** (4n or 4n + 2) and **conditions** (Δ or hν): decide whether the reaction is allowed.
- **Geometry**: the diene must reach the s-cis conformation; ring strain and bulky groups can block the cyclic transition state.
- **Electronic match**: in normal-demand Diels–Alder reactions, an electron-rich diene pairs with an electron-poor dienophile (HOMO of diene with LUMO of dienophile).
- **Temperature and time**: low temperature favours the faster (often endo) product; high temperature and long times allow equilibration to the more stable product or a retro reaction.
