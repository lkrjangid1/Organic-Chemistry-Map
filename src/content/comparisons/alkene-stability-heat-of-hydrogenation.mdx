## What decides the stability of an alkene?

The stability of an alkene is measured by its **heat of hydrogenation**, the heat released when it adds H₂ over a Pt or Pd catalyst to give the alkane. Isomeric alkenes that give the **same alkane** end at the same energy, so the **more stable alkene releases less heat**. Heat of combustion works the same way, but only for compounds with the same formula. Both are thermochemical yardsticks: they compare the energy stored in the starting compounds, not how fast they react.

<KeyPoint title="Two rules and their conditions">
**Heat of hydrogenation ∝ number of π bonds ∝ 1/stability**, the second part only when the number of π bonds (and the product) is the same.
**Heat of combustion ∝ number of carbon atoms ∝ 1/stability**, the second part only for isomers (same number of C and H).
</KeyPoint>

## Substitution and geometry

| Alkene | Substitution | ΔH hydrogenation (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH₂ | none | −136 |
| CH₃CH=CH₂ | mono | −125 |
| cis-CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | di | −119 |
| (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ | di | −118 |
| trans-CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | di | −115 |
| (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ | tri | −112 |
| (CH₃)₂C=C(CH₃)₂ | tetra | −110 |

**Order:** tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstituted > ethylene, and **trans > cis** (by about 2.8 kJ/mol for 2-butene). More alkyl groups stabilise the double bond by hyperconjugation and by the stronger sp²–sp³ C–C bonds; cis isomers lose stability to steric crowding. For example, cyclohexene < 1-ethylcyclohexene < 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene in stability, because the double bond becomes more substituted.

## Dienes: conjugated > isolated > cumulated

| Diene | Type | ΔH hydrogenation (kJ/mol) | Expected for two separate C=C |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂ | conjugated | −236 | about −252 |
| CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH=CH₂ | isolated | −253 | about −252 |
| CH₂=C=CH₂ (allene) | cumulated | −298 | about −252 |

A conjugated diene is about 16 kJ/mol more stable than expected, because its π electrons are delocalised over four carbons. An isolated diene behaves like two independent alkenes. In a cumulated diene the two π bonds share one sp carbon and are perpendicular, so there is no conjugation and the molecule is less stable than either.

## The exception: benzene and the cyclohexadienes

| Compound | π bonds | ΔH hydrogenation (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclohexene | 1 | −118 |
| Benzene | 3 | −206 |
| Cyclohexa-1,3-diene | 2 | −230 |
| Cyclohexa-1,4-diene | 2 | about −232 (1.6 kJ/mol less stable than the 1,3-isomer) |

Note that the comparison here is not between isomers: all four compounds give cyclohexane, but they add different amounts of hydrogen. That is exactly why the simple rule "heat of hydrogenation rises with the number of π bonds" is tested with this set.

So the heat of hydrogenation order is **cyclohexene < benzene < cyclohexa-1,3-diene < cyclohexa-1,4-diene**. Three times the cyclohexene value predicts about −354 kJ/mol for benzene; the real value is about 148 kJ/mol smaller. This is the aromatic stabilisation (resonance energy) of benzene, and it is why "more π bonds, more heat" fails here. The conjugated 1,3-diene is slightly more stable than the isolated 1,4-diene, as in open-chain dienes.

## Cycloalkanes and ring strain

Rings of different sizes have different formulas, so compare the heat of combustion **per CH₂ group**:

| Ring | ΔH combustion per CH₂ (kcal/mol) | Ring strain (kcal/mol) |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclopropane | 156.2 | 27.6 |
| Cyclobutane | 153.6 | 26.4 |
| Cyclopentane | 148.3 | 6.5 |
| Cyclohexane | 147.0 | 0 |

**Stability:** cyclopropane < cyclobutane < cyclopentane < cyclohexane. Cyclohexane (chair) has the same value per CH₂ as an open-chain alkane, so it is strain-free. Cyclopropane has C–C–C angles of 60° instead of 109.5° (angle strain) and all its C–H bonds are eclipsed (torsional strain); cyclobutane is almost as strained. Cyclopentane is only slightly strained, and medium rings (7 to 11 atoms) pick up some strain again from crowding across the ring, but much less than three- and four-membered rings. High strain is also why cyclopropane opens with H₂ or Br₂ much more easily than larger rings.

## Endocyclic versus exocyclic double bonds

- **Three-membered rings: exo is more stable.** A double bond inside a cyclopropane ring forces two sp² carbons into a 60° ring, so cyclopropene is very strained, more so than cyclopropane. Comparing isomers, 1-methylcyclopropene (endocyclic) is converted by base into methylenecyclopropane (exocyclic), the more stable isomer.
- **Six-membered rings: endo is more stable.** Here strain is not the issue and the substitution rule takes over: 1-methylcyclohexene (endocyclic, trisubstituted) is more stable than methylenecyclohexane (exocyclic, disubstituted), and dehydration of 2-methylcyclohexanol gives 1-methylcyclohexene as the main product.

<ExamTip title="Compare like with like">
Heat of hydrogenation or combustion only ranks stability for compounds that give the same product or have the same formula. Cyclopropene (C₃H₄) and methylenecyclopropane (C₄H₆) cannot be ranked from their heats of combustion directly; compare isomers instead.
</ExamTip>

## Solved examples

**1. Arrange in increasing heat of hydrogenation: 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, cis-but-2-ene, trans-but-2-ene, but-1-ene.**
Less heat means more stable; tetra > trans-di > cis-di > mono.
**Answer:** 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene < trans-but-2-ene < cis-but-2-ene < but-1-ene.

**2. Arrange penta-1,3-diene, penta-1,4-diene and penta-1,2-diene in decreasing stability.**
Conjugated > isolated > cumulated.
**Answer:** penta-1,3-diene > penta-1,4-diene > penta-1,2-diene.

**3. Which has the lower heat of combustion, pentane or 2,2-dimethylpropane?**
They are isomers (C₅H₁₂). 2,2-Dimethylpropane releases about 777 kcal/mol against 782 kcal/mol for pentane.
**Answer:** 2,2-dimethylpropane, so the branched isomer is more stable by about 5 kcal/mol.
