## What is the acidic strength order?

The **acidic strength order** ranks compounds by how readily they give up H⁺ to a base. The stronger acid has the larger Ka and the **smaller pKa**, and it always has the more stable conjugate base. For the common organic families the order is:

<KeyPoint title="Final order (aqueous pKa in brackets)">
**R–SO₃H (about −2) > R–COOH (4–5) > H₂CO₃ (6.4) > phenol (10) > H₂O (15.7) ≈ CH₃OH (15.5) > other alcohols R–OH (16–18) > R–C≡CH (25) > R–NH₂, N–H (35–40) > R–CH=CH₂ (44) > R–CH₃ (50 or more)**

Rule: acidic character ∝ Ka ∝ 1/pKa ∝ electron-withdrawing groups ∝ 1/electron-donating groups.
</KeyPoint>

## Acid–base theories in one table

| Theory | Acid | Base | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrhenius | gives H⁺ in water | gives OH⁻ in water | HCl, NaOH |
| Brønsted–Lowry | H⁺ donor | H⁺ acceptor | CH₃COOH + H₂O |
| Lewis | electron-pair acceptor | electron-pair donor | BF₃, carbocations (acids); NH₃ (base) |
| Lux–Flood | oxide-ion (O²⁻) acceptor | oxide-ion donor | CaO + SiO₂ gives CaSiO₃ |

Non-metal oxides (CO₂, SO₃, SiO₂) behave as acids and metal oxides (CaO, MgO) as bases, which is exactly the Lux–Flood picture. Organic chemistry mostly uses the Brønsted and Lewis definitions.

## Why each step of the order holds

Every comparison comes down to **how well the conjugate base holds the negative charge**.

| Acid | Conjugate base | What stabilises it |
|---|---|---|
| R–SO₃H | R–SO₃⁻ | charge spread over three oxygens next to S |
| R–COOH | R–COO⁻ | two equivalent resonance forms, charge on two O |
| Phenol | C₆H₅O⁻ | charge delocalised into the ring, but onto carbon |
| R–OH | R–O⁻ | no resonance; charge on one O |
| R–C≡C–H | R–C≡C⁻ | lone pair in an sp orbital (50% s) |
| R–NH₂ | R–NH⁻ | N is less electronegative than O |
| Alkene, alkane | vinyl, alkyl anion | sp² (33% s), sp³ (25% s) carbon |

- **Sulphonic versus carboxylic acids.** Sulphonic acids are about a million times stronger: methanesulphonic acid has pKa −1.9 and p-toluenesulphonic acid about −2.8, against 4.2 for benzoic acid and 4.76 for acetic acid.
- **Carboxylic acid versus phenol.** The carboxylate charge sits on two equivalent oxygens; in phenoxide it is spread partly onto less electronegative ring carbons, so phenol is weaker.
- **Phenol versus alcohol.** Phenol (pKa 10) is about a million times more acidic than ethanol (about 16) because phenoxide is resonance stabilised.
- **Alcohols.** Acidity falls 1° > 2° > 3°: alkyl groups push electron density onto oxygen, and bulky alkoxides are poorly solvated (tert-butyl alcohol pKa 18).
- **Hydrocarbons and s character.** Stability of a carbanion rises with % s character, so ethyne (25) > ethene (44) > ethane (50). Higher s character also means a shorter bond (bond length ∝ 1/% s character).

<ExamTip title="Water or methanol?">
On the aqueous pKa scale methanol (15.5) is a hair stronger than water (15.7), which is why many orders write CH₃OH > H₂O > R–OH. NCERT, comparing water with alcohols in general, states that water is the better proton donor. In a question with ethanol, propanol or tert-butyl alcohol, water is the stronger acid.
</ExamTip>

## Strong organic acids and other groups

Most organic acids are weak, unlike mineral acids (HCl has pKa about −7). The exceptions to remember are sulphonic acids, trifluoroacetic acid (pKa about 0.2, the strongest common carboxylic acid), **picric acid** (2,4,6-trinitrophenol, pKa 0.38) and **squaric acid** (pKa₁ about 1.5), whose anion is strongly stabilised by resonance.

For acidic hydrogens on charged groups the usual exam order is **–SO₃H > –OH₂⁺ > –COOH > –NH₃⁺**: protonated alcohols (oxonium ions) have negative pKa values, whereas alkylammonium ions are weak acids with pKa near 10.6.

Down a group, acid strength follows bond weakness: **HI (−10.4) > HBr (−5.8) > HCl (−3.9) > HF (3.1)**, so the basicity of the halide ions is the reverse, F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻.

HOCN (cyanic acid) and HNCO (isocyanic acid) are tautomers that lose H⁺ to give the same cyanate ion, so one pKa (about 3.7) describes the pair.

## Predicting acid–base equilibria

A proton always moves from the **stronger acid to the conjugate base of the weaker acid**. Keq equals Ka(acid on the left) divided by Ka(acid on the right).

<Eq ce="NaF + HCN <=> NaCN + HF" />

HF (pKa 3.2) is far stronger than HCN (pKa 9.2), so CN⁻ is the stronger base and the equilibrium lies to the **left**: Keq is about 10⁻⁶, not greater than 1.

## NaOH-equivalent questions

When a molecule has several acidic hydrogens, each equivalent of base removes the **most acidic remaining proton**.

- **NaOH** (conjugate acid H₂O, pKa 15.7) deprotonates –SO₃H, –COOH, phenolic –OH and ammonium ions, but alcohols only to a small extent.
- **NaHCO₃** (conjugate acid H₂CO₃, pKa 6.4) deprotonates only acids stronger than carbonic acid: –SO₃H, –COOH, picric acid and 2,4-dinitrophenol, giving CO₂ effervescence. Phenol does not react.

**Solved example 1.** 5-Sulphosalicylic acid has –SO₃H, –COOH and a phenolic –OH. With 1 equivalent of NaOH the –SO₃H proton goes; with 2 equivalents –SO₃H and –COOH; with 3 equivalents the phenolic OH as well.

**Solved example 2.** In a dication carrying an anilinium group (Ar–NH₃⁺, pKa about 4.6) and an alkylammonium group (R–NH₃⁺, pKa about 10.6), 1 equivalent of NaOH removes the anilinium proton first, and the second equivalent removes the alkylammonium proton.

**Solved example 3.** Arrange in increasing acidity: ethanol, phenol, acetic acid, benzenesulphonic acid, ethyne.
**Answer:** ethyne < ethanol < phenol < acetic acid < benzenesulphonic acid.
