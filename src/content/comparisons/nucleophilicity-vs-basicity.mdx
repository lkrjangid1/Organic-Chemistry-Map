## What is the difference between nucleophilicity and basicity?

**Basicity** is the ability of a species to donate an electron pair to a **proton**; it is an equilibrium (thermodynamic) property, measured by the pKa of the conjugate acid. **Nucleophilicity** is the ability of the same electron pair to **attack an electron-poor atom, usually carbon**; it is a rate (kinetic) property, measured by how fast the species reacts, for example in SN2.

<Eq ce="Nu- + H-X <=> Nu-H + X-" />
<Eq ce="Nu- + R-X -> Nu-R + X-" />

The first reaction is an acid–base equilibrium (basicity); the second is a substitution whose speed reflects nucleophilicity.

<KeyPoint title="Final rules">
1. **Same attacking atom, similar size:** nucleophilicity follows basicity. RO⁻ > HO⁻ > RCOO⁻ > ROH > H₂O; R–NH₂ > C₆H₅–NH₂ > R–CO–NH₂.
2. **Across a period:** both fall together. CH₃⁻ > NH₂⁻ > HO⁻ > F⁻.
3. **Down a group:** basicity falls (F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻), but nucleophilicity depends on the solvent: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ in protic solvents; F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ in polar aprotic solvents.
4. **Steric bulk:** bulk lowers nucleophilicity much more than basicity. Basicity (CH₃)₃CO⁻ > (CH₃)₂CHO⁻ > CH₃O⁻; nucleophilicity CH₃O⁻ > (CH₃)₂CHO⁻ > (CH₃)₃CO⁻.
</KeyPoint>

## Rule 1: same attacking atom

When the electron pair sits on the same element, the more available it is to a proton, the more available it is to carbon.

| Series | Basicity and nucleophilicity | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen nucleophiles | RO⁻ > HO⁻ > ArO⁻ > RCOO⁻ > H₂O | negative charge beats neutral; resonance in phenoxide and carboxylate spreads the charge |
| Nitrogen nucleophiles | R–NH₂ > C₆H₅–NH₂ > R–CO–NH₂ | in aniline the lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring; in an amide it is delocalised onto the C=O oxygen |

Aniline's conjugate acid has pKa about 4.6, against about 10.6 for methylammonium, and aniline is also a weaker nucleophile than aliphatic amines. Compare [basic strength of amines](/comparisons/basic-strength-of-amines/).

## Rule 2: across a period

Moving right along a row, electronegativity increases and the atom holds its lone pair more tightly, so both basicity and nucleophilicity fall: CH₃⁻ > NH₂⁻ > HO⁻ > F⁻, and NH₃ > H₂O.

## Rule 3: down a group, where solvent decides

| Ion | Basicity | Nucleophilicity in protic solvent (water, methanol) | Nucleophilicity in polar aprotic solvent (DMSO, DMF, acetone) |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻ | highest | lowest | highest |
| Cl⁻ | | | |
| Br⁻ | | | |
| I⁻ | lowest | highest | lowest |

In a **protic** solvent the small, charge-dense ions are caged by hydrogen bonds; the large, polarisable I⁻ is held weakly and attacks fastest. In a **polar aprotic** solvent there is no hydrogen-bond cage, so the intrinsic order, which follows basicity, appears. The same reasoning makes RS⁻ and HS⁻ better nucleophiles than RO⁻ and HO⁻ in protic solvents, even though they are weaker bases. See [solvent effects](/concepts/solvent-effects/).

A measured order in methanol (reaction with methyl bromide) is: CH₃COO⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < N₃⁻ < CH₃O⁻ < CN⁻ ≈ SCN⁻ < I⁻ < CH₃S⁻. In DMSO it becomes roughly the basicity order: I⁻ < SCN⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ ≈ N₃⁻ < CH₃COO⁻ < CN⁻ ≈ CH₃S⁻ < CH₃O⁻.

## Rule 4: steric hindrance

A proton is small and sits on the outside of a molecule, but SN2 needs the nucleophile to reach a carbon from the back. Branching therefore hurts nucleophilicity far more than basicity.

| Alkoxide | pKa of parent alcohol | Basicity | SN2 nucleophilicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₃O⁻ | about 15.5 | lowest | highest |
| CH₃CH₂O⁻ | about 15.9 | | |
| (CH₃)₂CHO⁻ | about 16.5 | | |
| (CH₃)₃CO⁻ | about 17 | highest | lowest |

Potassium tert-butoxide is the classic **strong base, weak nucleophile**: it removes protons and drives E2 elimination instead of Williamson ether synthesis. LDA is an even stronger non-nucleophilic base.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not write "nucleophilicity ∝ basicity" as a universal rule. It holds for the same attacking atom with similar bulk, across a period, and in polar aprotic solvents. It fails down a group in protic solvents (I⁻ is the best halide nucleophile in water but the weakest base) and for bulky bases such as (CH₃)₃CO⁻.
</ExamTip>

## Why it matters: substitution or elimination?

A reagent can use its lone pair on carbon (substitution) or on a β-hydrogen (elimination). Strong, unhindered nucleophiles that are weak bases (I⁻, Br⁻, RS⁻, N₃⁻, CN⁻) favour [SN2](/reactions/sn2-reaction/). Strong, hindered bases ((CH₃)₃CO⁻, LDA) favour [E2](/reactions/e2-reaction/). Strong, unhindered bases (OH⁻, CH₃O⁻) give both, with heat and secondary or tertiary substrates tipping the balance to elimination.

## Solved examples

**1. Arrange in increasing nucleophilicity in methanol: F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻.**
Protic solvent, so solvation dominates. **Answer:** F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻.

**2. Arrange CH₃COO⁻, CH₃O⁻, C₆H₅O⁻ in decreasing nucleophilicity.**
Same attacking atom (O), so follow basicity; resonance weakens phenoxide and acetate more. **Answer:** CH₃O⁻ > C₆H₅O⁻ > CH₃COO⁻.

**3. Which is the better nucleophile in DMSO, F⁻ or I⁻? Which is the stronger base?**
In a polar aprotic solvent nucleophilicity follows basicity. **Answer:** F⁻ is both the stronger base and the better nucleophile.
