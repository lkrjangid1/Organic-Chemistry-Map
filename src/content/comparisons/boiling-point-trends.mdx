  - q: "Why does branching lower the boiling point of isomers?"
    a: "Branching makes a molecule more compact and nearly spherical, so it has a smaller surface area of contact with its neighbours. The van der Waals forces are weaker and are overcome at a lower temperature: pentane boils at 309 K, 2-methylbutane at about 301 K, and 2,2-dimethylpropane lower still."
  - q: "Why do alcohols have higher boiling points than ethers of the same molar mass?"
    a: "The O–H group lets alcohol molecules form intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which need extra energy to break. Ethers have no O–H, so they only have weak dipole–dipole forces; methoxymethane therefore boils far below its isomer ethanol."
  - q: "Why is o-nitrophenol more volatile than p-nitrophenol?"
    a: "In o-nitrophenol the –OH and –NO₂ groups are close enough to form an intramolecular hydrogen bond, so the molecules do not associate with each other. p-Nitrophenol forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which hold its molecules together, so it boils much higher and is not steam volatile."
  - q: "What is the order of boiling points of 1°, 2° and 3° amines?"
    a: "For isomeric amines the order is primary > secondary > tertiary. A primary amine has two N–H hydrogens for hydrogen bonding, a secondary amine has one and a tertiary amine has none; NCERT gives 350.8 K, 329.3 K and 310.5 K for the three C₄H₁₁N isomers."
  - q: "Why do carboxylic acids have higher boiling points than alcohols?"
    a: "Carboxylic acid molecules are held together by two hydrogen bonds each, forming dimers that persist even in the vapour phase. This association is stronger than in alcohols of comparable molar mass, so acids boil higher."

## What decides the boiling point of an organic compound?

The **boiling point** of a liquid is reached when its molecules get enough energy to overcome the **intermolecular forces** holding them together. In organic compounds these forces are, from weakest to strongest, **van der Waals (dispersion) forces**, **dipole–dipole attraction** and **hydrogen bonding**. So boiling point rises with molar mass and surface area, falls with branching, and rises sharply when a molecule can hydrogen-bond to its neighbours.

<KeyPoint title="Rules to compare boiling points">
1. **Molar mass:** bigger molecule, more electrons, stronger dispersion forces, higher b.p.
2. **Branching:** for isomers, more branching means smaller surface area and lower b.p.
3. **Polarity:** for similar mass, a more polar molecule (larger dipole moment) boils higher.
4. **Hydrogen bonding:** intermolecular H-bonding raises b.p. most; intramolecular H-bonding does not.

**Classes of comparable molar mass:** RCOOH > ROH > ketones ≥ aldehydes ≈ 1° amines > ethers > alkanes.
</KeyPoint>

## Rule 1: molar mass

Within a homologous series the boiling point rises steadily with chain length: n-butane boils at 273 K and n-pentane at 309.1 K. For alkyl halides with the same alkyl group the order is **RI > RBr > RCl > RF**, because larger, heavier halogen atoms give stronger van der Waals forces.

## Rule 2: branching

| C₅H₁₂ isomer | b.p. |
|---|---|
| Pentane (straight chain) | 309.1 K |
| 2-Methylbutane | 300.8 K |
| 2,2-Dimethylpropane | lowest of the three |

A branched molecule is more compact and closer to a sphere, so it touches its neighbours over a smaller area. The same holds for alcohols and haloalkanes: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane has the lowest boiling point of its isomers, and n-hexane boils above 3-methylpentane.

## Rule 3: polarity

| Pair (same formula) | Higher b.p. | Data |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃C≡N vs CH₃N≡C | nitrile | about 82 °C vs 59–60 °C |
| CH₃NO₂ vs CH₃–O–N=O | nitro compound | 101.2 °C vs −12 °C |
| cis- vs trans-but-2-ene | cis | 3.7 °C vs 0.8 °C |
| o- vs p-dichlorobenzene | ortho | about 180 °C vs 174 °C |

Acetonitrile (3.92 D) and nitromethane (3.46 D) are strongly polar, and their molecules attract one another by dipole–dipole forces. In exam answers the lower boiling points of the isocyanide and the nitrite are put down to their lower polarity. The cis alkene is polar (cis-but-2-ene, 0.33 D) while the trans alkene is almost non-polar. In the para dichloro isomer the C–Cl dipoles cancel, but the difference in boiling point among the three dichlorobenzenes is small.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Ethane, ethene and ethyne are all non-polar, so polarity cannot explain their order. The measured values are **ethene (−103.7 °C) less than ethane (−88.5 °C) less than ethyne**, which sublimes at about −84 °C at 1 atm. Do not write "ethane less than ethene less than ethyne due to polarity".
</ExamTip>

## Rule 4: hydrogen bonding

**Alcohols versus ethers and alkanes.** Ethanol and propane have similar molar masses but boil far apart; methoxymethane, which is polar but cannot hydrogen-bond to itself, lies in between.

**Classes of molar mass 58–60 (NCERT data):**

| Compound | Class | b.p. (K) |
|---|---|---|
| n-Butane | alkane | 273 |
| Methoxyethane | ether | 281 |
| Propanal | aldehyde | 322 |
| Acetone | ketone | 329 |
| Propan-1-ol | alcohol | 370 |

Carboxylic acids boil even higher than alcohols of similar mass because they form hydrogen-bonded dimers that survive in the vapour.

**Amines.** Primary amines have two N–H hydrogens, secondary amines one and tertiary amines none:

| C₄H₁₁N isomer (molar mass 73) | b.p. (K) |
|---|---|
| n-Butylamine (1°) | 350.8 |
| Diethylamine (2°) | 329.3 |
| Ethyldimethylamine (3°) | 310.5 |
| Butan-1-ol (74), for comparison | 390.3 |

N–H···N hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H···O bonds, so amines boil below alcohols. Propylamine, for example, boils at about 320–324 K, close to propanal (322 K) and below acetone (329 K).

**Nitrophenols.** In o-nitrophenol the –OH bonds to the neighbouring –NO₂ group inside the same molecule, so the molecules stay separate and the compound is steam volatile. m- and p-Nitrophenol hydrogen-bond between molecules and boil much higher. This difference is used to separate o- and p-nitrophenol by steam distillation.

## Melting point is different

Melting point also depends on how well molecules **pack in the crystal**. Symmetric molecules pack better: p-dichlorobenzene melts at 53.5 °C while o-dichlorobenzene melts at −17 °C, even though their boiling points are close. Likewise trans-but-2-ene melts higher than cis-but-2-ene.

## Solved examples

**1. Arrange propan-1-ol, acetone, n-butane, methoxyethane and propanal in increasing b.p.**
No polarity, then weak polarity, then dipole–dipole, then H-bonding.
**Answer:** n-butane < methoxyethane < propanal < acetone < propan-1-ol.

**2. Arrange the isomers propylamine, ethylmethylamine and trimethylamine in decreasing b.p.**
Count N–H hydrogens available for hydrogen bonding: 2, 1, 0.
**Answer:** propylamine > ethylmethylamine > trimethylamine.

**3. Arrange ethanol, methoxymethane and ethane in increasing b.p.**
Ethane is non-polar, the ether is polar, ethanol hydrogen-bonds.
**Answer:** ethane < methoxymethane < ethanol.
