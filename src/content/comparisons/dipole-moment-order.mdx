## What is dipole moment?

**Dipole moment (μ)** measures how far positive and negative charge are separated in a bond or molecule. For two charges it is **μ = q × d**, charge multiplied by the distance between the charges, and it is expressed in **debye (D)**, where 1 D ≈ 3.34 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. A molecule's dipole moment is the **vector sum of its bond dipoles**, so both bond polarity and molecular shape decide the final value.

<KeyPoint title="Orders to remember">
- **Chloromethanes:** CH₃Cl > CH₂Cl₂ > CHCl₃ > CCl₄ (= 0)
- **Methyl halides:** CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I
- **Geometrical isomers:** cis > trans (trans with identical groups = 0)
- **Disubstituted benzenes (two identical groups):** ortho > meta > para (= 0)
</KeyPoint>

## Factors that decide the dipole moment

| Factor | Effect on μ | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity difference | larger difference, larger bond dipole | HF > HCl > HBr > HI |
| Bond length | longer bond, larger μ for the same charge | C–Cl beats C–F |
| Angle between bond dipoles | wider angle, more cancellation | o- > m- > p-dichlorobenzene |
| Symmetry | equal dipoles arranged symmetrically cancel | CO₂, CCl₄, trans-ClCH=CHCl are zero |

For two equal bond dipoles μ₁ at an angle θ, the resultant is **2μ₁ cos(θ/2)**. It is largest when the bonds point the same way and falls to zero at 180°. This is the meaning of the short rule "μ decreases as the bond angle increases". Water shows it well: bent at about 104.5°, it has μ ≈ 1.85 D, while linear CO₂ has zero dipole moment even though each C=O bond is polar.

**Dipole moment and ionic character.** A bond with a larger dipole moment is **more polar, and therefore more ionic**. The percentage ionic character is the measured μ divided by the value expected if one full electron charge were separated by the bond length, times 100. For CH₃Cl, full separation over 178 pm would give about 8.5 D; the observed 1.87 D means roughly 22% ionic character.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not write "dipole moment ∝ covalent character". Dipole moment increases with polarity, and polarity is the same thing as partial ionic character. A purely covalent bond between identical atoms (H–H, Cl–Cl) has μ = 0.
</ExamTip>

## Step 1: methyl halides, CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I

| Compound | C–X bond length (pm) | μ (D, NCERT) |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃F | 139 | 1.847 |
| CH₃Cl | 178 | 1.860 |
| CH₃Br | 193 | 1.830 |
| CH₃I | 214 | 1.636 |

Electronegativity alone would predict CH₃F at the top. But μ = q × d: fluorine creates the largest partial charge over a very short bond, while chlorine creates a slightly smaller charge over a much longer bond. The two effects nearly balance and chlorine edges ahead. From Cl to Br to I the charge falls faster than the length rises, so μ drops.

## Step 2: chloromethanes, CH₃Cl > CH₂Cl₂ > CHCl₃ > CCl₄

| Compound | μ (D) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃Cl | about 1.9 | one strong C–Cl dipole, nothing to oppose it |
| CH₂Cl₂ | about 1.6 | two C–Cl dipoles at about 109.5°, partly cancel |
| CHCl₃ | about 1.1 | three C–Cl dipoles spread round the carbon, more cancellation |
| CCl₄ | 0 | four equal dipoles in a regular tetrahedron cancel exactly |

The more chlorine atoms are distributed symmetrically round the tetrahedral carbon, the more their bond dipoles point in opposing directions. In CCl₄ the cancellation is complete, so it is non-polar although every C–Cl bond is polar.

## Step 3: cis versus trans

In a trans alkene with identical groups, the two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel. In the cis isomer they add.

| Pair | cis | trans |
|---|---|---|
| ClCH=CHCl | about 1.9 D | 0 D |
| CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | 0.33 D | almost 0 |

Because the cis isomer is polar, it usually has the higher boiling point (cis-1,2-dichloroethene boils at about 60 °C, the trans isomer at about 49 °C). The trans isomer packs better and usually melts higher.

## Step 4: ortho, meta and para isomers

For C₆H₄Cl₂ the two C–Cl dipoles make angles of 60° (ortho), 120° (meta) and 180° (para). Using 2μ₁ cos(θ/2), the resultant is about 1.7μ₁, μ₁ and 0 respectively, so **ortho > meta > para = 0**.

This ortho > meta > para rule holds for two **identical** groups. When the two groups differ, work out the direction of each bond dipole first and then add them as vectors.

## Solved examples

**1. Arrange CCl₄, CHCl₃, CH₂Cl₂ and CH₃Cl in increasing dipole moment.**
More symmetric distribution of C–Cl dipoles means more cancellation.
**Answer:** CCl₄ (0) < CHCl₃ < CH₂Cl₂ < CH₃Cl.

**2. Arrange CH₃F, CH₃Cl, CH₃Br, CH₃I in decreasing dipole moment.**
Charge falls and bond length rises down the group; for F the short bond outweighs the large charge.
**Answer:** CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I.

**3. Which of these have zero dipole moment: CO₂, H₂O, p-dichlorobenzene, trans-1,2-dichloroethene, CHCl₃?**
Zero only where equal bond dipoles are arranged symmetrically.
**Answer:** CO₂, p-dichlorobenzene and trans-1,2-dichloroethene.
