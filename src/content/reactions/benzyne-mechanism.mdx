## What is the benzyne mechanism?

The **benzyne mechanism** (elimination–addition) is a nucleophilic aromatic substitution used by **unactivated aryl halides** with very strong bases such as **sodium amide (NaNH₂) in liquid ammonia**. The base first removes HX from the ring to give **benzyne**, a short-lived intermediate with a strained triple bond; the nucleophile then adds across that bond. Chlorobenzene gives aniline this way. Because the nucleophile can add to **either** end of the triple bond, part of the product carries the new group on the carbon **next to** the original halogen; this is called **cine substitution**.

## Mechanism

1. **Proton removal.** NH₂⁻ removes a hydrogen **ortho** to the halogen, giving an aryl carbanion next to the C–X carbon.
2. **Elimination.** The carbanion expels X⁻ to form **benzyne**. The new π bond is made from sp² orbitals lying in the ring plane, so it is weak and very reactive. Steps 1 and 2 together are an elimination of HX.
3. **Addition.** NH₂⁻ adds to **either carbon** of the triple bond, giving a new aryl anion.
4. **Protonation.** Liquid ammonia protonates the aryl anion, giving aniline and regenerating NH₂⁻.

<Eq ce="C6H5Cl ->[NH2-][-NH3] C6H4Cl- ->[-Cl-] C6H4 ->[NH2-] C6H4NH2- ->[NH3] C6H5NH2 + NH2-" />

<KeyPoint>
Label the carbon that carried the halogen as C*. Products with NH₂ on C* come from **direct substitution**; products with NH₂ on the carbon next to C* come from **cine substitution**. Both form because benzyne can be attacked at either end.
</KeyPoint>

## Evidence: Roberts' carbon-14 experiment

In 1953 **John D. Roberts** and co-workers heated chlorobenzene labelled with ¹⁴C at C-1 with potassium amide in liquid ammonia. The aniline contained the label **at C-1 and at C-2 in equal amounts**. A direct addition–elimination would keep the label only at the carbon bearing NH₂ (C-1). An equal split means a symmetrical intermediate was formed, which is benzyne.

<Eq ce="C6H5-^{14}C-Cl ->[KNH2][liq. NH3] \text{aniline with label at C-1 (50\%)} + \text{label at C-2 (50\%)}" />

## Examples

**1. Bromobenzene to aniline.** The reaction with potassium amide in liquid ammonia is extremely fast even at −33 °C.

<Eq ce="C6H5Br + KNH2 ->[liq. NH3][240 K] C6H5NH2 + KBr" />

**2. Substituted benzynes give mixtures.** 4-Chlorotoluene gives 4-methylbenzyne. Hydroxide adding at either end of the triple bond then gives both 3- and 4-methylphenol; LibreTexts reports an equimolar mixture when the chloride is heated with NaOH at about 340 °C.

<Eq ce="CH3C6H4Cl ->[NaOH][\Delta] \text{3-methylphenol} + \text{4-methylphenol}" />

**3. 2-Chloroanisole gives only the meta product.** Amination of 2-chloromethoxybenzene gives 3-methoxyaniline exclusively. The benzyne formed has the triple bond next to –OCH₃, and the amide ion adds to the carbon farther from it, because the resulting aryl anion sits next to the electron-withdrawing (–I) oxygen. The new group is not where the chlorine was, which only the benzyne route explains.

<Eq ce="CH3OC6H4Cl ->[NaNH2][liq. NH3] \text{3-methoxyaniline}" />

<ExamTip>
To predict benzyne products: (a) find each ortho H that can be removed; (b) draw the benzyne for each; (c) add NH₂⁻ to both ends of each triple bond. Count only distinct products. If the halogen has no ortho H, there is no benzyne product.
</ExamTip>

## Benzyne vs SNAr (addition–elimination)

| Feature | Benzyne (elimination–addition) | SNAr (addition–elimination) |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Unactivated aryl halide with ortho H | Aryl halide with ortho/para –NO₂ |
| Reagent | Very strong base: NaNH₂/KNH₂ in liq. NH₃ | Nucleophile: OH⁻, RO⁻, amines |
| Intermediate | Neutral benzyne | Anionic Meisenheimer complex |
| Position of new group | Ipso and cine (adjacent) products | Only ipso (where X was) |

## Limitations and exceptions

- An aryl halide with **no ortho hydrogen** cannot form benzyne and does not react this way.
- Nucleophile addition to substituted benzynes is guided by which aryl anion is more stable, so electron-withdrawing substituents shift the product ratio.
- Under forcing hydroxide conditions (Dow-type phenol synthesis) both pathways may operate; activated substrates prefer SNAr.
