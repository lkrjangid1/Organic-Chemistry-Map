## What is ester hydrolysis?

**Ester hydrolysis** is the splitting of an ester, R–COO–R′, by water into a **carboxylic acid (or its salt) and an alcohol**. It is **catalysed by acid** (reversible, the reverse of Fischer esterification) or **promoted by base** (irreversible; called **saponification**). Mechanistically, the key questions are **which C–O bond breaks**, the acyl–oxygen bond or the alkyl–oxygen bond, and **how many species take part in the slow step**. Ingold's notation labels all the possibilities.

<Eq ce="RCOOR' + H2O <=>[H+] RCOOH + R'OH" />
<Eq ce="RCOOR' + OH- -> RCOO- + R'OH" />

## Ingold's classification

Each label has three parts: **A** (acid) or **B** (base); **AC** (acyl–oxygen cleavage, R–CO–|–OR′) or **AL** (alkyl–oxygen cleavage, R–COO–|–R′); and **1 or 2**, the molecularity of the rate-determining step.

| Label | Medium | Bond broken | Key intermediate | When it happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AAC2** | acid | acyl–O | tetrahedral intermediate | normal esters (the general acid mechanism) |
| AAC1 | acid | acyl–O | acylium ion R–C≡O⁺ | rare; very hindered acyl groups in strong acid |
| **AAL1** | acid | alkyl–O | carbocation R′⁺ | R′ forms a stable cation (tert-butyl) |
| AAL2 | acid | alkyl–O | — | not observed |
| **BAC2** | base | acyl–O | tetrahedral alkoxide | normal saponification (the general base mechanism) |
| BAC1 | base | acyl–O | — | not observed |
| BAL1 | neutral/weak base | alkyl–O | carbocation R′⁺ | tert-alkyl esters in water |
| BAL2 | base | alkyl–O | SN2 transition state | methyl esters with strong nucleophiles |

<KeyPoint>
Almost every exam question is about **AAC2** (acid) and **BAC2** (base). Switch to **alkyl–oxygen cleavage** only when R′ can form a stable carbocation (AAL1, BAL1) or when R′ is methyl and a strong nucleophile does SN2 on it (BAL2).
</KeyPoint>

## AAC2: acid-catalysed hydrolysis

1. The **carbonyl oxygen is protonated**, making the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
2. **Water attacks the carbonyl carbon** (slow step), giving a tetrahedral intermediate with an –OH₂⁺ group.
3. A **proton transfer** moves H⁺ onto the –OR′ oxygen.
4. **R′OH leaves**, giving the protonated acid.
5. **Loss of H⁺** gives R–COOH and regenerates the catalyst.

With H₂¹⁸O, the label ends up in the **acid**, and the alcohol keeps the original alkoxy oxygen.

## BAC2: saponification

1. **OH⁻ adds to the carbonyl carbon** (slow step), giving a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate.
2. The C=O re-forms and **R′O⁻ is expelled**.
3. The alkoxide **removes the acidic proton** of R–COOH, giving R–COO⁻ and R′OH. This last step pulls the reaction to completion, and a full equivalent of base is used up.

<Eq ce="CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH ->[\Delta] CH3COONa + C2H5OH" />

**Isotopic evidence:** when the alkoxy oxygen of ethyl propanoate is labelled with ¹⁸O, the label is found in the **ethanol** after saponification. This proves acyl–oxygen cleavage; an SN2 attack on the ethyl carbon would have left the ¹⁸O in the propanoate.

## Alkyl–oxygen cleavage: AAL1, BAL1 and BAL2

- **AAL1 (like SN1):** after protonation, the O–R′ bond ionises to give the acid and a **carbocation R′⁺**, which water then captures. With H₂¹⁸O the label appears in the **alcohol** R′–¹⁸OH. Tracer and kinetic studies on tert-butyl acetate in water found 85–97% alkyl–oxygen fission. In exam answers benzyl and cyclopropylmethyl esters are also placed here because their cations are stabilised.
- **BAL1:** tert-alkyl esters can ionise even without acid (uncatalysed solvolysis in water).
- **BAL2 (like SN2):** a strong nucleophile attacks the **methyl carbon** of a methyl ester and carboxylate leaves; halide salts in DMSO or DMF cleave methyl esters this way.
- **AAC1:** when the acyl group is very hindered, the protonated ester can lose R′OH to give an **acylium ion**, which water then attacks.

<ExamTip>
For "where does ¹⁸O go?" questions: in AAC2/BAC2 the oxygen from water (or OH⁻) ends up in the **acid/carboxylate**; in AAL1 it ends up in the **alcohol**. Check whether R′ is tertiary first.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Acid hydrolysis of methyl benzoate**

<Eq ce="C6H5COOCH3 + H2O <=>[H+] C6H5COOH + CH3OH" />

**2. Saponification of a fat (soap making)**

<Eq ce="(C17H33COO)3C3H5 + 3 NaOH -> 3 C17H33COONa + C3H5(OH)3" />

Glyceryl trioleate gives sodium oleate (soap) and glycerol.

**3. tert-Butyl acetate in acid (AAL1)**

<Eq ce="CH3COOC(CH3)3 + H2O ->[H+] CH3COOH + (CH3)3COH" />
