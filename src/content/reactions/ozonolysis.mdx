## What is ozonolysis?

**Ozonolysis** is the cleavage of a **C=C double bond by ozone (O₃)**, in which each alkene carbon becomes a **C=O** group. Ozone first adds to give an **ozonide**, which is then broken down in a work-up step. A **reductive work-up (Zn/H₂O, or dimethyl sulphide)** gives **aldehydes and ketones**; an **oxidative work-up (H₂O₂)** turns any aldehyde fragments into **carboxylic acids**. For example, but-2-ene gives two molecules of acetaldehyde with O₃ then Zn/H₂O.

## Mechanism (Criegee mechanism)

1. **1,3-Dipolar cycloaddition.** Ozone adds across the C=C bond to form an unstable five-membered **molozonide** (primary ozonide, a 1,2,3-trioxolane).
2. **Fragmentation.** The molozonide splits into a carbonyl compound and a **carbonyl oxide** (Criegee intermediate).
3. **Recombination.** These two pieces add together again the other way round to give the more stable **ozonide** (1,2,4-trioxolane), with a C–O–C bridge and an O–O bond.
4. **Work-up.** Hydrolysis of the ozonide gives two carbonyl compounds and H₂O₂. Zinc removes the H₂O₂ (Zn + H₂O₂ → ZnO + H₂O), protecting any aldehyde.

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH-CH3 ->[O3] ozonide ->[Zn/H2O] 2CH3-CHO" />

<Trick>
**"Zn नहीं तो"**: if zinc is not present, the H₂O₂ formed oxidises the aldehyde to the acid: CH₃CHO → CH₃COOH.
</Trick>

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH-CH3 ->[(i) O3][(ii) H2O2] 2CH3-COOH" />

<KeyPoint>
Reductive work-up: =CH– becomes –CHO, =CR₂ becomes a ketone, =CH₂ becomes HCHO. Oxidative work-up: –CHO becomes –COOH, but ketones stay as ketones.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. But-2-ene**: two CH₃CHO (Zn/H₂O) or two CH₃COOH (without Zn).

**2. 2-Methylpropene** gives acetone + formaldehyde:

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C=CH2 ->[(i) O3][(ii) Zn/H2O] (CH3)2C=O + HCHO" />

**3. Benzene** gives three molecules of **glyoxal**. Ozone reacts with each of the three Kekulé C=C bonds:

<Eq ce="C6H6 ->[(i) 3O3][(ii) Zn/H2O] 3OHC-CHO" />

**Substituted benzenes.** Using the same rule, toluene gives one methylglyoxal (CH₃–CO–CHO) and two glyoxal. o-Xylene gives a mixture of glyoxal, methylglyoxal and biacetyl (CH₃–CO–CO–CH₃). Drawing both Kekulé structures of o-xylene shows why all three products appear: each Kekulé form is cut at different bonds.

<ExamTip>
To work backwards from products to an alkene, write the carbonyl compounds with their C=O carbons facing each other, delete the oxygens and join the carbons with a double bond. Count carbons on both sides to check.
</ExamTip>

## Reductive versus oxidative work-up

The same ozonide gives different products depending on what is added after ozone:

| Substrate | O₃, then Zn/H₂O or (CH₃)₂S (reductive) | O₃, then H₂O/H₂O₂ (oxidative) |
|---|---|---|
| R–CH=CR′₂ | R–CHO + R′₂C=O | R–COOH + R′₂C=O |
| R–C≡C–R′ | R–CO–CO–R′ (1,2-diketone) | R–COOH + R′–COOH |
| Benzene | 3 OHC–CHO (glyoxal) | 3 HOOC–COOH (oxalic acid, in exam answers) |

Alkynes are not fully split under reductive conditions: the two carbons stay joined as a **1,2-dicarbonyl** compound. In water the alkyne ozonide hydrolyses (via the anhydride) to **two carboxylic acids**. Some books write the oxidative step as KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ instead of H₂O₂; the carboxylic acid products are the same.

<Eq ce="CH3-C#C-CH3 ->[(i) O3][(ii) Zn/H2O] CH3-CO-CO-CH3" />

<Eq ce="CH3-C#C-C2H5 ->[(i) O3][(ii) H2O] CH3COOH + C2H5COOH" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The benzene product OHC–CHO is **glyoxal** (a dialdehyde), not glycol. Glycol (ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂–CH₂OH) is an alcohol and is never formed by ozonolysis with Zn/H₂O.
</ExamTip>

## Multi-step questions

A typical chain: ozonolysis of a ring alkene with an oxidative (O₃/H₂O, no Zn) work-up gives a cyclic **β-keto acid**; on heating, the β-keto acid loses CO₂ (decarboxylation) to give **cyclohexanone**; Clemmensen reduction (Zn–Hg/HCl) then removes the C=O to give **cyclohexane**. Recognise the β-keto acid → ketone + CO₂ step whenever a C=O sits two carbons from a COOH.

In exam answers, when only **one mole** of O₃ is used on a diene, the **more electron-rich** C=C (for example one carrying an OCH₃ group) is taken as the one that is cleaved, since ozone acts as an electrophile; the other C=C survives.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Ozone and peroxide intermediates are hazardous, so the ozonide is normally not isolated; the work-up is done straight after ozone treatment.
- Sodium borohydride as the work-up reagent gives **alcohols** instead of carbonyl compounds.
- Alkynes react differently: they give a 1,2-diketone or an acid anhydride, which in water hydrolyses to two carboxylic acids.
- The end of the reaction is easy to see in the lab: the solution turns blue once excess, unreacted ozone builds up.
