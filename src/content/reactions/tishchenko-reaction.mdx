## What is the Tishchenko reaction?

The **Tishchenko reaction** converts **two molecules of an aldehyde into one ester** in the presence of an **aluminium alkoxide**, typically **aluminium ethoxide, Al(OC₂H₅)₃**. It is a disproportionation: one aldehyde carbon is oxidised to the ester (acid) level and the other is reduced to the alcohol level. Acetaldehyde gives **ethyl acetate**. The reaction is named after the Russian chemist Vyacheslav Tishchenko, who found that aluminium alkoxides make it work even for enolisable aldehydes.

## Mechanism

1. **Coordination.** Aluminium in Al(OC₂H₅)₃ is a Lewis acid. The carbonyl oxygen of one aldehyde binds to it, making that carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
2. **Two additions.** An ethoxide group on aluminium adds to the activated carbonyl. The new alkoxide oxygen then adds to the carbonyl of a second aldehyde. The result is an aluminium-bound **hemiacetal alkoxide** in which the two former aldehyde carbons are linked through one oxygen.
3. **1,3-Hydride shift.** The carbon carrying the O–Al group passes its hydrogen, as hydride, across the bridging oxygen to the other former aldehyde carbon, which releases the ethoxide. The hydride donor becomes the ester C=O; the acceptor becomes the –O–CH₂– of the ester.
4. **Release.** The ester leaves the aluminium and the alkoxide catalyst is regenerated.

<Eq ce="2 CH3CHO ->[Al(OC2H5)3] CH3-CO-O-CH2-CH3" />

<KeyPoint>
The acetyl part (CH₃CO–) comes from the aldehyde that lost a hydride (oxidised), and the ethyl part (–OCH₂CH₃) comes from the aldehyde that gained it (reduced). Both halves of an ester from a Tishchenko reaction always have the same carbon skeleton as the starting aldehyde.
</KeyPoint>

<Trick>
Shortcut (ब्रह्मास्त्र): write the acid of the aldehyde (CH₃COOH) and the alcohol of the aldehyde (CH₃CH₂OH), then join them as an ester (CH₃COOCH₂CH₃). This is only a bookkeeping trick; no free acid or alcohol is formed.
</Trick>

A side reaction can occur when one of the ethoxide groups on aluminium adds to an aldehyde instead of a second aldehyde molecule. Low temperature and small amounts of catalyst keep this to a minimum.

## Aluminium alkoxides in exam questions

Three named reactions use aluminium alkoxides and are often confused:

| Reagent | Partner | Reaction | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al(OC₂H₅)₃ | the aldehyde itself | Tishchenko reaction | 2 RCHO → RCOOCH₂R |
| Al(OCH(CH₃)₂)₃ | isopropanol | Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reduction | C=O → CH–OH |
| Aluminium tert-butoxide or isopropoxide | excess acetone | Oppenauer oxidation | CH–OH → C=O |

All three work by hydride transfer through aluminium, which is why they are taught together.

## Cannizzaro vs Tishchenko

| Feature | Cannizzaro | Tishchenko |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | Concentrated NaOH/KOH | Aluminium alkoxide (catalytic) |
| Aldehyde | No α-H | With or without α-H |
| Products | Alcohol + carboxylate salt | One ester |
| Example | 2 HCHO → CH₃OH + HCOONa | 2 CH₃CHO → CH₃COOC₂H₅ |

<ExamTip>
If the reagent is Al(OEt)₃ and the aldehyde has α-hydrogens, do not write an aldol product. Write the ester made of the aldehyde's own acid and alcohol.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Water must be excluded, since it destroys the aluminium alkoxide.
- With strong hydroxide instead of an alkoxide, enolisable aldehydes give aldol products, and non-enolisable ones give the Cannizzaro products.
