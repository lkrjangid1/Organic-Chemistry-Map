## What is the Bamberger rearrangement?

The **Bamberger rearrangement** is the acid-catalysed conversion of **N-phenylhydroxylamine (C₆H₅NHOH)** into **4-aminophenol (p-aminophenol)** in **strong aqueous acid**, usually sulphuric acid. The –OH group leaves nitrogen as water and a new –OH appears on the ring carbon **para** to the nitrogen. It is named after the German chemist **Eugen Bamberger**, who described it in 1894.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5NHOH ->[H2SO4, H2O] HO-C6H4-NH2 (para)" />

Phenylhydroxylamine is itself made from **nitrobenzene** by partial (four-electron) reduction, for example with **Zn dust and aqueous NH₄Cl**:

<Eq ce="C6H5NO2 ->[Zn, NH4Cl (aq)] C6H5NHOH ->[H2SO4, H2O] p-HOC6H4NH2" />

In exam answers the two steps are often merged: **electrolytic reduction of nitrobenzene in a strongly acidic medium** is written as giving p-aminophenol, because the phenylhydroxylamine formed at the cathode rearranges at once in the acid.

## Mechanism

1. **Protonation.** The acid can protonate nitrogen or oxygen. N-protonation is favoured but leads nowhere; the productive path starts from **O-protonation**, which turns –OH into a good leaving group (–OH₂⁺).
2. **Loss of water.** Water leaves, giving an electron-deficient **nitrenium ion**, C₆H₅NH⁺. Its positive charge is delocalised onto the ortho and para ring carbons.
3. **Attack by water.** A water molecule from the solvent attacks the **para carbon**, where the positive charge is best stabilised.
4. **Rearomatisation.** Loss of H⁺ from that carbon restores the aromatic ring and gives 4-aminophenol (as its ammonium salt in the acid).

<Eq ce="C6H5NHOH ->[H+] C6H5NH-OH2+ ->[-H2O] C6H5NH+ ->[+H2O, -H+] p-HOC6H4NH2" />

<KeyPoint>
The new ring –OH comes from the **solvent water**, not from the original N–OH group. Computational work (2013) suggests that a free nitrenium ion may not survive in water and that the step is assisted by a hydrogen-bonded water network, but the overall result is the same: substitution para to nitrogen.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Nitrobenzene to p-aminophenol (two steps).**

<Eq ce="C6H5NO2 ->[Zn/NH4Cl] C6H5NHOH ->[dil. H2SO4][\Delta] p-H2NC6H4OH" />

**2. Changing the acid changes the product.** With hydrochloric acid, chloride competes with water as the nucleophile, so **2- and 4-chloroanilines** form along with aminophenol. Sulphuric acid, whose anion is a poor nucleophile, gives 4-aminophenol cleanly.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing an intramolecular "OH shift" from N to the para carbon. The OH group is lost as water and a new OH arrives from the solvent. If a question uses HCl instead of H₂SO₄, expect chloroanilines too.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Over-reduction:** phenylhydroxylamine sits between nitrosobenzene and aniline in oxidation level, so if the reduction of nitrobenzene goes too far, **aniline** forms instead and lowers the yield.
- Phenylhydroxylamine is **unstable on heating**, so it is used soon after it is made.
- Oxidation instead of rearrangement: dichromate converts phenylhydroxylamine into **nitrosobenzene**.
