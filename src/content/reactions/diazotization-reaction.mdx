## What is the diazotization reaction?

**Diazotization** is the conversion of a **primary aromatic amine** into an **arenediazonium salt** using **nitrous acid**, which is generated in situ from **sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid** at **273–278 K (0–5 °C)**. Aniline gives **benzenediazonium chloride**, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻, the starting point for the Sandmeyer, Gattermann and Balz–Schiemann reactions and for azo dyes.

Primary **aliphatic** amines react with the same reagent, but their diazonium ions decompose instantly, releasing nitrogen gas and giving mainly alcohols.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="NaNO2 + HCl -> HNO2 + NaCl" />

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl ->[273-278 K] C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + 2H2O" />

<Eq ce="R-NH2 ->[NaNO2, HCl] [R-N2^+] -> R^+ + N2 ^ ->[H2O] R-OH" />

## Mechanism

1. **Formation of NO⁺.** Nitrous acid is protonated by the excess acid and loses water, giving the nitrosonium ion, NO⁺, the actual electrophile.
2. **N-Nitrosation.** The lone pair on the amine nitrogen attacks NO⁺. Loss of H⁺ gives an N-nitrosamine, R–NH–N=O.
3. **Tautomerism.** A proton shifts from nitrogen to oxygen, giving the diazoic acid (diazohydroxide), R–N=N–OH.
4. **Loss of water.** The OH is protonated and leaves as water, giving the diazonium ion, R–N≡N⁺.
5. **Fate of the diazonium ion.** For an aryl group the ion is stable in the cold and is used in further reactions. For an alkyl group it loses N₂ to give a carbocation R⁺, which is captured by water to give R–OH. In exam answers, this loss of N₂ is shown as the slow, rate-determining step.

<Eq ce="HNO2 + H+ -> H2O + NO+" />

<Eq ce="R-NH2 ->[NO+][-H+] R-NH-N=O <=> R-N=N-OH ->[H+][-H2O] R-N#N+" />

<KeyPoint>
Aryl diazonium ions are stabilised by resonance with the ring, and aryl cations are hard to form, so ArN₂⁺ survives at 0–5 °C. Alkyl diazonium ions have no such stabilisation and lose N₂ immediately.
</KeyPoint>

## Primary aliphatic amines: carbocation products

Because the intermediate is a **carbocation**, a primary aliphatic amine gives a mixture. The main product is the alcohol, with nitrogen gas evolved quantitatively (the basis of the Van Slyke estimation of amino groups). The carbocation can take several routes:

- R⁺ + H₂O → **R–OH** (major)
- R⁺ + R–OH → **R–O–R** (ether)
- R⁺ + ⁻O–N=O → **R–O–N=O** (alkyl nitrite)
- R⁺ + Cl⁻ → **R–Cl**
- loss of H⁺ at higher temperature → **alkene**

Carbocation **rearrangement** is common: propanamine gives about half its products from the rearranged isopropyl cation. A chiral amine gives the alcohol with both retention and inversion (racemisation), as expected for an open carbocation.

<ExamTip>
For methylamine, many JEE study materials write CH₃NH₂ + HNO₂ → CH₃–O–CH₃ (methoxymethane) rather than methanol. NCERT only states that primary aliphatic amines give alcohols with evolution of N₂, so write the alcohol unless a question specifically asks about the methylamine case.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Aniline to benzenediazonium chloride (NCERT):**

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl ->[273-278 K] C6H5N2^+ Cl^- + NaCl + 2H2O" />

**2. Ethylamine with nitrous acid:**

<Eq ce="C2H5NH2 + HNO2 -> C2H5OH + N2 ^ + H2O" />

**3. Secondary and tertiary amines:**

<Eq ce="(CH3)2NH + HNO2 -> (CH3)2N-N=O + H2O" />

<Eq ce="(CH3)3N + HNO2 -> (CH3)3NH^+ NO2^-" />

Secondary amines give yellow, water-insoluble **N-nitrosamines**; tertiary aliphatic amines only undergo an acid–base reaction. **N,N-Dimethylaniline** is attacked on the ring by NO⁺ at the para position, giving p-nitroso-N,N-dimethylaniline; HNO₃ then oxidises the –N=O group to –NO₂.

## Limitations and exceptions

- The reaction must be kept cold; above about 283 K the aryl diazonium salt is hydrolysed to phenol.
- Dry benzenediazonium chloride can decompose explosively, so it is used in solution straight away. The tetrafluoroborate salt is stable enough to isolate.
- Aliphatic diazotization has little synthetic use because of mixed and rearranged products. A useful exception is ring expansion of aminomethylcycloalkanes ([Demjanov rearrangement](/reactions/demjanov-rearrangement/)).
