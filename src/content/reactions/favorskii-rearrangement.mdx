## What is the Favorskii rearrangement?

The **Favorskii rearrangement** is the conversion of an **α-halo ketone** into a **carboxylic acid derivative** by a base. With **hydroxide** the product is a **carboxylic acid** (as its salt until acidified); with an **alkoxide** such as CH₃O⁻ or C₂H₅O⁻ it is an **ester**. The reaction goes through a strained **cyclopropanone** intermediate, and when the α-halo ketone is cyclic the ring **contracts by one carbon**. It was discovered by the Russian chemist Alexei Favorskii in 1894.

Essential condition: one α-carbon carries a **halogen** and the other α-carbon (α′) carries at least **one hydrogen**.

## Mechanism

Chloroacetone with hydroxide shows every step:

1. **Enolate formation.** OH⁻ removes an α′-hydrogen from the CH₃ group, on the side of the C=O away from chlorine.
2. **Cyclopropanone formation.** The enolate carbon attacks the carbon bearing Cl and pushes out Cl⁻ in an intramolecular SN2 reaction. A three-membered ring with the C=O at one corner (cyclopropanone) is formed.
3. **Nucleophilic addition.** Because of ring strain the cyclopropanone C=O is very reactive. A second OH⁻ adds to it, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide.
4. **Ring opening.** The alkoxide re-forms C=O and one ring C–C bond breaks, releasing the **more stable carbanion**.
5. **Protonation.** The carbanion picks up a proton, giving the carboxylic acid, which stays as the carboxylate in base until acid work-up.

<Eq ce="CH3COCH2Cl + OH- -> -CH2COCH2Cl + H2O" />
<Eq ce="-CH2COCH2Cl -> (CH2)2CO + Cl-" />
<Eq ce="(CH2)2CO + OH- -> CH3CH2COO- ->[H3O+] CH3CH2COOH" />

<KeyPoint>
When the cyclopropanone opens, the bond that breaks is the one giving the more stable carbanion: **benzylic > primary > secondary > tertiary**. This decides the product from unsymmetrical substrates, and labelling experiments confirm that both ends of the ring can open.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Acid with hydroxide:**

<Eq ce="CH3COCH2Cl ->[1. NaOH][2. H3O+] CH3CH2COOH" />

**Ester with an alkoxide (ring contraction):** 2-chlorocyclohexanone gives methyl cyclopentanecarboxylate.

<Eq ce="C6H9ClO ->[CH3ONa][CH3OH] C5H9COOCH3" />

With aqueous NaOH the same ketone gives cyclopentanecarboxylic acid. Ring contraction is the most common synthetic use of the reaction; it was used twice in the classic synthesis of cubane.

**α,α′-Dihalo ketones give α,β-unsaturated acids.** In 1,3-dichlorobutan-2-one, the cyclopropanone still carries one chlorine. When the ring opens, the carbanion expels that chloride, forming a C=C bond conjugated with the carboxyl group:

<Eq ce="ClCH2COCHClCH3 ->[1. OH-][2. H3O+] CH3CH=CHCOOH" />

## When there is no α′-hydrogen

- **Quasi-Favorskii (semibenzilic) rearrangement.** If no enolate can form on the far side, a cyclopropanone is impossible. Under forcing conditions the base adds directly to the C=O, and a group on the carbonyl carbon migrates to the halogen-bearing carbon while the halide leaves, just like the [benzilic acid rearrangement](/reactions/benzilic-acid-rearrangement/). C₆H₅COCHClCH₃ gives 2-phenylpropanoic acid, CH₃CH(C₆H₅)COOH, with the phenyl group migrating.
- **Simple substitution.** For a substrate such as C₆H₅C(CH₃)₂COCH₂Cl, whose far α-carbon is quaternary, exam answers usually show OH⁻ simply replacing the reactive primary chloride (SN2) to give C₆H₅C(CH₃)₂COCH₂OH.

<ExamTip>
First check the α′-carbon. If it has a hydrogen, draw the cyclopropanone and open it to the more stable carbanion. If it has none, think of the semibenzilic shift or, for a primary halide, plain SN2 substitution.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- α,α,α-Trihalomethyl ketones undergo the [haloform reaction](/reactions/haloform-reaction/) instead.
- It should not be confused with the Favorskii *reaction*, the addition of a terminal alkyne to a ketone.
