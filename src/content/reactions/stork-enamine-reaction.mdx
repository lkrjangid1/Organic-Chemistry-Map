## What is the Stork enamine reaction?

The **Stork enamine reaction** is a three-step method for putting a new group on the **α-carbon of a ketone (or aldehyde)**. The ketone is first converted into an **enamine** with a **secondary amine** such as pyrrolidine. The enamine's nucleophilic α-carbon then reacts with an electrophile, most reliably a **Michael acceptor**, giving an iminium ion, and **dilute aqueous acid** hydrolyses this back to a ketone. It is named after Gilbert Stork of Columbia University.

## Step 1: enamine formation

A secondary amine adds to the carbonyl group, and the carbinolamine formed then loses water. Because nitrogen has no hydrogen left, the proton is removed from the **α-carbon**, giving a C=C bonded to nitrogen.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C=O + HNR2 <=> (CH3)2C(OH)-NR2 ->[-H2O] CH3-C(NR2)=CH2" />

1. **Addition.** The nitrogen lone pair attacks the C=O carbon; proton transfer gives the **carbinolamine** (CH₃)₂C(OH)–NR₂.
2. **Loss of water.** The OH (after protonation) leaves, giving an iminium ion (CH₃)₂C=N⁺R₂.
3. **Loss of an α-H.** A base (another amine molecule) removes a proton from a CH₃ group, giving the enamine CH₃–C(NR₂)=CH₂.

Acid catalysis is used when the amine is not very basic; with strongly basic amines such as pyrrolidine it is often not needed. Removing the water (for example with a drying agent) pushes the equilibrium toward the enamine.

<KeyPoint>
Primary amines (RNH₂) give **imines** (C=N–R); secondary amines (R₂NH) give **enamines** (C=C–NR₂). Tertiary amines have no N–H and do not condense with C=O at all.
</KeyPoint>

## Why enamines are nucleophilic at carbon

The nitrogen lone pair overlaps with the C=C π bond, so electron density moves onto the α-carbon:

<Eq ce="R2N-C=CH2 <-> R2N^+=C-CH2^-" />

Enamines therefore behave like neutral enolates. They are easier to make and handle than enolate ions, and an enamine from a simple monoketone works well as a Michael donor, whereas only β-dicarbonyl enolates give good Michael reactions.

## Steps 2 and 3: reaction with an electrophile, then hydrolysis

**Michael addition (most reliable).** Cyclohexanone and pyrrolidine give the enamine; it adds to but-3-en-2-one (methyl vinyl ketone) at the β-carbon; hydrolysis gives a **1,5-diketone**.

<Eq ce="\text{cyclohexanone enamine} + CH2=CHCOCH3 -> \text{iminium adduct} ->[H3O+] \text{2-(3-oxobutyl)cyclohexanone}" />

**Alkylation.** Reactive halides, such as benzylic, allylic and α-halo carbonyl halides, alkylate the enamine carbon; hydrolysis gives the 2-alkylated ketone.

<Eq ce="R2N-CH=CHR' + R''X -> [R2N^+=CH-CHR'R'']X^- ->[H2O] R'R''CH-CHO + R2NH" />

**Acylation.** Acyl chlorides react at carbon, and hydrolysis gives a **1,3-diketone** (Stork acylation).

<ExamTip>
For the product, ignore the amine: it goes in and comes out. The net change is that the electrophile is attached to the **α-carbon** of the original ketone. With an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl acceptor, the answer is always a 1,5-dicarbonyl compound.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Enamine from acetone.** Acetone with a secondary amine gives CH₃–C(NR₂)=CH₂ (2-dialkylaminopropene).

**2. Cyclopentanone enamine with ethyl propenoate.** After hydrolysis the product is ethyl 3-(2-oxocyclopentyl)propanoate.

**3. Cyclohexanone enamine with methyl vinyl ketone.** Gives 2-(3-oxobutyl)cyclohexanone, the same 1,5-diketone that a base-catalysed Robinson annulation passes through.

## Limitations

- Enamines are also nucleophilic at **nitrogen**. Simple primary alkyl halides such as CH₃I often attack N first, so they give only low to moderate yields of the C-alkylated product.
- The method works best with **Michael acceptors** and very reactive halides.
- Water must be removed during enamine formation; the enamine is hydrolysed easily by aqueous acid.
