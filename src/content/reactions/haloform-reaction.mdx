## What is the haloform reaction?

The **haloform reaction** is the reaction of a **methyl ketone (R–CO–CH₃) or acetaldehyde with a halogen (Cl₂, Br₂ or I₂) in aqueous NaOH**. All three hydrogens of the CH₃ group are replaced by halogen, and the CX₃ group is then cleaved off by hydroxide. The products are a **haloform, CHX₃**, and the **sodium salt of a carboxylic acid with one carbon atom fewer** than the starting carbonyl compound. With iodine the reaction is the **iodoform test**, which gives a yellow precipitate of CHI₃.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-CO-CH3 + 3 X2 + 4 NaOH -> R-COONa + CHX3 + 3 NaX + 3 H2O" />

The halogen and NaOH together form sodium hypohalite, which is the actual reagent:

<Eq ce="X2 + 2 NaOH -> NaOX + NaX + H2O" />

So NCERT describes the reaction as oxidation of methyl ketones by sodium hypohalite. A C=C double bond elsewhere in the molecule is not affected.

## Mechanism

1. **Enolate formation (slow).** OH⁻ removes an α-hydrogen from the CH₃ group to give an enolate ion, R–CO–CH₂⁻ ↔ R–C(O⁻)=CH₂. This is the rate-determining step.
2. **Halogenation.** The enolate attacks the halogen (or hypohalite), giving R–CO–CH₂X.
3. **Repeat twice.** Each halogen makes the remaining α-hydrogens more acidic, so the second and third halogenations are faster. The CH₃ group becomes CX₃: R–CO–CX₃.
4. **Cleavage.** OH⁻ adds to the carbonyl carbon. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses and expels ⁻CX₃, which is a reasonable leaving group because three electronegative halogens stabilise the negative charge.
5. **Proton transfer.** ⁻CX₃ takes a proton from the carboxylic acid, giving CHX₃ and the carboxylate ion.

<Eq ce="R-CO-CH3 ->[3 X2, OH-] R-CO-CX3 ->[OH-] R-COO- + CHX3" />

<KeyPoint>
Only the CH₃ attached directly to the carbonyl group is halogenated completely, because only those hydrogens are acidic enough. That CH₃ carbon becomes the carbon of CHX₃, so the product acid always has one carbon fewer than the starting compound.
</KeyPoint>

## Kinetics: why the halogen does not matter

Kinetic studies on acetone show that, in base, the rate of halogenation is **rate = k[ketone][OH⁻]**. The halogen does not appear in the rate law: the reaction goes at the same rate whether chlorine, bromine or iodine is used, and even at very low halogen concentration. The slow step is therefore **formation of the enolate ion**; once formed, the enolate reacts with the halogen very quickly. Each halogen added makes the remaining α-hydrogens more acidic, so the second and third substitutions are faster still, and base-promoted halogenation of a methyl ketone cannot be stopped at the monohalo stage.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing the reactivity order as F₂ = Cl₂ = Br₂ = I₂. The rate is the same for **Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂** because none of them takes part in the slow step, but fluorine is not used at all: the haloform reaction needs the hypohalite ion, and hypofluorite is too unstable, so fluoroform cannot be made this way.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**Acetone with iodine and NaOH** (iodoform test):

<Eq ce="CH3COCH3 + 3 I2 + 4 NaOH -> CH3COONa + CHI3 v + 3 NaI + 3 H2O" />

**Acetophenone with bromine and NaOH** (an example of the general R–CO–CH₃ + NaOH/Br₂ case):

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 ->[Br2, NaOH] C6H5COONa + CHBr3" />

**Ethanol** is first oxidised to acetaldehyde by the hypoiodite, which then gives iodoform:

<Eq ce="CH3CH2OH ->[I2, NaOH] CH3CHO ->[I2, NaOH] HCOONa + CHI3 v" />

## Which compounds respond?

- **Positive:** methyl ketones (CH₃CO–R), acetaldehyde (the only aldehyde), ethanol (the only primary alcohol), secondary alcohols of the type CH₃CH(OH)–R such as propan-2-ol and butan-2-ol, and 1,3-diketones such as acetylacetone.
- **Negative:** methanol, formaldehyde, other aldehydes, ketones without a CH₃CO group (for example pentan-3-one, benzophenone), acetyl chloride and acetamide.

<ExamTip>
For "distinguish by a chemical test" questions, look for CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)–. If one compound has it and the other does not, the iodoform test (I₂/NaOH, yellow precipitate) is the answer. Example: ethanol (positive) vs methanol (negative); pentan-2-one (positive) vs pentan-3-one (negative).
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Fluoroform cannot be made** this way, because the hypofluorite ion is too unstable.
- **Primary amides with Br₂/NaOH** do not give a haloform; they undergo the Hofmann bromamide degradation to amines instead. Keep the two reactions separate: R–CO–NH₂ gives R–NH₂, while R–CO–CH₃ gives R–COO⁻ and CHBr₃.
- β-Keto acids such as acetoacetic acid also give the test on heating, after losing CO₂ to form a methyl ketone.
