## What is the E1 reaction?

The **E1 reaction** (elimination, unimolecular) is a two-step β-elimination in which the leaving group departs first to give a **carbocation**, and a weak base then removes a proton from the neighbouring (β) carbon to form a C=C bond. It converts tertiary or secondary alkyl halides, sulphonates and protonated alcohols into **alkenes**, usually in a polar protic solvent with heat and with little or no strong base.

Because the slow step involves only the substrate, the rate law is **rate = k[RX]**, which is why the reaction is called unimolecular.

## Mechanism

1. **Ionisation (slow, rate-determining).** The C–X bond breaks heterolytically. The halide leaves with the bonding pair and a planar carbocation forms. This is the same first step as in SN1.
2. **Deprotonation (fast).** A weak base, often just the solvent (water, ethanol) or the counter-anion, removes an H⁺ from a carbon next to the positive centre. The C–H electron pair becomes the new π bond.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-Br <=>[slow] (CH3)3C+ + Br-" />
<Eq ce="(CH3)3C+ + C2H5OH -> CH2=C(CH3)2 + C2H5OH2+" />

<KeyPoint>
The C–H bond breaks only after the rate-determining step. So E1 shows first-order kinetics and no significant primary deuterium isotope effect, and the H and leaving group do not need to be anti-periplanar.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane in hot ethanol.** The tertiary cation can lose a proton from the CH₂ or from a CH₃ group. Loss from CH₂ gives the trisubstituted alkene, which is the major (Saytzeff) product.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2CBr-CH2CH3 ->[C2H5OH][\Delta] (CH3)2C=CHCH3 (major) + CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3 (minor)" />

**2. tert-Butyl chloride in aqueous ethanol.** Warming 2-chloro-2-methylpropane in 80% aqueous ethanol gives both 2-methylpropan-2-ol (SN1) and 2-methylpropene (E1), showing that the two pathways share one intermediate.

**3. Acid-catalysed dehydration of a tertiary alcohol.** Protonation turns –OH into –OH₂⁺, water leaves to form a tertiary cation, and loss of H⁺ gives the alkene. See [dehydration of alcohols](/reactions/dehydration-of-alcohols/) for the full treatment.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3 ->[conc. H2SO4][\Delta] (CH3)2C=CHCH3 + H2O" />

## Features to remember

- **Substrate:** 3° greater than 2°; 1° and methyl substrates do not react by E1 because their cations are too unstable.
- **Base:** weak base or none. The base is not in the rate law, so its strength or concentration does not change the rate.
- **Solvent:** polar protic (water, alcohols) to stabilise the ion pair.
- **Leaving group:** good leaving groups (I⁻, Br⁻, TsO⁻, H₂O from a protonated alcohol).
- **Regiochemistry:** the more substituted alkene usually dominates (Saytzeff rule); the more stable *trans* alkene is favoured where cis/trans isomers are possible.
- **Rearrangement:** possible, because a free carbocation is formed. A hydride or alkyl shift to a more stable cation happens before deprotonation.

<ExamTip>
If a question gives a 3° halide or alcohol with a weak base, a protic solvent and heat, think E1 (with SN1 competing). If it gives a strong base such as alcoholic KOH or NaOEt, think E2. Always check whether the carbocation can rearrange before choosing the final alkene.
</ExamTip>

## E1 versus SN1

E1 and SN1 compete whenever a substrate ionises in a protic solvent with a non-basic nucleophile. Raising the temperature favours elimination, because elimination produces more molecules and is helped by the entropy term. In exam questions, "heat" with a 3° substrate and weak base usually points to the alkene.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Mixtures are common: SN1 products, alkene isomers and rearranged alkenes may all form.
- No stereospecificity: since the planar cation forgets the original geometry, E1 does not give a single alkene stereoisomer from a given diastereomer, unlike E2.
- In cyclic systems E1 can give the Saytzeff alkene even when no anti-periplanar H is available; E2 under the same conditions may give a different (non-Saytzeff) alkene.
