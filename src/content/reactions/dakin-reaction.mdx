## What is the Dakin reaction?

The **Dakin reaction (Dakin oxidation)** converts an **ortho- or para-hydroxy (or amino) aryl aldehyde or ketone** into a **phenol** using **hydrogen peroxide in aqueous base**. The –CHO or –COR group is replaced by **–OH**, and the carbonyl carbon leaves as a **carboxylate**. So **salicylaldehyde gives catechol**, and **4-hydroxybenzaldehyde gives hydroquinone**. It is named after Henry Drysdale Dakin and is closely related to the [Baeyer–Villiger oxidation](/reactions/baeyer-villiger-oxidation/).

## General reaction

<Eq ce="HO-C6H4-CO-R + H2O2 + OH- -> HO-C6H4-OH + R-COO- + H2O" />

R = H (aldehyde, gives formate) or alkyl (ketone, gives acetate etc.). The activating group (OH or NH₂) must be **ortho or para** to the carbonyl.

## Mechanism

1. **Hydroperoxide ion forms.** OH⁻ removes a proton from H₂O₂, giving HOO⁻, a much stronger nucleophile than H₂O₂.
2. **Nucleophilic addition.** HOO⁻ adds to the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral intermediate, Ar–C(O⁻)(R)–O–OH.
3. **[1,2]-Aryl migration.** The intermediate collapses: the C=O reforms, the aryl group moves from carbon to the neighbouring oxygen, and OH⁻ leaves from the O–O bond. The product is an **aryl ester**, R–CO–O–Ar.
4. **Ester hydrolysis.** OH⁻ attacks the ester carbonyl, and the tetrahedral intermediate expels the **phenoxide ion**.
5. **Proton transfer.** The phenoxide takes the acidic proton of the carboxylic acid, giving the phenoxide and carboxylate. Acid work-up gives the free benzenediol.

<Eq ce="Ar-CHO ->[HOO-] Ar-CH(O-)-O-OH ->[\text{aryl shift}][- OH-] H-CO-O-Ar ->[OH-] Ar-O- + HCOOH" />

<KeyPoint>
The two slow steps are the **addition of HOO⁻** and the **aryl migration**. An OH or NH₂ group ortho or para to the carbonyl feeds electron density to the migrating ring carbon, so the aryl group moves instead of the hydrogen. This is why only o- and p-hydroxy/amino carbonyl compounds give phenols.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Salicylaldehyde → catechol:**

<Eq ce="2-HOC6H4CHO ->[H2O2, NaOH] 1,2-C6H4(OH)2 + HCOO-" />

**2. 4-Hydroxybenzaldehyde → hydroquinone:**

<Eq ce="4-HOC6H4CHO ->[H2O2, NaOH] 1,4-C6H4(OH)2 + HCOO-" />

**3. 4-Hydroxyacetophenone → hydroquinone + acetate:** ketones react more slowly than aldehydes, but warming compensates.

<Eq ce="4-HOC6H4COCH3 ->[H2O2, OH-] 1,4-C6H4(OH)2 + CH3COO-" />

**4. 4-Aminoacetophenone → 4-aminophenol:** an NH₂ group activates the ring in the same way as OH.

<Eq ce="4-H2NC6H4COCH3 ->[H2O2, OH-] 4-H2NC6H4OH + CH3COO-" />

## Factors that control the reaction

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Aldehyde vs ketone | Aldehydes react faster (more electrophilic C=O) |
| OH ortho vs para | Ortho reacts faster in weak base (internal H-bond activates C=O) |
| OH meta | Hydrogen migrates instead; gives a hydroxybenzoic acid, not a diol |
| EWG (e.g. NO₂) ortho/para | Slows aryl migration; acid product favoured |
| pH | Faster as more HOO⁻ forms; stops above about pH 13.5 |

<ExamTip>
Treat it as a Baeyer–Villiger on an activated aromatic carbonyl: O is inserted between the ring and the carbonyl carbon, then base cuts the aryl ester. Product = ring with OH where the carbonyl group was; by-product = RCOO⁻. Check that the activating group is ortho or para; if it is meta, expect the carboxylic acid instead.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and variants

- m-Hydroxy and electron-poor aryl carbonyls give carboxylic acids instead of phenols.
- Very strong base (above about pH 13.5) stops the reaction, probably because the doubly deprotonated peroxide cannot lose O²⁻.
- **Acid-catalysed version:** H₂O₂ with a little H₂SO₄ in methanol works by the same aryl-shift pathway; adding boric acid raises the phenol yield.
- Catalysts such as methyltrioxorhenium in ionic liquids, or the solid urea–H₂O₂ complex, speed up the oxidation.
