## What is the Wöhler synthesis?

The **Wöhler synthesis** is the conversion of **ammonium cyanate (NH₄OCN) into urea ((NH₂)₂CO)** by heating. Friedrich Wöhler described it in **1828**. In the usual school demonstration, solutions of **potassium cyanate** and **ammonium chloride** are mixed and warmed: ammonium cyanate forms by double decomposition and then rearranges to urea. Because both compounds have the formula CH₄N₂O, the reaction is an **isomerisation** of an inorganic salt into an organic molecule.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="KCNO + NH4Cl -> NH4CNO + KCl" />

<Eq ce="NH4CNO ->[\Delta] NH2-CO-NH2" />

Ammonium cyanate is only a short-lived intermediate; it is never isolated in the demonstration.

## Mechanism

1. **Salt exchange.** K⁺OCN⁻ and NH₄⁺Cl⁻ swap partners in water, giving NH₄⁺OCN⁻ (and KCl).
2. **Proton transfer.** On heating, the ammonium ion passes a proton to the cyanate ion, so the salt is in equilibrium with free **ammonia** and **cyanic acid** (HOCN, present largely as isocyanic acid, HN=C=O).
3. **Addition of ammonia.** The lone pair on NH₃ attacks the electron-poor carbon of HN=C=O. A proton shift from nitrogen to nitrogen then gives the neutral urea molecule, H₂N–CO–NH₂.

<Eq ce="NH4CNO <=> NH3 + HNCO -> NH2CONH2" />

<KeyPoint>
The last step lies far on the urea side, so heating drives the salt almost completely to urea. Adding **oxalic acid** precipitates white **urea oxalate**, which is a quick proof that urea has formed.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. The classroom version (KCNO + NH₄Cl).** Equimolar aqueous solutions are mixed, heated and cooled again; urea is present in the solution.

<Eq ce="KCNO + NH4Cl ->[\Delta] NH2CONH2 + KCl" />

**2. Wöhler's own variants.** In the 1828 paper he obtained urea from several pairs: cyanic acid with ammonia, silver cyanate with ammonium chloride, and lead cyanate with ammonia. With lead cyanate the first step is again a double displacement that produces ammonium cyanate.

<Eq ce="Pb(OCN)2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O -> Pb(OH)2 + 2NH4OCN" />

<ExamTip>
If a question asks "the first organic compound synthesised in the laboratory from inorganic material", the answer is **urea**, by Wöhler, from **ammonium cyanate**, in 1828. Remember that ammonium cyanate and urea are **isomers** (CH₄N₂O).
</ExamTip>

## Why it matters

- It showed that **urea**, a compound found in urine, could be made outside a living body from inorganic salts.
- Berzelius used the result to accept that NH₄OCN and (NH₂)₂CO are different compounds with the same formula, an early example of **isomerism**.
- Textbooks often say the synthesis "destroyed" the **vital force theory**. Historians describe a slower change: support for vitalism declined over the following decades, helped by later syntheses such as Kolbe's preparation of acetic acid from inorganic carbon compounds in 1845.

## Limitations and exceptions

- The reaction only interconverts isomers; it is not a general route to other organic compounds.
- Urea made industrially today comes from **ammonia and carbon dioxide** at high pressure, not from cyanates.
- Ammonium cyanate cannot be stored: in solution or on warming it turns into urea.
