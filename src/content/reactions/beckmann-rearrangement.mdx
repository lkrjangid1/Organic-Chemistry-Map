## What is the Beckmann rearrangement?

The **Beckmann rearrangement** is the acid-promoted conversion of a **ketoxime (R₁R₂C=N–OH) into an N-substituted amide (R₁–CO–NH–R₂)**. It is carried out with a protic acid such as concentrated H₂SO₄ or polyphosphoric acid, or with reagents such as PCl₅, SOCl₂ or TsCl, usually on heating. The group **anti to the OH** moves from carbon to nitrogen. Cyclic ketoximes give **lactams**; cyclohexanone oxime gives ε-caprolactam, the monomer of nylon-6. The reaction is named after Ernst Otto Beckmann (1886).

## Mechanism

1. **Activation of OH.** The acid protonates the oxime OH (or PCl₅/TsCl converts it into a better leaving group), so it can leave as water.
2. **Concerted anti migration.** The group anti-periplanar to the leaving group moves from carbon to nitrogen **at the same moment** as the N–O bond breaks. No free nitrene forms. The product is a **nitrilium ion**, R₁–C≡N⁺–R₂.
3. **Attack of water.** Water adds to the nitrilium carbon; loss of H⁺ gives an imidic acid (iminol), R₁–C(OH)=N–R₂.
4. **Tautomerisation.** The iminol tautomerises to the stable amide R₁–CO–NH–R₂.

<Eq ce="R1R2C=N-OH ->[H+] R1R2C=N-OH2+ ->[-H2O][\text{anti }R2\text{ shift}] R1-C#N+-R2 ->[H2O][-H+] R1-C(OH)=N-R2 -> R1-CO-NH-R2" />

<KeyPoint>
The rearrangement is **stereospecific**: the group anti to the leaving OH always migrates, and it keeps its own configuration. Because the shift happens as the N–O bond breaks, oxime geometry (E or Z), not migratory aptitude, decides the product.
</KeyPoint>

## Why the anti group migrates

Oximes do not easily flip between their E and Z forms because the C=N bond has a high barrier to inversion. The migrating bond must line up behind the departing N–O bond (anti-periplanar), just as in a backside attack, so only the group trans to OH is in the right position. This is why an unsymmetrical ketone can give two different amides, one from each oxime isomer.

## Examples

1. **Cyclohexanone oxime → ε-caprolactam (nylon-6 monomer):** a ring carbon migrates, so the ring grows from six to seven atoms with N inside it. Industrially, conc. H₂SO₄ (oleum) is used and the acid is later neutralised with ammonia, giving ammonium sulphate as a by-product.

<Eq ce="\text{cyclohexanone oxime} ->[conc. H2SO4][\Delta] \text{ε-caprolactam}" />

2. **Acetophenone oxime → acetanilide:** in the usual (E) oxime the phenyl group is anti to OH, so phenyl moves to nitrogen.

<Eq ce="C6H5C(CH3)=N-OH ->[H+][\Delta] CH3-CO-NH-C6H5" />

3. **Benzophenone oxime → benzanilide:**

<Eq ce="(C6H5)2C=N-OH ->[PCl5] C6H5-CO-NH-C6H5" />

<ExamTip>
Draw the oxime with its geometry, find the group trans to OH, and put that group on nitrogen. The other group stays on the carbonyl carbon. For a cyclic ketoxime, insert NH into the ring next to C=O to get the lactam.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Oxime isomerisation:** some acidic conditions interconvert the E and Z oximes, so both possible amides form.
- **Aldoximes** (RCH=NOH) seldom give clean primary amides; fragmentation to the nitrile commonly competes.
- **Beckmann fragmentation:** if the group α to the oxime carbon can stabilise a carbocation (for example a quaternary carbon or a carbon next to O or N), the molecule splits into a nitrile and a carbocation instead of rearranging.
- The nitrilium ion can be trapped by nucleophiles other than water.
