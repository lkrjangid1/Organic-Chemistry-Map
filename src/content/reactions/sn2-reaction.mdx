## What is the SN2 reaction?

The **SN2 reaction** (substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular) is a **single-step** nucleophilic substitution in which the nucleophile bonds to an sp³ carbon from the side opposite the leaving group while the leaving group departs. There is no intermediate, only one transition state, and a chiral carbon ends up with **inverted configuration** (Walden inversion). It works best with **methyl and primary halides**, a **strong nucleophile** and a **polar aprotic solvent**, and follows **rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻]**.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="Nu- + R-CH2-X -> [Nu^{\delta-}...CH2R...X^{\delta-}]^{\ddagger} -> Nu-CH2-R + X-" />

## Mechanism

1. **Backside approach.** The nucleophile's filled orbital (HOMO) overlaps with the back lobe of the empty C–X σ* orbital (LUMO), 180° away from the leaving group.
2. **Transition state (rate-determining).** Carbon is partly bonded to both groups. It is five-coordinate and roughly sp² hybridised, with the three other groups lying in one plane and partial negative charge on Nu and X.
3. **Inversion.** The C–X bond breaks completely, X⁻ leaves, and the three groups snap over to the other side, giving an inverted sp³ carbon.

<KeyPoint>
SN2 is a **one-step** reaction, rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻], and **inversion takes place**. It needs a **strong (negatively charged) nucleophile**, a **good leaving group** and a **polar aprotic solvent**. अभिक्रियाशीलता ∝ संक्रमण अवस्था का स्थायित्व ∝ 1/steric hindrance (reactivity ∝ stability of the transition state ∝ 1/steric hindrance).
</KeyPoint>

## Order of reactivity

NCERT gives **CH₃X > 1° > 2° > 3°**, because bulky groups block the back of the carbon. Substrates next to a π system react faster, because conjugation stabilises the transition state, and electron-withdrawing groups next to the reaction centre also favour SN2.

An extended order often used in exams:

R–CO–CH₂–X > R–O–CH₂–X > C₆H₅CH₂–X (benzyl) > CH₂=CH–CH₂–X (allyl) > 1° > 2°, with **3°, aryl and bridgehead halides showing no SN2**. Aryl halides fail because the C–X bond lies in the ring plane and the back side is blocked by the ring; bridgehead carbons cannot invert.

## Examples

**1. Hydroxide and methyl chloride (NCERT).** The classic second-order case.

<Eq ce="CH3Cl + OH- -> CH3OH + Cl-" />

**2. Inversion at a stereocentre.** (R)-2-Bromobutane with sodium azide in DMF gives (S)-2-azidobutane. Azide is a strong nucleophile but a weak base, so elimination is minor.

<Eq ce="(R)-CH3CH(Br)C2H5 + N3- ->[DMF] (S)-CH3CH(N3)C2H5 + Br-" />

**3. Tosylate to bromide.** (S)-Butan-2-yl tosylate with NaBr in acetone gives (R)-2-bromobutane; the tosylate is an excellent leaving group.

<Eq ce="(S)-CH3CH(OTs)C2H5 + NaBr ->[acetone] (R)-CH3CH(Br)C2H5 + NaOTs" />

## Ambident nucleophiles

Cyanide and nitrite each have two atoms that can donate an electron pair. NCERT gives the standard results:

| Reagent | Attacking atom | Product |
|---|---|---|
| KCN / NaCN | C | Alkyl cyanide (nitrile) R–C≡N |
| AgCN | N | Alkyl isocyanide R–N≡C |
| KNO₂ / NaNO₂ | O | Alkyl nitrite R–O–N=O |
| AgNO₂ | N | Nitroalkane R–NO₂ |

The ionic salts give free anions, while the silver salts are covalent and attack through the other atom. Wikipedia's article on nitro compounds also says that alkali nitrites mostly give nitrite esters, while silver nitrite with primary halides is the classic route to nitroalkanes. In exam answers the NaCN reaction is marked SN2 and the AgCN reaction SN1-like: Ag⁺ is said to pull off the halide and give carbocation character.

<ExamTip>
For "KCN vs AgCN" or "KNO₂ vs AgNO₂" questions, remember: ionic salt → C-attack (or O-attack for nitrite); silver salt → N-attack. Write the product, then name it carefully (nitrile vs isocyanide, nitrite vs nitro).
</ExamTip>

## Hydrolysis of alkyl halides

Hydrolysis with water in aqueous acetone or ethanol is a weak-nucleophile, protic-solvent reaction, so for tertiary halides it goes by SN1 and its rate follows carbocation stability. With hydroxide ion and a primary halide, the same overall conversion R–X → R–OH goes by SN2. Choosing between them depends on the substrate and nucleophile, not on the product.

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Tertiary, aryl, vinyl and bridgehead halides** do not react by SN2.
- **Neopentyl halides** are primary but extremely slow, because the bulky tert-butyl group blocks the back side.
- **Strong, bulky bases** (tert-butoxide) give E2 elimination instead of substitution.
