## What is the Wurtz–Fittig reaction?

The **Wurtz–Fittig reaction** is the coupling of an **aryl halide** with an **alkyl halide** using **sodium metal in dry ether**, giving an **alkylarene** (alkylbenzene). It is a way of putting an alkyl group onto a benzene ring that already carries a halogen: for example, **bromobenzene + bromomethane → toluene**. Fittig extended Wurtz's alkyl coupling to mixed aryl–alkyl pairs, so the reaction carries both names.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="Ar-X + R-X + 2 Na ->[dry ether] Ar-R + 2 NaX" />

In short: Ph–X + R–X → Ph–R with Na/ether.

## Mechanism

As with the Wurtz reaction, the mechanism has not been studied by modern methods. Exam answers use one of two pictures.

**Organosodium (ionic) pathway**, the one favoured by the Organic Chemistry Portal:

1. The **more reactive alkyl halide** reacts with sodium first, forming an alkylsodium, R⁻Na⁺.
2. This carbanion-like nucleophile attacks the aryl halide, displacing X⁻ and forming the Ar–R bond.

<Eq ce="R-X + 2 Na -> R^-Na+ + NaX" />
<Eq ce="R^-Na+ + Ar-X -> Ar-R + NaX" />

**Radical pathway:**

1. Sodium transfers electrons to both halides, giving an alkyl radical R• and an aryl radical Ar•.
2. The two radicals combine to give Ar–R.

Radicals explain why the self-coupled products appear too: R• + R• → R–R, and Ar• + Ar• → Ar–Ar.

<KeyPoint>
The cross product is favoured when the two halides differ in reactivity. Alkyl halides react with sodium faster than aryl halides, which is summed up as "Reactivity: Wurtz &gt; Fittig". Using halogens from different periods (for example ArCl with RBr) increases the difference.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Toluene from bromobenzene and bromomethane**:

<Eq ce="C6H5Br + CH3Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] C6H5CH3 + 2 NaBr" />

2. **Ethylbenzene from bromobenzene and bromoethane**:

<Eq ce="C6H5Br + C2H5Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] C6H5C2H5 + 2 NaBr" />

3. **n-Butylbenzene from chlorobenzene and 1-bromobutane**, where the halogen difference helps the cross-coupling:

<Eq ce="C6H5Cl + CH3(CH2)3Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] C6H5(CH2)3CH3 + NaCl + NaBr" />

## Side products

Three couplings can occur in the same flask:

| Coupling | Product (from C₆H₅Br + CH₃Br) | Name |
|---|---|---|
| Ar + R (wanted) | toluene | Wurtz–Fittig |
| R + R | ethane | Wurtz |
| Ar + Ar | biphenyl | Fittig |

An excess of alkyl halide and sodium can be used when the symmetrical side products are easy to separate. Here ethane is a gas and biphenyl is a high-boiling solid, so toluene is easily isolated.

<ExamTip>
If a question gives one aryl halide and one alkyl halide with Na/dry ether and asks for all products, write three: Ar–R (major), R–R and Ar–Ar. If it asks only for the main product, write the alkylbenzene.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **For JEE/NEET**, the reaction is a clean route to alkylbenzenes. **In practice**, side reactions (rearrangement, elimination) limit its use, and yields are moderate.
- **Tertiary alkyl halides** tend to eliminate rather than couple.
- Ring substituents that react with sodium (OH, NO₂, C=O, COOH) are not tolerated.
- The reaction also works with other metals (K, Li, Fe, Cu); with lithium it needs ultrasound to give a useful yield.
- Unlike Friedel–Crafts alkylation, the alkyl group is introduced exactly where the halogen was on the ring, so the position of substitution is fixed by the starting aryl halide.
