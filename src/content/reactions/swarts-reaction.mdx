## What is the Swarts reaction?

The **Swarts reaction** is the preparation of **alkyl fluorides** by **heating an alkyl chloride or alkyl bromide with a metallic fluoride** such as **AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₂ or SbF₃**. The chlorine or bromine atom is exchanged for fluorine. It is the standard NCERT method for making fluoroalkanes, because direct fluorination of alkanes is explosive. It was first described by the Belgian chemist Frédéric Swarts in 1892.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-X + AgF ->[\Delta] R-F + AgX \quad (X = Cl, Br)" />

The same exchange works with mercurous fluoride, cobalt(II) fluoride or antimony trifluoride:

<Eq ce="2 CH3Br + Hg2F2 ->[\Delta] 2 CH3F + Hg2Br2" />

In the original Swarts method, SbF₃ is used together with a little chlorine or SbCl₅. These form the more reactive antimony chlorofluoride species (such as SbCl₂F₃) that actually transfers fluorine to carbon.

## Mechanism

1. **Activation of C–X.** The metal ion (Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Sb⁵⁺ species) has a strong attraction for chloride or bromide. It coordinates to the halogen on carbon and weakens the C–X bond.
2. **Fluoride transfer.** Fluoride from the metal fluoride bonds to the carbon as the halide leaves, forming the strong C–F bond.
3. **By-product.** The displaced halide stays with the metal as AgCl, AgBr, Hg₂Br₂ or an antimony chloride.

<KeyPoint>
The exchange is favourable because the C–F bond is the strongest carbon–halogen bond and the heavy-metal chloride or bromide formed is very stable.
</KeyPoint>

In exam answers the reaction is sometimes marked **SN1**, reasoning that Ag⁺ pulls off the halide and gives carbocation character. Standard references describe it only as a halogen exchange and do not assign SN1 or SN2, so use the SN1 label only if your coaching material expects it.

## Examples

**Ethyl bromide to ethyl fluoride:**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2Br + AgF ->[\Delta] CH3CH2F + AgBr" />

**Preparation of Freon-12 (CCl₂F₂) from CCl₄.** NCERT states that Freon-12 is manufactured from tetrachloromethane by the Swarts reaction. Industrially, HF is the fluorine source and antimony pentachloride is the catalyst:

<Eq ce="CCl4 + 2 HF ->[SbCl5 (cat.)] CCl2F2 + 2 HCl" />

The same reaction also gives some CCl₃F, CClF₃ and CF₄, depending on how far the exchange goes.

## Nomenclature of freons (CFC numbering)

Freons are chlorofluorocarbons of methane and ethane. Their numbers follow a simple code, read from the right:

| Digit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| units (z) | number of F atoms |
| tens (y) | number of H atoms + 1 |
| hundreds (x) | number of C atoms − 1 (not written when it is zero) |

All remaining valencies are filled by chlorine. A shortcut is to add 90 to the number: the three digits then give C, H and F directly.

- **Freon-12:** C = 1, H = 0, F = 2 → **CCl₂F₂**.
- **R-22 (HCFC-22):** C = 1, H = 1, F = 2 → **CHClF₂** (one Cl fills the last valency).
- **Freon-132:** C = 2, H = 2, F = 2 → C₂H₂F₂Cl₂, for example CHF₂–CHCl₂.

<ExamTip>
The leading zero is dropped: write Freon-12, not Freon-012. Check every freon formula by counting valencies: carbon must have exactly four bonds, so R-22 is CHClF₂, not CHF₂Cl₂.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Silver and mercury fluorides are costly heavy-metal reagents, so AgF and Hg₂F₂ are mainly laboratory and exam reagents; large-scale fluorination of chlorocarbons uses HF with an antimony catalyst.
- The substrates are alkyl chlorides and bromides, whose halogen is replaced readily. In very reactive cases simple shaking or stirring is enough, without heating.
- With polychloro compounds such as CCl₄, the exchange can stop at different stages, so a mixture of fluorochlorocarbons is obtained.
