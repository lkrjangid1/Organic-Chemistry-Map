## What is the Schmidt reaction?

The **Schmidt reaction** converts a **carboxylic acid into a primary amine with one carbon fewer**, using **hydrazoic acid (HN₃)** and a strong acid such as **concentrated H₂SO₄**. The carboxyl carbon leaves as **CO₂** and two nitrogen atoms leave as **N₂**. It is a "step-down" reaction, like the Hofmann bromamide degradation, and both pass through an **isocyanate** (R–N=C=O). Karl Friedrich Schmidt reported it in 1924.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH2COOH + HN3 ->[conc. H2SO4] CH3CH2CH2NH2 + CO2 + N2" />

## Mechanism

Hydrazoic acid is a resonance hybrid, H–N=N⁺=N⁻ ↔ H–N⁻–N⁺≡N; the terminal N₂ unit is an excellent leaving group.

1. **Protonation.** The acid protonates the OH of R–COOH, giving R–C(=O)–OH₂⁺.
2. **Acylium ion.** Water leaves, giving the acylium ion R–C≡O⁺ (in concentrated H₂SO₄). In exam answers this step is often marked as rate-determining.
3. **Attack of HN₃.** The nitrogen bearing H attacks the acylium carbon, giving a protonated acyl azide, R–CO–NH–N⁺≡N.
4. **Rearrangement.** R moves from carbon to nitrogen as N₂ leaves, giving a protonated isocyanate R–NH⁺=C=O. Loss of H⁺ gives the **isocyanate** R–N=C=O.
5. **Hydrolysis.** Water adds to the isocyanate carbon; proton transfer gives a **carbamic acid**, R–NH–COOH.
6. **Decarboxylation.** The carbamic acid loses CO₂, giving the amine (as its ammonium salt in the acid, freed on basification).

<Eq ce="R-COOH ->[H+][-H2O] R-C#O+ ->[HN3] R-CO-NH-N2+ ->[-N2][-H+] R-N=C=O ->[H2O] R-NH-COOH ->[-CO2] R-NH2" />

<KeyPoint>
The migrating group keeps its configuration (**retention**): an optically active acid gives an amine with the same configuration at the migrating carbon, which shows the shift is intramolecular. The N that carried H in HN₃ ends up in the amine; the other two N atoms leave as N₂.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Butanoic acid → propan-1-amine:**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH2COOH ->[HN3, conc. H2SO4] CH3CH2CH2NH2 + CO2 + N2" />

2. **Benzoic acid → aniline:**

<Eq ce="C6H5COOH ->[HN3, H2SO4] C6H5NH2 + CO2 + N2" />

3. **Ketone → amide:** with ketones the same reagent inserts NH next to the carbonyl. Schmidt's first example was benzophenone giving benzanilide.

<Eq ce="(C6H5)2C=O + HN3 ->[H2SO4] C6H5-CO-NH-C6H5 + N2" />

<ExamTip>
For an acid, delete the COOH carbon and write NH₂ in its place, with configuration unchanged. Products: amine + CO₂ + N₂. Compare: Hofmann (amide, Br₂/NaOH) and Curtius (acyl azide, heat) give the same amine through the same isocyanate.
</ExamTip>

In exam answers the reaction is said to go faster when the migrating group is electron-releasing, since it can better support the electron-poor nitrogen during the shift.

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Hazard:** hydrazoic acid is highly toxic and explosive, so it is generated in situ (for example from NaN₃ and acid) and handled with care.
- The strongly acidic conditions rule out acid-sensitive substrates; the Curtius rearrangement (acyl azide from an acid chloride and NaN₃, then heat) runs under neutral conditions instead.
- With ketones, unsymmetrical substrates can give two amides, and the migrating group can change with the acid used.
- Alkenes and tertiary alcohols also react with HN₃ under acid, giving imines.
