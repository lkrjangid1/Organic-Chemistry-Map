## What is the dehydration of alcohols?

**Dehydration of alcohols** is a β-elimination in which an alcohol loses a molecule of **water** (–OH from one carbon and H from the neighbouring carbon) to form an **alkene**. It is carried out by heating the alcohol with a protic acid such as **concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄**, or over catalysts such as alumina or anhydrous ZnCl₂. Ethanol gives ethene when heated with concentrated sulphuric acid at **443 K**.

## Mechanism (NCERT, for ethanol)

1. **Protonation of the alcohol.** The oxygen lone pair takes H⁺ from the acid, giving CH₃CH₂–OH₂⁺. Water is now a good leaving group.
2. **Formation of the carbocation.** Water leaves. NCERT treats this as the **slowest, rate-determining step**.
3. **Loss of a β-proton.** A base (HSO₄⁻ or water) removes H⁺ from the adjacent carbon to give the C=C bond, and the acid is regenerated. Ethene is removed as it forms, pulling the equilibrium forward.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2OH + H+ <=> CH3CH2OH2+ <=>[slow] CH3CH2+ + H2O" />
<Eq ce="CH3CH2+ -> CH2=CH2 + H+" />

<KeyPoint>
Rate of dehydration depends on carbocation stability: **3° greater than 2° greater than 1°**. In textbooks, 1° alcohols need about 170–180 °C, 2° about 100–140 °C, and 3° only about 25–80 °C.
</KeyPoint>

**Exam convention versus lab detail.** NCERT writes the carbocation (E1) mechanism even for ethanol. University texts point out that a primary carbocation is too unstable, so **primary alcohols react by an E2 path** (loss of water and the β-H together), while **secondary and tertiary alcohols react by E1**. For JEE/NEET answers, write the NCERT three-step mechanism unless the question asks about E1/E2 specifically.

## Effect of temperature: alkene or ether?

With ethanol and concentrated H₂SO₄, temperature decides the product:

| Conditions | Main product |
|---|---|
| Below about 140 °C (acid added slowly, cooled) | Ethyl hydrogen sulphate, CH₃CH₂OSO₃H |
| 413 K (140 °C), excess ethanol | Diethyl ether (SN2 on the protonated alcohol) |
| 443 K (170 °C), excess acid | Ethene (elimination) |

At lower temperature a nucleophile (another alcohol molecule) captures the intermediate and substitution wins; at higher temperature elimination wins. Remember that concentrated H₂SO₄ plays three roles: acid, dehydrating agent and oxidising agent (sulphur +6 is reduced to SO₂, +4), so some oxidation side products can form. Dilute H₂SO₄ acts only as an acid.

## Dehydration with POCl₃ in pyridine (E2)

Acid is not the only way to remove water. **Phosphorus oxychloride (POCl₃) in pyridine** dehydrates 2° and 3° alcohols under mild, basic conditions (often at 0 °C):

1. **Activation.** The alcohol oxygen attacks phosphorus and a chloride leaves, converting –OH (a poor leaving group) into a **dichlorophosphate, –OPOCl₂**, a good leaving group.
2. **E2 elimination.** Pyridine, which is both solvent and base, removes a β-hydrogen while –OPOCl₂ departs in the same step, forming the C=C bond.

<Eq ce="C6H11OH ->[POCl3][pyridine] C6H10" />

Cyclohexanol gives cyclohexene this way. Because no carbocation forms, there are **no hydride or alkyl shifts**, which makes POCl₃/pyridine the reagent of choice when acid would cause rearrangement.

## Examples

**1. Butan-2-ol (Saytzeff rule).** Loss of H from C-3 gives but-2-ene (mostly *trans*), the major product; loss from C-1 gives but-1-ene (minor).

<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 ->[conc. H2SO4][\Delta] CH3CH=CHCH3 (major) + CH2=CHCH2CH3 (minor)" />

**2. 2-Methylbutan-2-ol (tertiary, E1).** The tertiary cation loses H from the CH₂ to give 2-methylbut-2-ene (major), with 2-methylbut-1-ene as the minor alkene.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3 ->[H3PO4][\Delta] (CH3)2C=CHCH3 + H2O" />

**3. One alcohol, three products.** 1-Cyclopentylethanol gives a different alkene depending on the reagent:

- **H⁺/Δ (conc. H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄):** E1. The secondary cation next to the ring undergoes a ring-expanding alkyl shift (five-membered to six-membered ring), then a hydride shift gives the tertiary 1-methylcyclohexyl cation; loss of H⁺ gives **1-methylcyclohexene**.
- **POCl₃/pyridine (E2, no carbocation):** no rearrangement; the more substituted alkene, **ethylidenecyclopentane**, forms.
- **ThO₂/Δ:** thoria is reported to dehydrate 2-alkanols selectively to terminal alkenes, so this route gives **vinylcyclopentane** (Hofmann alkene).

<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)C5H9 ->[H+][\Delta] 1-methylcyclohexene + H2O" />

<ExamTip>
Acid plus heat means carbocation: always check for a hydride, alkyl or ring-expansion shift before choosing the alkene. POCl₃/pyridine means E2 and no rearrangement. The same acid conditions also isomerise a less substituted alkene into a more substituted one: methylenecyclohexane with H⁺/Δ gives 1-methylcyclohexene through the same tertiary cation.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Rearrangements** are common for 2° alcohols whose cation can shift to a more stable one.
- **Ether formation** competes for 1° alcohols at lower temperatures; acid dehydration is not a good way to make ethers from 2° or 3° alcohols, since they give alkenes.
- **Vicinal diols** do not simply lose water to an alkene; they undergo the [pinacol–pinacolone rearrangement](/reactions/pinacol-pinacolone-rearrangement/) to a ketone.
- Unhindered 1° alcohols with POCl₃ partly give alkyl chlorides by SN2 instead of the alkene.
