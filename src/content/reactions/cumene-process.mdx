## What is the cumene process?

The **cumene process** is the industrial preparation of **phenol** from **cumene (isopropylbenzene)**. Cumene is first oxidised by **air** to **cumene hydroperoxide**, and this hydroperoxide is then treated with **dilute acid**, which rearranges and splits it into **phenol and acetone**. The acid step is called the **cumene hydroperoxide rearrangement** (also the **Hock rearrangement**), because a phenyl group moves from carbon to oxygen. Most of the world's phenol is made this way.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5CH(CH3)2 ->[O2 (air)] C6H5C(CH3)2-O-O-H ->[H+, H2O] C6H5OH + CH3COCH3" />

The three stages of the full industrial route are:

| Stage | Change | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Alkylation | Benzene + propene → cumene | Acid catalyst (Friedel–Crafts) |
| 2. Autoxidation | Cumene + O₂ → cumene hydroperoxide | Air, radical chain at the benzylic C–H |
| 3. Rearrangement | Hydroperoxide → phenol + acetone | Dilute acid (H₂SO₄ or an acidic clay) |

## Mechanism

**Formation of the hydroperoxide (radical chain).** Oxygen removes the single tertiary benzylic hydrogen of cumene, giving a stabilised cumyl radical. The radical picks up O₂ to form a peroxyl radical, which takes a hydrogen from another cumene molecule. This gives cumene hydroperoxide, C₆H₅C(CH₃)₂–O–O–H, and a new cumyl radical that continues the chain.

**Acid-catalysed rearrangement.**

1. **Protonation.** H⁺ adds to the terminal –OH of the hydroperoxide, so it can leave as water.
2. **Phenyl shift with loss of water.** As water departs, the phenyl group moves from carbon to the oxygen that is losing water. The product is a tertiary carbocation, (CH₃)₂C⁺–O–C₆H₅, stabilised by the lone pair on the oxygen beside it. In exam answers this migration is treated as the slow (rate-determining) step.
3. **Attack of water.** Water adds to the carbocation; loss of H⁺ gives a hemiketal, (CH₃)₂C(OH)–O–C₆H₅.
4. **Breakdown.** A proton moves to the ether oxygen, and the hemiketal falls apart into **phenol** and **acetone**.

<Eq ce="C6H5C(CH3)2-O-OH2+ ->[- H2O][\text{phenyl shift}] (CH3)2C+-O-C6H5 ->[+ H2O][- H+] (CH3)2C(OH)-O-C6H5 -> C6H5OH + (CH3)2CO" />

<KeyPoint>
The rearrangement happens because the oxygen becomes **electron-deficient** when water leaves. An electron-rich group on the neighbouring carbon moves to that oxygen. The phenyl group, which can best share the positive charge during the shift, migrates in preference to methyl.
</KeyPoint>

The same carbon-to-oxygen shift drives the [Baeyer–Villiger oxidation](/reactions/baeyer-villiger-oxidation/), the [Dakin reaction](/reactions/dakin-reaction/) and the oxidation step of [hydroboration–oxidation](/reactions/hydroboration-oxidation/).

## Examples

**1. Phenol and acetone from cumene (NCERT):**

<Eq ce="C6H5CH(CH3)2 + O2 -> C6H5C(CH3)2OOH ->[H3O+] C6H5OH + CH3COCH3" />

**2. Hydroquinone from 1,4-diisopropylbenzene:** both isopropyl groups are converted to hydroperoxides, and acid cleavage gives hydroquinone and two molecules of acetone. 1,3-Diisopropylbenzene gives resorcinol in the same way.

<Eq ce="C6H4(CH(CH3)2)2 ->[O2] C6H4(C(CH3)2OOH)2 ->[H3O+] C6H4(OH)2 + 2 CH3COCH3" />

**3. Phenol and cyclohexanone:** cyclohexylbenzene hydroperoxide cleaves in acid to phenol and cyclohexanone instead of acetone.

<ExamTip>
To predict the products of any "hydroperoxide + acid" question, find the group that migrates to oxygen (aryl first). That group ends up as the phenol or alcohol; the carbon that lost it becomes the C=O of the ketone. For cumene: phenyl → phenol, the C(CH₃)₂ carbon → acetone.
</ExamTip>

## Advantages and limitations

- Cheap feedstocks (benzene, propene, air) are turned into two useful products.
- The route is economical only if the acetone co-product can also be sold.
- Small amounts of acetophenone and α-methylstyrene form as by-products.
- Organic hydroperoxides are unstable, so the oxidation is run under controlled pressure and temperature.
