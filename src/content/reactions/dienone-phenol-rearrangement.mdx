## What is the dienone–phenol rearrangement?

The **dienone–phenol rearrangement** is the acid-catalysed conversion of a **cyclohexadienone into a phenol**. In the standard case a **4,4-disubstituted cyclohexa-2,5-dienone** rearranges, on heating with acid, into a **3,4-disubstituted phenol**: one of the two groups on C-4 moves to C-3 and the ring becomes aromatic. It was first reported by Karl von Auwers and Karl Ziegler in 1921.

The textbook example: **4,4-dimethylcyclohexa-2,5-dienone → 3,4-dimethylphenol** (H⁺, Δ).

## Mechanism

1. **Protonation of the carbonyl.** A lone pair on the C=O oxygen picks up H⁺, giving C=O⁺H.
2. **Delocalised cation.** The positive charge spreads through the ring by resonance: onto C-1 (now bearing OH) and onto C-3/C-5, the carbons right next to the quaternary C-4.
3. **1,2-Methyl shift.** A methyl group on C-4 moves, with its bonding pair, to the cationic C-3. The positive charge is now on C-4, which carries the remaining methyl (a tertiary, allylic, OH-stabilised cation).
4. **Loss of H⁺ and aromatisation.** C-3 loses its H⁺, the ring becomes a benzene ring, and **3,4-dimethylphenol** is formed.

<Eq ce="C8H10O ->[H+] [C8H11O]+ ->[\text{1,2-CH3 shift}] [C8H11O]+ ->[-H+] \text{3,4-(CH3)2C6H3OH}" />

<KeyPoint>
The driving force is **aromaticity**. A 4,4-disubstituted dienone cannot become aromatic because C-4 has no H to lose; shifting one group to C-3 puts an H-bearing carbon next to the cation, and losing that H⁺ gives a stable phenol.
</KeyPoint>

## Which group migrates?

When the two groups on C-4 are different, the group that moves is the one whose shift gives the **more stable carbocation**. Orders reported for the acid-catalysed reaction include COOEt over phenyl or alkyl, phenyl over methyl, vinyl over methyl, and methyl over alkoxy.

<Trick>
**"Electron withdrawing group migrate hota h"**: the electron-withdrawing group migrates. It works for an ester (COOEt): if the ester stayed on C-4 it would destabilise the cation that forms there, so it moves away and leaves the electron-donating group behind. It is **not** a general rule (methyl migrates ahead of alkoxy), so check carbocation stability whenever in doubt.
</Trick>

## Examples

1. **4,4-Dimethyl dienone (standard):**

<Eq ce="\text{4,4-dimethylcyclohexa-2,5-dienone} ->[H+][\Delta] \text{3,4-dimethylphenol}" />

2. **4-Methyl-4-phenyl dienone:** phenyl migrates in preference to methyl, giving mainly **4-methyl-3-phenylphenol** (methyl stays on C-4, phenyl moves to C-3).

3. **Fused (bicyclic) dienones**, such as steroid dienones: a ring bond can migrate instead of a simple alkyl group, which changes the ring system. This is how the reaction is used in steroid, anthracene and phenanthrene synthesis.

<ExamTip>
Number the dienone with C=O as C-1. Protonate the oxygen, put the positive charge on C-3, shift the best migrating group from C-4 to C-3, then remove H⁺ from C-3. OH ends up at C-1, the migrated group at C-3, and the remaining group at C-4.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- The carbon receiving the migrating group must carry an H, or the final aromatising deprotonation is impossible (a quinol substrate then stops at a non-aromatic enedione instead).
- Allyl and benzyl groups can move by a Cope-type pathway instead of a simple 1,2-shift.
- Some substituents on C-4 (for example a dichloromethyl group) block the rearrangement.
- 2,2-Disubstituted cyclohexadienones also rearrange, giving disubstituted phenols.
- The reverse process (phenol to dienone) is the Zincke–Suhl reaction.
