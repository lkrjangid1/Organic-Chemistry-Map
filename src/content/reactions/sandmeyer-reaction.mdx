## What is the Sandmeyer reaction?

The **Sandmeyer reaction** replaces the diazonium group (–N₂⁺) of an **arenediazonium salt** with **Cl, Br or CN** using the matching **copper(I) salt**: CuCl/HCl, CuBr/HBr or CuCN/KCN. Benzenediazonium chloride gives **chlorobenzene**, bromobenzene or benzonitrile, and nitrogen gas escapes. It was discovered by Traugott Sandmeyer in 1884, who obtained chlorobenzene while trying to make phenylacetylene with copper(I) acetylide.

Together with [diazotization](/reactions/diazotization-reaction/), it converts an –NH₂ group on a ring into a halogen or cyano group.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="Ar-N2^+ X^- ->[CuX/HX] Ar-X + N2 ^ \quad (X = Cl, Br)" />

<Eq ce="Ar-N2^+ Cl^- ->[CuCN/KCN] Ar-CN + N2 ^" />

The copper(I) halide is sometimes written in the older dimer form, **Cu₂X₂ + HX** (X = Cl, Br); this is the same reagent as CuX.

## Mechanism

The Sandmeyer reaction is a **radical-nucleophilic aromatic substitution** (SRNAr), not a simple SN1 loss of N₂:

1. **Electron transfer.** Cu(I) gives one electron to the diazonium ion, forming Cu(II).
2. **Loss of nitrogen.** The resulting diazenyl radical breaks down to an **aryl radical** and N₂ gas.
3. **Group transfer.** The aryl radical picks up Cl, Br or CN from the copper(II) species, giving Ar–X and regenerating Cu(I). So copper acts as a catalyst.

<Eq ce="ArN2^+ + Cu^+ -> Ar^. + N2 + Cu^2+" />

<Eq ce="Ar^. + CuCl2 -> ArCl + CuCl" />

<KeyPoint>
Evidence for the radical route: biaryl (Ar–Ar) by-products form, and Cu(II) is needed for the halide transfer step.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Drawing an aryl cation for every diazonium replacement. That picture fits hydrolysis to phenol and the Balz–Schiemann reaction, but not the copper-catalysed Sandmeyer reaction.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Chlorobenzene from benzenediazonium chloride:**

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- ->[CuCl/HCl] C6H5Cl + N2 ^" />

**2. Bromobenzene:**

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- ->[CuBr/HBr] C6H5Br + N2 ^" />

**3. Benzonitrile (cyanation):**

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ Cl^- ->[CuCN/KCN] C6H5CN + N2 ^" />

Benzonitrile can be hydrolysed to benzoic acid, so the Sandmeyer cyanation is a standard step in conversions such as aniline → benzoic acid.

<ExamTip>
In conversion questions, the sequence ArNO₂ → ArNH₂ → ArN₂⁺ → ArX lets you put Cl, Br or CN at positions that direct electrophilic substitution cannot reach, because the –NH₂ group sets the orientation first and is then replaced.
</ExamTip>

## Sandmeyer vs Gattermann vs other replacements

| Product | Reagent | Name |
|---|---|---|
| ArCl, ArBr | CuCl/HCl, CuBr/HBr | Sandmeyer |
| ArCl, ArBr | Cu powder + HCl/HBr | Gattermann |
| ArCN | CuCN/KCN | Sandmeyer |
| ArI | KI (no copper) | not Sandmeyer |
| ArF | HBF₄, then heat | Balz–Schiemann |

NCERT states that the Sandmeyer reaction gives better yields than the Gattermann reaction.

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Iodine**: KI alone reacts with the diazonium salt, so no copper is needed; this is usually not called a Sandmeyer reaction.
- **Fluorine**: copper fluorides do not work; use the [Balz–Schiemann reaction](/reactions/balz-schiemann-reaction/).
- **Cyanide**: a common mistake is writing KCN alone; in the Sandmeyer method the cyanide is delivered by **CuCN** (often dissolved in KCN).
- The diazonium salt must be freshly prepared in the cold; warming it with water alone gives phenol instead.
