## What is the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction?

The **Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction** is the **α-halogenation of a carboxylic acid**. A carboxylic acid that has at least one **α-hydrogen** is treated with **bromine or chlorine in the presence of a small amount of red phosphorus**, and one α-hydrogen is replaced by a halogen to give an **α-halocarboxylic acid**. NCERT lists it as the key substitution reaction of the hydrocarbon part of carboxylic acids.

<Eq ce="R-CH2-COOH ->[X2, red P][then H2O] R-CHX-COOH + HX" />

The reaction is named after Carl Magnus von Hell, Jacob Volhard and Nikolay Zelinsky, who developed it in the 1880s.

## Why the acid needs help

Ketones and aldehydes are halogenated at the α-carbon simply with X₂ and acid, because they form enols. A free carboxylic acid hardly enolises: base removes the O–H proton long before an α-hydrogen. The trick of the HVZ reaction is to turn the acid into an **acyl halide**, which has no acidic O–H and enolises readily.

## Mechanism

1. **Making PX₃.** Red phosphorus reacts with the halogen: 2P + 3X₂ → 2PX₃. So "red P + Br₂" and "PBr₃ + Br₂" are effectively the same reagent.
2. **Acyl halide.** PX₃ converts R–CH₂–COOH into the acyl halide R–CH₂–COX.
3. **Enolisation.** The acyl halide tautomerises (catalysed by the HX formed) to its enol, R–CH=C(OH)X.
4. **α-Halogenation.** The electron-rich C=C of the enol attacks X₂; loss of H⁺ gives the α-halo acyl halide R–CHX–COX.
5. **Back to the acid.** Water hydrolyses R–CHX–COX to R–CHX–COOH. Without water, R–CHX–COX can also exchange with unreacted acid, regenerating the acyl halide, so only a catalytic amount of phosphorus is needed.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2COOH ->[PBr3] CH3CH2COBr <=> CH3CH=C(OH)Br ->[Br2] CH3CHBrCOBr ->[H2O] CH3CHBrCOOH" />

<KeyPoint>
Two conditions to remember: (1) the carboxylic acid must have **at least one α-hydrogen**, and (2) the role of red phosphorus is only to **generate PX₃**, which makes the acyl halide that can enolise.
</KeyPoint>

Because the α-carbon is flat in the enol, a new stereocentre at the α-carbon is formed as a **racemic mixture**. If the reaction is quenched with an alcohol instead of water, the **α-bromo ester** is obtained.

## Examples

**1. Propanoic acid to 2-bromopropanoic acid**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2COOH + Br2 ->[red P] CH3CHBrCOOH + HBr" />

**2. Acetic acid to chloroacetic acid**

<Eq ce="CH3COOH + Cl2 ->[red P] ClCH2COOH + HCl" />

**3. Acetic acid to glycine (HVZ, then ammonolysis)**

<Eq ce="CH3COOH ->[Br2, red P] BrCH2COOH ->[excess NH3] H2N-CH2-COOH" />

The same two-step route from propanoic acid gives racemic alanine, the textbook use of the reaction. α-Halo acids are very reactive in SN2 reactions, so the halogen is easily replaced by NH₂, OH or CN.

## Reagent variations in exam questions

| Reagent on propanoic acid | Product |
|---|---|
| Br₂, red P (then H₂O) | CH₃CHBrCOOH (2-bromopropanoic acid) |
| Cl₂, red P (then H₂O) | CH₃CHClCOOH |
| PCl₅ alone | CH₃CH₂COCl (acyl chloride, no α-substitution) |
| Br₂ + PCl₃ | CH₃CHBrCOOH |
| Cl₂ + PBr₃ | CH₃CHClCOOH |
| excess Br₂, red P | CH₃CBr₂COOH |

<ExamTip>
In exam answers, the α-halogen always comes from the **free halogen X₂**, because it is X₂ that reacts with the enol; the phosphorus halide only makes the acyl halide. So Cl₂/PBr₃ gives the α-chloro acid and Br₂/PCl₃ gives the α-bromo acid. With excess halogen, the remaining α-hydrogens can also be replaced.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **No α-hydrogen, no reaction:** benzoic acid, 2,2-dimethylpropanoic acid and formic acid are not α-halogenated.
- Only **chlorine and bromine** are used; iodination and fluorination are not carried out this way.
- If an aqueous work-up is used during the reaction, a full equivalent of PX₃ is needed because water breaks the catalytic exchange cycle.
