## What is the Dieckmann condensation?

The **Dieckmann condensation** is the **intramolecular Claisen condensation** of a diester. A base such as sodium ethoxide converts one ester group into its enolate, which attacks the other ester group in the same molecule. The product is a **cyclic β-keto ester**. It works best for **five- and six-membered rings**: a 1,6-diester (diethyl adipate) gives a cyclopentanone ring, and a 1,7-diester (diethyl pimelate) gives a cyclohexanone ring.

## Mechanism

The steps are the same as in the [Claisen condensation](/reactions/claisen-condensation/), except that both ester groups are in one molecule.

1. **Enolate formation.** C₂H₅O⁻ removes an α-H next to one ester group.
2. **Ring closure.** The enolate carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of the other ester group (a 5-exo-trig attack for adipate), giving a cyclic tetrahedral alkoxide.
3. **Loss of ethoxide.** The intermediate expels C₂H₅O⁻ to give the cyclic β-keto ester.
4. **Deprotonation and work-up.** The acidic CH between the ring C=O and the ester is removed by ethoxide, which drives the reaction. Dilute acid then gives the neutral β-keto ester.

<Eq ce="C2H5OOC(CH2)4COOC2H5 ->[C2H5ONa] cyclic beta-keto ester + C2H5OH" />

## Hydrolysis and decarboxylation to the cyclic ketone

The ester group can then be removed. Acid hydrolysis gives a cyclic β-keto acid, and gentle heating expels CO₂:

<Eq ce="ethyl 2-oxocyclopentanecarboxylate ->[H3O+] 2-oxocyclopentanecarboxylic acid ->[\Delta] cyclopentanone + CO2" />

So diethyl adipate → (Dieckmann) → β-keto ester → (H₃O⁺) → β-keto acid → (Δ) → **cyclopentanone**.

<KeyPoint>
Ring-size rule: the new ring contains the α-carbon, every carbon between it and the other ester carbonyl, and that carbonyl carbon itself. In diethyl adipate (hexanedioate), C-2 attacks C-6, so the ring has five members. In diethyl pimelate (heptanedioate), C-2 attacks C-7, so the ring has six members.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

| Diester | Dieckmann product | After hydrolysis and Δ |
|---|---|---|
| Diethyl adipate, EtOOC(CH₂)₄COOEt | Ethyl 2-oxocyclopentane-1-carboxylate | Cyclopentanone |
| Diethyl pimelate, EtOOC(CH₂)₅COOEt | Ethyl 2-oxocyclohexane-1-carboxylate | Cyclohexanone |

The β-keto ester can also be alkylated at the acidic carbon between the two carbonyls before hydrolysis and decarboxylation. This route (Dieckmann, then alkylation, then decarboxylation) gives **2-substituted cyclopentanones and cyclohexanones**.

<ExamTip>
In conversion questions, "diester + NaOEt, then H₃O⁺, Δ" almost always means a Dieckmann cyclisation followed by decarboxylation. Write the ring ketone and count one carbon lost as CO₂ along with the ethanol.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Ring size:** five-, six- and (less easily) seven-membered rings form well. Larger rings form poorly, and three- or four-membered rings are too strained.
- **Enolisable product needed:** yields are good only if the cyclic β-keto ester keeps an acidic H between its two carbonyls. Otherwise the reverse ring-opening (retro-Claisen) competes.
- **Unsymmetrical diesters:** if both ester groups have α-H and differ, each can act as donor or acceptor, so a mixture of two β-keto esters can form.
- **Base choice:** as in the Claisen reaction, use an alkoxide or NaH, not aqueous NaOH, which would hydrolyse the esters.
