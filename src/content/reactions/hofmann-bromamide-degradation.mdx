## What is Hofmann bromamide degradation?

**Hofmann bromamide degradation** (the **Hofmann rearrangement**) converts a **primary amide (R–CO–NH₂) into a primary amine (R–NH₂) with one carbon atom fewer**. The amide is warmed with **bromine and aqueous sodium hydroxide**, which together form sodium hypobromite. The carbonyl carbon leaves as carbonate, and the alkyl or aryl group moves from carbon to nitrogen through an **isocyanate** intermediate. It is named after A. W. von Hofmann (1881).

<Eq ce="R-CO-NH2 + Br2 + 4NaOH -> R-NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2NaBr + 2H2O" />

## Mechanism

1. **First deprotonation.** OH⁻ removes an N–H proton, giving the amide anion R–CO–NH⁻.
2. **N-Bromination.** The anion attacks Br₂, giving the **N-bromoamide** R–CO–NH–Br.
3. **Second deprotonation.** OH⁻ removes the remaining, now more acidic, N–H proton: R–CO–N⁻–Br.
4. **Rearrangement.** R migrates from carbon to nitrogen **as Br⁻ leaves**, giving the **isocyanate** R–N=C=O. The nitrogen has become electron-deficient, and that is what drives the shift. A free acyl nitrene (R–CO–N:) is **not** formed.
5. **Hydrolysis.** OH⁻ adds to the isocyanate carbon; proton transfer gives a carbamate/carbamic acid, R–NH–COO⁻ ⇌ R–NH–COOH.
6. **Decarboxylation.** The carbamic acid loses CO₂ (taken up by NaOH as Na₂CO₃), giving R–NH₂.

<Eq ce="R-CO-NH2 ->[Br2, OH-] R-CO-NHBr ->[OH-] R-CO-N^{-}Br ->[-Br-] R-N=C=O ->[H2O] R-NH-COOH ->[-CO2] R-NH2" />

<KeyPoint>
The rearrangement happens because the nitrogen is electron-deficient. The migrating group keeps its configuration (**retention**), so a chiral R gives a chiral amine of the same configuration. In exam answers the reaction is said to be faster when the migrating group is electron-releasing.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Ethanamide → methanamine:**

<Eq ce="CH3CONH2 + Br2 + 4NaOH -> CH3NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2NaBr + 2H2O" />

2. **Butanamide → propan-1-amine:**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH2CONH2 ->[Br2, NaOH] CH3CH2CH2NH2" />

3. **Benzamide → aniline:**

<Eq ce="C6H5CONH2 ->[Br2, NaOH] C6H5NH2" />

Other textbook uses: phthalimide gives anthranilic acid, and nicotinamide gives 3-aminopyridine.

<ExamTip>
Count carbons: the amine has one fewer than the amide. If the question uses **NaOD/D₂O** with Br₂, the N–H hydrogens exchange with deuterium, so acetamide is drawn as giving CH₃ND₂. If the solvent is **methanol**, the isocyanate is trapped as a methyl carbamate (R–NH–COOCH₃) instead of giving the amine.
</ExamTip>

## Cyclic imides: an exam twist

A cyclic imide treated with NaOH and Br₂ first has its ring opened by OH⁻, which gives a carboxylate at one end and an amide at the other. The amide end then undergoes Hofmann degradation. For N-bromosuccinimide (NBS, a **five-membered** ring), the ring-opened ⁻OOC–CH₂–CH₂–CO–NHBr gives **3-aminopropanoate (β-alanine anion)**, H₂N–CH₂–CH₂–COO⁻. Similarly, phthalimide gives anthranilic acid.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Only **primary** amides (–CONH₂) work; the nitrogen needs two H atoms to lose.
- Other reagents can replace Br₂/NaOH: NaOCl, NBS, lead tetraacetate or hypervalent iodine reagents.
- Nucleophilic solvents trap the isocyanate: methanol gives carbamates, tert-butanol gives Boc-protected amines.
- Some α,β-unsaturated or α-hydroxy amides stop at carbamates or other products.
