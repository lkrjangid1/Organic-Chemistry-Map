## What is the Clemmensen reduction?

The **Clemmensen reduction** converts the **carbonyl group of an aldehyde or ketone into a CH₂ group**, so the carbonyl compound becomes an **alkane** (or an alkylbenzene). The reagent is **zinc amalgam (Zn-Hg) with concentrated hydrochloric acid**, heated under reflux. Because the medium is strongly acidic, the method is used for compounds that are stable to acid. It was reported by the Danish-American chemist Erik Christian Clemmensen in 1913.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-CO-R' ->[Zn-Hg, conc. HCl][\Delta] R-CH2-R' + H2O" />

NCERT lists it together with the Wolff–Kishner reduction under "reduction to hydrocarbons" of aldehydes and ketones.

## Mechanism

The mechanism is not fully known, because the reaction happens on the surface of solid zinc (a heterogeneous reaction). The currently accepted picture is:

1. **Protonation.** The acid protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electron-poor.
2. **Electron transfer from zinc.** Zinc gives electrons to the carbonyl carbon at the metal surface, and the carbon becomes bonded to zinc.
3. **Loss of oxygen.** After further protonation the oxygen leaves as water, giving a **zinc carbenoid** (a carbon attached to zinc with carbene-like character).
4. **Protonation steps.** More electron transfer from zinc and protonation by HCl convert the carbenoid into the CH₂ group.

<KeyPoint>
The alcohol R–CH(OH)–R′ is **not** an intermediate. When the alcohol itself is treated with Zn-Hg and HCl it is generally not reduced to the alkane, so a pathway through the alcohol cannot explain the reaction.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not write the Clemmensen mechanism as carbonyl → alcohol → carbocation → alkane. That sequence would need the alcohol to be reduced under the same conditions, which does not happen. Write "reduction on the zinc surface via organozinc (carbenoid) intermediates".
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Acetophenone to ethylbenzene.** Aryl alkyl ketones, such as those made by Friedel–Crafts acylation, respond particularly well.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 ->[Zn-Hg, conc. HCl][\Delta] C6H5CH2CH3 + H2O" />

**2. Propiophenone to propylbenzene.** This acylation–reduction sequence gives an unrearranged n-propyl group on benzene.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH2CH3 ->[Zn-Hg, conc. HCl] C6H5CH2CH2CH3" />

**3. An aldehyde.** Butanal is reduced to butane in the same way; the CHO group becomes CH₃.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH2CHO ->[Zn-Hg, HCl] CH3CH2CH2CH3" />

## Clemmensen versus Wolff–Kishner

| Feature | Clemmensen | Wolff–Kishner |
|---|---|---|
| Reagents | Zn-Hg, conc. HCl | NH₂NH₂, KOH, ethylene glycol |
| Medium | strongly acidic | strongly basic |
| Temperature | reflux | about 180–200 °C |
| Choose it when the substrate is | stable to acid, unstable to base | stable to base, unstable to acid |
| Overall change | C=O to CH₂ | C=O to CH₂ |

If a compound is sensitive to both acid and base, the **Mozingo reduction** (thioacetal, then Raney nickel) is the mild alternative.

<ExamTip>
Spot the sensitive group first. An ester or an amide that hot KOH would hydrolyse points to Clemmensen; an acetal, a tertiary alcohol that would dehydrate, or another acid-sensitive group points to Wolff–Kishner.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Acid-sensitive groups** do not survive 37% HCl at reflux.
- **Nitro groups** are also reduced. Metals in acidic medium reduce –NO₂ to –NH₂, so a nitro group in the substrate ends up as an amino group: R–NO₂ → R–NH₂.
- **Aliphatic and cyclic ketones** are reduced less efficiently than aryl alkyl ketones under the classical conditions. A modified version with activated zinc dust and dry HCl in ether works better for them.
- **α,β-Unsaturated and 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds** behave unusually. In exam answers, Clemmensen is flagged as a reaction where rearranged products are possible, particularly with 1,3-diketones; such substrates are best avoided in simple "predict the product" questions.
