## What is the Thorpe reaction?

The **Thorpe reaction** is the **base-catalysed self-condensation of aliphatic nitriles**. The base removes an α-hydrogen from one nitrile, and the carbanion adds to the C≡N carbon of a second nitrile, giving a **β-iminonitrile** that tautomerises to a **β-enaminonitrile**. It was discovered by Jocelyn Field Thorpe in the early 1900s. Its intramolecular version on dinitriles, the **Thorpe–Ziegler reaction**, is a route to cyclic ketones.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="2 RCH2C#N ->[base] RCH2C(=NH)CH(R)C#N <=> RCH2C(NH2)=C(R)C#N" />

Think of it as the nitrile version of the [Claisen condensation](/reactions/claisen-condensation/): one molecule supplies the carbanion, the other supplies the electrophilic carbon. Because C≡N has no leaving group, nothing is lost, and the product keeps the nitrogen as =NH (or –NH₂ in the enamine form).

## Mechanism

1. **Carbanion formation.** The base removes a proton from the carbon next to C≡N. The negative charge is shared with nitrogen: ⁻CH₂–C≡N ⟷ CH₂=C=N⁻.
2. **Addition to the nitrile carbon.** The carbanion attacks the electrophilic carbon of a second nitrile. The C≡N π bond breaks and an **imine anion** forms, just as an alkoxide forms when a nucleophile adds to C=O.
3. **Protonation.** The imine anion picks up a proton, giving the β-iminonitrile, CH₃–C(=NH)–CH₂–C≡N.
4. **Tautomerism.** The imine shifts a hydrogen to become the enamine, CH₃–C(NH₂)=CH–C≡N. The enamine is favoured because its C=C is conjugated with C≡N.

<Eq ce="CH3C#N + B- -> ^-CH2C#N + BH" />
<Eq ce="^-CH2C#N + CH3C#N -> CH3C(=N^-)CH2C#N ->[BH] CH3C(=NH)CH2C#N" />

<KeyPoint>
The α-hydrogen of a simple nitrile has a pKa of about 25, similar to an ester and much less acidic than a ketone (about 19). That is why a strong base is needed; nitrile anions in general are made with bases such as LDA or butyllithium. Exam answers often write OH⁻ for simplicity.
</KeyPoint>

## Thorpe–Ziegler reaction (intramolecular version)

When both nitrile groups sit in the same molecule, the carbanion formed at one end attacks the C≡N at the other end and closes a ring. Acid hydrolysis of the cyclic enaminonitrile then gives a **cyclic ketone**. The relationship to the [Dieckmann condensation](/reactions/dieckmann-condensation/) is exact: Dieckmann closes a diester to a cyclic β-keto ester, Thorpe–Ziegler closes a dinitrile to a cyclic β-enaminonitrile.

<Eq ce="NC(CH2)4CN ->[base] \text{2-aminocyclopent-1-ene-1-carbonitrile} ->[H3O+][\Delta] \text{cyclopentanone}" />

Hydrolysis turns the enamine into C=O and the remaining nitrile into –COOH. That acid is now a β-keto acid, so it loses CO₂ on warming, leaving the plain cyclic ketone. Ziegler used this method to make large-ring ketones with more than six ring atoms.

## Cyano-aldol: a nitrile carbanion meeting an aldehyde

If the electrophile is an aldehyde instead of a second nitrile, the same nitrile carbanion adds to C=O. In exam answers this is sometimes called a **cyano-aldol** condensation: the β-hydroxy nitrile forms first and then dehydrates on heating to an α,β-unsaturated nitrile.

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + ^-CH2C#N -> CH3CH(OH)CH2C#N ->[OH-][\Delta] CH3CH=CHC#N + H2O" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not assume that OH⁻ alone deprotonates acetonitrile efficiently. Its α-H (pKa about 25) is far less acidic than the α-H of acetaldehyde (about 17), so with aqueous base the aldehyde mainly undergoes its own aldol reaction. In practice nitrile anions are made with strong bases such as LDA or butyllithium.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Acetonitrile (self-condensation).**

<Eq ce="2 CH3C#N ->[base] CH3C(NH2)=CHC#N" />

**2. Propanenitrile.** The carbanion forms on the CH₂ next to C≡N, so the new bond joins that carbon to the nitrile carbon of the second molecule.

<Eq ce="2 CH3CH2C#N ->[base] CH3CH2C(NH2)=C(CH3)C#N" />

**3. Thorpe–Ziegler ring closure** of hexanedinitrile, as shown above, gives a five-membered ring and finally cyclopentanone.

## Limitations

- Only nitriles with **α-hydrogens** can supply the carbanion; benzonitrile, for example, can only act as the electrophile.
- Strong, dry bases are needed, because simple nitriles are weak carbon acids.
- For large rings in the Thorpe–Ziegler version, intermolecular condensation competes, so the ring closure must be favoured over chain-forming reactions.
