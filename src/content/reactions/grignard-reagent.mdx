## What is a Grignard reagent?

A **Grignard reagent** is an **alkyl or aryl magnesium halide, RMgX**, made by reacting an **alkyl or aryl halide with magnesium metal in dry ether**. The C–Mg bond is polar, with the carbon carrying a partial negative charge, so the R group behaves like a carbanion: a **strong nucleophile and a strong base**. Its most important use is adding to **aldehydes and ketones to form alcohols** after acidic work-up. The reagents were discovered by Victor Grignard in 1900.

## Preparation

<Eq ce="R-X + Mg ->[dry ether] R-MgX" />

<Eq ce="C6H5Br + Mg ->[dry ether] C6H5MgBr" />

- **R** is an alkyl, vinyl or aryl group, not hydrogen.
- **X** is Cl, Br or I. Alkyl fluorides do not react with ordinary magnesium because the C–F bond is too strong.
- **Solvent:** dry diethyl ether or THF. The ether oxygen coordinates to Mg and stabilises the reagent.

## Reaction with protic compounds (why everything must be dry)

RMgX is a much stronger base than water, alcohols, phenols, amines or terminal alkynes. Any compound with an acidic hydrogen (written HA) protonates it and releases the alkane:

<Eq ce="R-MgX + H-A -> R-H + Mg(A)X" />

<Eq ce="CH3MgBr + H2O -> CH4 + Mg(OH)Br" />

<KeyPoint>
Grignard reactions must be run under strictly anhydrous conditions. Even moist air converts RMgX into R–H. The same reaction can be used on purpose to convert R–X into the alkane R–H (via RMgX and water).
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism of addition to a carbonyl group

1. **Nucleophilic attack.** The carbanion-like R group attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon; the C=O π electrons move onto oxygen.
2. **Alkoxide adduct.** A magnesium alkoxide (R–C–O–MgX) forms.
3. **Acidic work-up.** Dilute acid protonates the alkoxide to give the alcohol and Mg(OH)X.

<Eq ce="R'2C=O + RMgX -> R'2C(R)-OMgX ->[H3O+] R'2C(R)-OH + Mg(OH)X" />

## Which alcohol forms?

| Carbonyl compound | Product after H₃O⁺ |
|---|---|
| Methanal (HCHO) | 1° alcohol |
| Other aldehydes (RCHO) | 2° alcohol |
| Ketones (R₂CO) | 3° alcohol |

## Examples

**Ketone → tertiary alcohol** (butan-2-one with ethylmagnesium bromide):

<Eq ce="CH3COC2H5 + C2H5MgBr -> (C2H5)2C(OMgBr)CH3 ->[H3O+] (C2H5)2C(OH)CH3" />

**Benzophenone → triphenylmethanol:**

<Eq ce="(C6H5)2CO + C6H5MgBr ->[1) dry ether][2) H3O+] (C6H5)3COH" />

**Carbonation → carboxylic acid** (one carbon added):

<Eq ce="C6H5MgBr + CO2 -> C6H5COOMgBr ->[H3O+] C6H5COOH" />

**Nitrile → ketone** via an imine salt:

<Eq ce="C6H5CN + CH3MgBr -> C6H5C(=NMgBr)CH3 ->[H3O+] C6H5COCH3" />

**Epoxide ring opening:** RMgX attacks the less hindered carbon of the epoxide (SN2-like), giving an alcohol with the new C–C bond two carbons away from oxygen.

**Acid chlorides and esters:** the first addition gives a ketone, which reacts with a second molecule of RMgX. With excess reagent the product is a tertiary alcohol with two identical R groups.

<ExamTip>
To design a Grignard synthesis, cut the bond between the carbinol carbon and one of its R groups. That R group comes from RMgX; the rest is the aldehyde or ketone. Never put an OH, NH or COOH group in the same molecule as the Grignard reagent.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Substrates with acidic hydrogens (–OH, –NH₂, –COOH, terminal alkyne C–H) quench the reagent instead of reacting at the carbonyl group.
- With very hindered ketones, RMgX can act as a base (forming the enolate) or deliver a hydride (reduction) instead of adding.
- Grignard reagents generally do not couple with ordinary alkyl halides without a metal catalyst.
