## What is the Gattermann–Koch reaction?

The **Gattermann–Koch reaction** is the direct **formylation of benzene**: benzene, or an alkylbenzene, is treated with **carbon monoxide and hydrogen chloride** in the presence of **anhydrous aluminium chloride** and a little **copper(I) chloride**, and a **–CHO group replaces a ring hydrogen**. Benzene gives **benzaldehyde**. It is an electrophilic aromatic substitution, closely related to Friedel–Crafts acylation, and the electrophile is the **formyl cation, HCO⁺**. The reaction was reported by Ludwig Gattermann and Julius Arnold Koch in 1897.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="Ar-H + CO + HCl ->[anhyd. AlCl3, CuCl] Ar-CHO" />

The CO is supplied under pressure. Both CO and HCl end up in the product, so nothing but the aldehyde appears on the right of the net equation.

## Mechanism

1. **Making the electrophile.** Carbon monoxide can be drawn as a resonance hybrid, :C=O: ↔ ⁻:C≡O⁺, so its carbon carries a lone pair. HCl protonates that carbon, and AlCl₃ takes up the chloride. The result is the **formyl cation**, H–C≡O⁺ ↔ H–C⁺=O.

<Eq ce="CO + HCl + AlCl3 -> HCO+ + AlCl4-" />

2. **Attack by the ring.** The π electrons of benzene attack the carbon of HCO⁺, giving a resonance-stabilised arenium ion (σ-complex). This step breaks aromaticity and is the slow step.
3. **Deprotonation.** AlCl₄⁻ removes the proton from the sp³ ring carbon. Aromaticity is restored, HCl and AlCl₃ are regenerated, and benzaldehyde is formed.

<KeyPoint>
**Why CO + HCl?** The "natural" Friedel–Crafts reagent for a –CHO group would be formyl chloride, H–CO–Cl, but it is far too unstable to isolate. Mixing CO with HCl generates the same formyl electrophile in situ. Older books describe the reaction as going through formyl chloride; the modern view is that the formyl cation reacts directly.
</KeyPoint>

**Role of CuCl.** When CO is not used at high pressure, or when ZnCl₂ replaces AlCl₃, traces of copper(I) chloride are needed. The copper binds CO as a carbonyl complex and acts as a carrier that feeds CO into the formation of the electrophile.

## Examples

**1. Benzaldehyde from benzene (NCERT example).**

<Eq ce="C6H6 + CO + HCl ->[anhyd. AlCl3/CuCl] C6H5CHO" />

**2. Toluene.** The methyl group activates the ring and directs ortho/para; substitution goes mainly para, giving 4-methylbenzaldehyde (p-tolualdehyde).

<Eq ce="C6H5CH3 + CO + HCl ->[AlCl3/CuCl] p-CH3C6H4CHO" />

## Gattermann–Koch versus related formylations

| Reaction | Reagents | Electrophile | Typical substrate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gattermann–Koch | CO + HCl, AlCl₃/CuCl | HC≡O⁺ | benzene, alkylbenzenes |
| Gattermann aldehyde synthesis | HCN + HCl, AlCl₃ (or Zn(CN)₂ + HCl), then H₂O | HC⁺=NH (iminium-type) | benzene, phenols, phenol ethers |
| Reimer–Tiemann | CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH | :CCl₂ (dichlorocarbene) | phenol (ortho-CHO) |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | RCOCl, AlCl₃ | RC≡O⁺ | arenes, gives ketones |

<ExamTip>
Don't confuse three "Gattermann" reactions. **Gattermann–Koch** (CO + HCl) and the **Gattermann aldehyde synthesis** (HCN + HCl) both put –CHO on a ring. The plain **Gattermann reaction** of diazonium salts (Cu powder + HCl/HBr) makes aryl halides.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Phenols and phenol ethers do not work.** Unlike the HCN version, the Gattermann–Koch reaction is not applicable to phenol, anisole and similar substrates.
- **Deactivated rings fail.** As in Friedel–Crafts reactions, rings carrying strong electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –CN, –COR) are too unreactive.
- **Strictly anhydrous conditions.** Water destroys AlCl₃ and the formyl cation.
- **Gases under pressure.** CO is toxic and is normally used under pressure, which makes the method more suited to industry than to a school laboratory.
