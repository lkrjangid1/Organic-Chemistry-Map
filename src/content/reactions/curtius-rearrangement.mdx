## What is the Curtius rearrangement?

The **Curtius rearrangement** is the **thermal decomposition of an acyl azide, R–CO–N₃, into an isocyanate, R–N=C=O, with loss of nitrogen gas**. The acyl azide is usually made from an **acid chloride and sodium azide**. Hydrolysis of the isocyanate gives a **primary amine with one carbon fewer** than the starting acid, so like the Hofmann reaction it is a **step-down** route from acids to amines. Theodor Curtius described it in 1885.

<Eq ce="R-COCl + NaN3 -> R-CO-N3 + NaCl" />
<Eq ce="R-CO-N3 ->[\Delta] R-N=C=O + N2 ^" />
<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + H2O -> R-NH2 + CO2" />

## Mechanism

1. **Azide ion.** Sodium azide supplies N₃⁻, drawn as ⁻N=N⁺=N⁻ in resonance with N²⁻–N⁺≡N.
2. **Acyl azide.** The terminal nitrogen of azide attacks the acyl carbon of R–COCl; Cl⁻ leaves (nucleophilic acyl substitution), giving R–CO–N⁻–N⁺≡N.
3. **Rearrangement.** On warming, N₂ leaves and R shifts from the carbonyl carbon to the adjacent nitrogen **at the same time**, giving the isocyanate R–N=C=O. In exam answers this loss of N₂ with migration is the slow, rate-determining step.
4. **Hydrolysis.** Water adds to the C=N of the isocyanate to give a **carbamic acid**, R–NH–COOH.
5. **Decarboxylation.** Carbamic acids are unstable and lose CO₂, giving R–NH₂.

<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + H2O -> R-NH-COOH -> R-NH2 + CO2" />

Older textbooks show an **acyl nitrene** (R–CO–N:) formed first and then rearranging. Modern evidence (no nitrene by-products, calculations) shows the thermal reaction is **concerted**; free nitrenes appear only in the photochemical version, where they give side products.

<KeyPoint>
Key facts: (1) **step-down** reaction (amine has one carbon fewer than the acid); (2) **intramolecular** 1,2-shift with **retention of configuration**; (3) the **isocyanate is the intermediate** and can be isolated; (4) the shift happens because nitrogen becomes **electron-deficient** as N₂ leaves; (5) electron-rich groups migrate best, roughly 3° &gt; 2° ≈ aryl &gt; 1°.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Acetyl chloride to methylamine**

<Eq ce="CH3COCl ->[NaN3][\Delta] CH3-N=C=O ->[H2O] CH3NH2 + CO2" />

**2. Benzoyl chloride to aniline**

<Eq ce="C6H5COCl ->[NaN3] C6H5CON3 ->[\Delta] C6H5N=C=O ->[H2O] C6H5NH2 + CO2" />

**3. Trapping the isocyanate**

<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + C2H5OH -> R-NH-COOC2H5" />
<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + R'NH2 -> R-NH-CO-NH-R'" />

An alcohol gives a **carbamate (urethane)** and an amine gives a **substituted urea**.

## Curtius vs Hofmann vs Schmidt

| Reaction | Starting material | Reagents | Common intermediate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Curtius | Acyl azide (from RCOCl) | NaN₃, then heat; H₂O | R–N=C=O |
| [Hofmann bromamide](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/) | Primary amide RCONH₂ | Br₂ + NaOH (aq.) | R–N=C=O |
| [Schmidt](/reactions/schmidt-reaction/) | Carboxylic acid RCOOH | HN₃, conc. H₂SO₄ | R–N=C=O |
| [Lossen](/reactions/lossen-rearrangement/) | Acyl hydroxamate | base | R–N=C=O |

<ExamTip>
If a question gives RCOCl + NaN₃ and heat, write the isocyanate first, then the amine on hydrolysis. Count carbons: the carbonyl carbon leaves as CO₂, so CH₃COCl gives CH₃NH₂, not CH₃CH₂NH₂.
</ExamTip>

## Variations and limitations

- The acyl azide can also be made from an **acyl hydrazide and nitrous acid**, or directly from the acid with **diphenylphosphoryl azide (DPPA)**.
- Brønsted or Lewis acids (e.g. BF₃) lower the decomposition temperature by about 100 °C.
- Acyl azides are **potentially explosive** and must be heated carefully.
