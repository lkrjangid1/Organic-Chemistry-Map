## What is ether cleavage with HX?

**Ether cleavage** is the breaking of a C–O bond of an ether by a **strong hydrogen halide, usually concentrated HI or HBr, on heating**. The ether R–O–R′ gives an **alcohol and an alkyl halide**. With excess HX the alcohol is converted into a second alkyl halide. The reaction is an acid-catalysed nucleophilic substitution: it goes by **SN2** when the groups are methyl or primary, and by **SN1** when one group is tertiary, benzylic or allylic.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-O-R' + HX ->[\Delta] R-OH + R'-X" />

<Eq ce="R-OH + HX -> R-X + H2O \quad (\text{with excess HX})" />

Order of reactivity of the acids: **HI > HBr > HCl**. HF does not cleave ethers.

## Mechanism

**Step 1: protonation.** The ether oxygen takes up H⁺ from HX to form an oxonium ion. This turns the poor leaving group (RO⁻) into a good one (ROH).

**Step 2 (SN2 path, methyl and 1°/2° groups).** The halide ion attacks the **less hindered carbon** of the oxonium ion and pushes out the alcohol in one step. With methyl ethyl ether, for example, iodide attacks the methyl group, giving CH₃I and ethanol.

<Eq ce="CH3-O-CH2CH3 + HI -> CH3I + CH3CH2OH" />

**Step 2 (SN1 path, 3°, benzyl, allyl groups).** The oxonium ion breaks on its own to give the **more stable carbocation** and an alcohol. The halide then captures the carbocation. So the tertiary (or benzylic) group ends up as the halide.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-O-CH3 + HI -> (CH3)3C-I + CH3OH" />

<KeyPoint>
Decide the path from the alkyl groups: if one group can give a stable carbocation (3°, benzyl, allyl), use SN1 and that group becomes the halide. Otherwise use SN2: the halide attacks the smaller group, which becomes the halide.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Anisole with HI** (Zeisel's method, map edges anisole → phenol): the O–CH₃ bond breaks, never the O–C₆H₅ bond, because the aryl carbon is sp² and the bond has partial double-bond character. Phenol does not react further to give an aryl halide.

<Eq ce="C6H5OCH3 + HI ->[\Delta] C6H5OH + CH3I" />

Measuring the CH₃I formed is the basis of **Zeisel's method** for counting methoxy groups in a compound.

**Diethyl ether with excess HI:**

<Eq ce="C2H5OC2H5 + 2 HI ->[\Delta] 2 C2H5I + H2O" />

**Diaryl ethers** such as diphenyl ether are **not cleaved** by HI.

## Acid-catalysed hydrolysis of ethers (isotope-labelling questions)

Exam questions sometimes ask what happens when an ether with a carbocation-forming group is heated with H₂¹⁸O and acid. Following the SN1 logic above, the protonated ether breaks to give the more stable carbocation (R′⁺) and R–OH. Water then traps the carbocation, so the **¹⁸O label ends up in the alcohol formed from the carbocation group**:

<Eq ce="R-O-R' ->[H2^{18}O, H+] R-OH + R'-^{18}OH" />

Ethers do **not** react with water or OH⁻ alone; an acid is essential.

## Ring opening of unsymmetrical epoxides

Epoxides are cyclic ethers whose ring strain makes them far more reactive than ordinary ethers. For 2-methyloxirane the site of attack depends on the conditions:

- **Basic or strongly nucleophilic conditions (SN2):** the nucleophile attacks the **less substituted** carbon.
- **Acidic conditions:** the protonated epoxide opens with substantial SN1 character, so the nucleophile attacks the **more substituted** carbon.

<Eq ce="\text{2-methyloxirane} ->[CH3O^{-}Na+, CH3OH] CH3-CH(OH)-CH2-OCH3" />

<Eq ce="\text{2-methyloxirane} ->[CH3OH, H+] CH3-CH(OCH3)-CH2-OH" />

Grignard reagents behave as basic nucleophiles: CH₃MgBr opens 2-methyloxirane at the CH₂ end, and work-up gives butan-2-ol.

<ExamTip>
For any ether + HI question, write three things: protonation, which group leaves as the alcohol, and which carbon the iodide ends up on. For aryl alkyl ethers the answer is always phenol + alkyl iodide.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- The reaction is slow at room temperature and needs heat and concentrated acid.
- With poorly nucleophilic acids (for example trifluoroacetic acid), tertiary ethers undergo E1 elimination to an alkene instead of substitution.
- Excess HX and high temperature convert both groups into alkyl halides (except an aryl group, which stays as phenol).
