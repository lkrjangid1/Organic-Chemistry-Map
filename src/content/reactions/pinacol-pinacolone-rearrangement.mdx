## What is the pinacol–pinacolone rearrangement?

The **pinacol–pinacolone rearrangement** is the acid-catalysed conversion of a **vicinal (1,2-) diol** into a **ketone**. Water is lost, and a group on the neighbouring carbon moves across in a **1,2-shift**. It is named after the classic example: **pinacol** (2,3-dimethylbutane-2,3-diol) rearranges to **pinacolone** (3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one). The reaction was first described by Wilhelm Rudolph Fittig in 1860.

The only structural requirement is two OH groups on adjacent carbons: –C(OH)–C(OH)–.

## Mechanism

1. **Protonation.** The acid protonates one OH group, turning it into a good leaving group (–OH₂⁺).
2. **Carbocation formation.** Water leaves, giving a carbocation next to the carbon that still carries an OH. In exam answers this step is treated as the slow, rate-determining step.
3. **1,2-shift.** A methyl group (with its bonding pair) moves from the carbinol carbon to the positively charged carbon.
4. **Deprotonation.** The positive charge now sits on the carbon bearing the OH, stabilised as an oxonium ion. Loss of H⁺ from oxygen gives the C=O of pinacolone.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C(OH)-C(OH)(CH3)2 ->[H+] (CH3)2C(OH)-C+(CH3)2 ->[\text{1,2-CH3 shift}] (CH3)3C-C+(OH)CH3 ->[-H+] (CH3)3C-CO-CH3" />

<KeyPoint>
The driving force is the conversion of a carbocation into a resonance-stabilised oxonium ion, which then becomes a strong C=O bond.
</KeyPoint>

## Which OH leaves, and which group moves?

For unsymmetrical diols, two choices decide the product:

- **Which OH leaves:** the one that gives the **more stable carbocation**. A carbon carrying phenyl groups loses its OH first, because the cation is stabilised by resonance.
- **Which group migrates (migratory aptitude):** roughly **aryl > H > alkyl**, with methyl among the poorest migrators. The group that can best share the positive charge during the shift moves.

<ExamTip>
First pick the more stable carbocation, then move the group with the highest migratory aptitude from the neighbouring carbon. The carbon that kept its OH becomes the C=O carbon.
</ExamTip>

## Ring expansion and ring contraction

When the diol is part of a ring, or attached to one, the migrating bond can be a ring bond. The ring then **expands** (for example, a cyclobutyl group next to the cation becomes a cyclopentanone) or **contracts**. In rigid cyclic diols, a group that is *trans* to the leaving water migrates much faster than a *cis* group.

**Worked example: a spiro ketone.** A favourite multi-step question builds the diol first and then rearranges it:

1. Cyclohexylidenecyclohexane (two rings joined by a C=C) is treated with **OsO₄, then aqueous work-up**, which adds two OH groups *syn* across the C=C, giving 1,1′-bi(cyclohexyl)-1,1′-diol.
2. With **H⁺ and heat**, one OH leaves to give a tertiary carbocation on one ring.
3. A **ring C–C bond** of the other ring (not a methyl group) migrates to the cation. That ring grows from six to seven atoms, and its carbinol carbon becomes the C=O.
4. The product is **spiro[5.6]dodecan-7-one**: a cycloheptanone and a cyclohexane sharing one spiro carbon.

<Eq ce="(CH2)5C=C(CH2)5 ->[OsO4][H3O+] (CH2)5C(OH)-C(OH)(CH2)5 ->[H+][\Delta] spiro ketone + H2O" />

The same rearrangement of 1,1′-bi(cyclopentyl)-1,1′-diol gives spiro[4.5]decan-6-one. Whenever both carbinol carbons sit in rings, expect a **ring-expanded spiro ketone**.

## Related reactions

- **Pinacol coupling** (Mg/Hg, then H₂O) is how pinacols are made from ketones, so the two reactions are often asked together as a sequence: acetone → pinacol → pinacolone.
- The **semipinacol rearrangement** is the same 1,2-shift starting from substrates where only one carbon carries an OH.
- The **dienone–phenol rearrangement** is another acid-catalysed 1,2-shift driven by forming a more stable product.
