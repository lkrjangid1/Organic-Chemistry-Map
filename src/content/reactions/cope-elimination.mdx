## What is the Cope elimination?

The **Cope elimination** (Cope reaction) is the thermal conversion of a **tertiary amine N-oxide** into an **alkene** and an **N,N-dialkylhydroxylamine**. No external base is needed: the negatively charged oxygen of the N-oxide removes a β-hydrogen from the **same side** of the molecule, so the reaction is a **syn elimination** that passes through a cyclic five-membered transition state. It was developed by Arthur C. Cope, and the amine oxide is usually made in situ from the tertiary amine with hydrogen peroxide or a peroxy acid.

## Mechanism (Ei, internal elimination)

1. **Oxidation.** A tertiary amine R–CH₂CH₂–NMe₂ is oxidised by H₂O₂ or mCPBA to the N-oxide R–CH₂CH₂–N⁺Me₂–O⁻.
2. **Heating.** The O⁻ swings round to a β-hydrogen on the same face. In one concerted step six electrons move around a five-membered ring (O, N, Cα, Cβ, H): the O–H bond forms, the C–H bond breaks, the C=C forms and the C–N bond breaks.
3. **Products:** alkene + Me₂N–OH.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2-N(CH3)2-O ->[\Delta] CH2=CH2 + (CH3)2N-OH" />

<KeyPoint>
Cope elimination is a **syn** process and gives the **Hofmann alkene**. It is sometimes miscalled "syn addition"; it is a syn *elimination*. The H removed and the N-oxide group must be able to sit roughly in the same plane on the same side.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Ethyl amine oxide.** N,N-Dimethylethylamine N-oxide gives ethene and N,N-dimethylhydroxylamine on heating.

**2. Synthesis of methylenecyclohexane.** The N-oxide of N,N-dimethylcyclohexylmethylamine eliminates on heating to give the exocyclic alkene methylenecyclohexane, a classic preparative example.

<Eq ce="C6H11CH2N(CH3)2 ->[H2O2] C6H11CH2N(CH3)2-O ->[\Delta] C6H10=CH2 + (CH3)2NOH" />

**3. Regioselectivity.** When β-hydrogens are available on both a CH₃ and a CH₂ group, the bulky N-oxide removes the more accessible CH₃ hydrogen, so the less substituted alkene is the major product (Hofmann rule).

<ExamTip>
Spot the Ei family by three clues: heat only (no added base or acid), syn stereochemistry, and Hofmann (less substituted) alkene. Cope uses a five-membered transition state; ester and xanthate pyrolysis use a six-membered one.
</ExamTip>

## Other Ei eliminations: ester and xanthate pyrolysis

The Cope reaction is grouped with **pyrolysis of esters and xanthates**, because all three are internal (Ei) syn eliminations.

- **Ester pyrolysis.** An ester with a β-hydrogen, heated strongly (by vacuum pyrolysis, typically several hundred °C; reported examples run near 575–700 °C), gives an alkene and the corresponding carboxylic acid. The **carbonyl oxygen** abstracts the β-hydrogen through a six-membered cyclic transition state, so if the C=O oxygen is labelled (O*), the label ends up in the –OH of the acid. Ethyl acrylate, for example, gives acrylic acid and ethene.

<Eq ce="R-CO-O-CH2CH3 ->[\Delta] R-COOH + CH2=CH2" />

- **Xanthate pyrolysis (Chugaev elimination).** The same idea applied to an S-methyl xanthate ester made from an alcohol, again giving an alkene by syn elimination.

Like the Cope reaction, these pyrolyses are syn and tend to give the less hindered alkene when the cyclic transition state is crowded.

## Limitations and exceptions

- A β-hydrogen that can be syn-coplanar with the N-oxide is required.
- In six-membered rings the reaction is rare, because the required syn geometry is hard to reach; five-membered and seven-membered or larger rings react well.
- The reverse reaction (retro-Cope) is known: an N,N-disubstituted hydroxylamine can add to an alkene to give an N-oxide.
