## What is the Fries rearrangement?

The **Fries rearrangement** is the Lewis-acid-catalysed conversion of a **phenolic ester** (an aryl ester) into a **hydroxyaryl ketone**. Heating **phenyl acetate** with **anhydrous AlCl₃** moves the acyl group from oxygen to the ring, giving a mixture of **2-hydroxyacetophenone (ortho)** and **4-hydroxyacetophenone (para)**. The ratio is controlled by temperature: **low temperature favours para, high temperature favours ortho**. It is named after the German chemist Karl Theophil Fries.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5-O-CO-R ->[AlCl3][then H3O+] 2-HO-C6H4-CO-R + 4-HO-C6H4-CO-R" />

The ester is usually made first from the phenol and an acyl chloride or anhydride. Phenol itself cannot be ring-acylated by [Friedel–Crafts acylation](/reactions/friedel-crafts-acylation/): under those conditions it reacts at oxygen and gives the ester, which is exactly the Fries substrate.

## Mechanism

1. **Ester formation (if starting from phenol).** The phenolic oxygen attacks the acyl chloride, chloride leaves, and the phenyl ester C₆H₅–O–CO–R forms.
2. **Complexation.** AlCl₃ coordinates to an oxygen of the ester, which weakens the O–C(acyl) bond.
3. **Acylium ion.** The complex breaks into an **acylium ion, R–C≡O⁺**, and an aluminium phenoxide, C₆H₅–O–AlCl₃⁻.
4. **Electrophilic attack.** The O–Al phenoxide is strongly electron-donating, so its ortho and para carbons attack the acylium ion (an electrophilic aromatic substitution). A cyclohexadienone intermediate forms.
5. **Rearomatisation.** Loss of the ring proton (tautomerisation) restores the aromatic ring.
6. **Work-up.** Aqueous acid breaks the O–Al bond and liberates the free hydroxyaryl ketone.

<Eq ce="C6H5OCOR + AlCl3 -> C6H5O-AlCl3^- + RCO+ -> 2/4-(RCO)C6H4OAlCl2 ->[H3O+] 2/4-(RCO)C6H4OH" />

<KeyPoint>
**Intramolecular or intermolecular?** The acylium ion and the phenoxide can recombine inside the solvent cage (an ion pair, effectively intramolecular), or the acylium ion can drift away and acylate another molecule (intermolecular). Both happen, so crossover products are possible. Many exam answers describe it simply as intermolecular, because the acyl group fully separates as a free acylium ion.
</KeyPoint>

## Temperature control of ortho and para

| Condition | Major product | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Low temperature | para-hydroxyaryl ketone | Kinetic control: para attack is faster and less hindered |
| High temperature | ortho-hydroxyaryl ketone | Thermodynamic control: the ortho product forms a stable chelate with Al |
| Non-polar solvent | more ortho | Observed solvent effect |
| More polar solvent | more para | The para share rises as solvent polarity increases |

<ExamTip>
Remember "**low–para, high–ortho**". The ortho product is the thermodynamic one because its OH and C=O oxygens grip the aluminium in a six-membered chelate. At high temperature the reaction can reach this more stable product.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and variations

- The acyl group must survive the harsh, strongly acidic conditions.
- Heavily substituted (crowded) esters give low yields.
- Strongly deactivating, meta-directing groups on the ring slow or stop the reaction, as in Friedel–Crafts acylation.
- AlCl₃ reacts violently with water and is needed in excess; protic acids such as HF or methanesulphonic acid are alternatives.
- **Photo-Fries rearrangement:** UV light gives the same o/p ketones through a radical pathway. It works even with deactivating groups but yields are low, so it is not used industrially. It also slowly degrades aromatic polyester and polycarbonate plastics in sunlight.
