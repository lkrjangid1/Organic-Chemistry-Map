## What is the Diels–Alder reaction?

The **Diels–Alder reaction** is a **[4+2] cycloaddition** between a **conjugated diene** (4 π electrons) and a **dienophile** (an alkene or alkyne, 2 π electrons) that forms a **cyclohexene ring**. Two new C–C σ bonds form in a single concerted step on **heating**; no light, catalyst or intermediate is involved. Otto Diels and Kurt Alder described it in 1928 and received the 1950 Nobel Prize. The simplest example is buta-1,3-diene + ethene → cyclohexene.

## Mechanism

1. **s-cis diene.** The diene rotates about its central single bond into the **s-cis** conformation, so C-1 and C-4 point the same way.
2. **One cyclic transition state.** The diene and dienophile approach in parallel planes. Six π electrons move round a ring at the same time: the two π bonds of the diene and the π bond of the dienophile break, two new σ bonds form (C-1 and C-4 of the diene to the dienophile carbons), and a new π bond forms between C-2 and C-3.
3. **Product.** A cyclohexene with the double bond at the former C-2/C-3 of the diene.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH-CH=CH2 + CH2=CH2 ->[\Delta] cyclohexene" />

<KeyPoint>
The Diels–Alder reaction is **concerted, stereospecific and thermally allowed** ([π4s + π2s] in Woodward–Hoffmann terms). The driving force is turning two π bonds into two stronger σ bonds.
</KeyPoint>

## Stereochemistry and selectivity

- **Dienophile geometry is kept.** A cis-dienophile gives cis substituents in the ring; a trans-dienophile gives trans substituents.
- **Endo rule.** With cyclic dienes such as cyclopentadiene or furan, the electron-withdrawing group of the dienophile tucks under the diene in the transition state. The resulting **endo** product is usually formed faster (kinetic product); the **exo** product is often more stable (thermodynamic product).
- **Electronic demand.** In the normal reaction, an electron-rich diene reacts with an electron-poor dienophile (HOMO of diene with LUMO of dienophile). Groups such as CHO, COR, COOR and CN on the dienophile speed it up.

## Examples

**1. Buta-1,3-diene + ethene → cyclohexene**. Ethene has no electron-withdrawing group, so it is a weak dienophile and the reaction needs strong heating.

**2. Buta-1,3-diene + maleic anhydride → cis-1,2,3,6-tetrahydrophthalic anhydride.** The anhydride is a strong dienophile and the cis geometry of its two C=O groups is kept in the product.

<Eq ce="C4H6 + C4H2O3 ->[\Delta] C8H8O3" />

**3. Furan + maleic anhydride → oxa-bridged bicyclic adduct.** Furan acts as the diene and the oxygen becomes a one-atom bridge (7-oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptene skeleton). Furan loses its aromaticity in the reaction, so the adduct readily undergoes the **retro-Diels–Alder** reaction on heating. At room temperature the endo adduct forms faster; given heat and time the more stable exo adduct takes over.

<Eq ce="C4H4O + C4H2O3 <=> C8H6O4" />

<ExamTip>
To predict the product, number the diene C1–C4, connect C1 and C4 to the two dienophile carbons, and place the new double bond between C2 and C3. Keep the dienophile substituents on the same side if they were cis.
</ExamTip>

## Retro-Diels–Alder reaction

Because the forward reaction has negative ΔH and negative ΔS, the reverse becomes favourable at high temperature. Heating dicyclopentadiene, for example, regenerates cyclopentadiene, and furan adducts revert easily because furan regains its aromatic stabilisation.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Dienes that cannot reach the s-cis conformation (for example, those locked in s-trans by a ring) do not react.
- Very bulky groups at C-2 and C-3 of the diene destabilise the s-cis form and slow the reaction.
- Aromatic rings are poor dienes: naphthalene needs forcing conditions and very reactive dienophiles, while anthracene, less aromatic in its central ring, reacts there with maleic anhydride.

## Related reactions
