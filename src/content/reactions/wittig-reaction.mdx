## What is the Wittig reaction?

The **Wittig reaction** converts an **aldehyde or ketone into an alkene** by treating it with a **phosphorus ylide** (a Wittig reagent, Ph₃P=CHR). The carbonyl oxygen is swapped for the ylide carbon, so the new C=C bond forms exactly where the C=O was, and **triphenylphosphine oxide (Ph₃P=O)** is released. It was reported by Georg Wittig and Ulrich Schöllkopf in 1954, and Wittig shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry partly for this work.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R2C=O + Ph3P=CHR' -> R2C=CHR' + Ph3P=O" />

A useful picture: circle the oxygen of C=O and the PPh₃ part of the ylide together. They leave as O=PPh₃, while the carbonyl carbon and the CH₂ of the ylide join to form the C=C bond.

## Preparing the ylide

1. **SN2 alkylation.** Triphenylphosphine, a good nucleophile, attacks a primary alkyl halide to give an alkyltriphenylphosphonium salt.
2. **Deprotonation.** The C–H next to the positive phosphorus is weakly acidic, so a strong base such as n-butyllithium removes it to give the neutral ylide.

<Eq ce="Ph3P + CH3Br -> Ph3P^+-CH3 Br^- ->[n-BuLi] Ph3P=CH2" />

The ylide is a resonance hybrid of Ph₃P=CH₂ and Ph₃P⁺–CH₂⁻; the carbanion form explains why its carbon is nucleophilic. Secondary halides alkylate Ph₃P poorly, so most ylides come from primary halides.

## Mechanism

1. **Addition.** The nucleophilic ylide carbon bonds to the carbonyl carbon while the carbonyl oxygen bonds to phosphorus.
2. **Oxaphosphetane.** This gives a four-membered ring containing C–C–P–O, the **oxaphosphetane**. Under lithium-free conditions it forms in one concerted [2+2] step; older textbooks show a zwitterionic **betaine** that closes to the ring, and lithium salts can make betaine-like intermediates matter.
3. **Fragmentation.** The oxaphosphetane splits into the alkene and Ph₃P=O.

<KeyPoint>
The driving force is the formation of the very strong P=O bond in triphenylphosphine oxide.
</KeyPoint>

## Stereochemistry

- **Non-stabilised ylides** (R = alkyl) react fast and give mainly the **Z-alkene**.
- **Stabilised ylides** (R = –COOR, –COR) are less reactive and give mainly the **E-alkene**.
- **Semi-stabilised ylides** (R = aryl) often give E/Z mixtures.

## Examples

**1. Methylenation of cyclohexanone.**

<Eq ce="(CH2)5C=O + Ph3P=CH2 -> (CH2)5C=CH2 + Ph3P=O" />

**2. Benzaldehyde to styrene.**

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + Ph3P=CH2 -> C6H5CH=CH2 + Ph3P=O" />

**3. Industrial use.** BASF makes vitamin A using a Wittig reaction between a 15-carbon ylide and a 5-carbon aldehyde, and β-carotene is built by a double Wittig reaction of a diylide with two equivalents of an aldehyde. Even a hindered ketone such as camphor can be converted into its methylene derivative with Ph₃P=CH₂.

<ExamTip>
To find the reactants for a given alkene, cut the C=C bond: one half becomes the C=O compound and the other half becomes the ylide carbon attached to PPh₃. Choose the split where the ylide comes from a primary (or methyl) halide.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Tetrasubstituted alkenes** cannot be made this way because of steric hindrance; mono-, di- and trisubstituted alkenes are fine.
- **Hindered ketones** react slowly and give poor yields, especially with stabilised ylides. The Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons reaction, which uses a phosphonate carbanion, is preferred then.
- **Strong base needed.** Non-stabilised ylides are made with bases such as n-BuLi, so the reaction is run under anhydrous conditions.

## Related reactions

- [Grignard reagent](/reactions/grignard-reagent/) additions also attack the C=O carbon, but give alcohols instead of alkenes.
- [Dehydration of alcohols](/reactions/dehydration-of-alcohols/) is the older route to alkenes and often gives isomer mixtures.
- [Wolff–Kishner reduction](/reactions/wolff-kishner-reduction/) is another way to remove the carbonyl oxygen, giving CH₂ instead of C=C.
- The [SN2 reaction](/reactions/sn2-reaction/) is the first step in making the phosphonium salt.
