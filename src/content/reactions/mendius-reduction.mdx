## What is the Mendius reduction?

The **Mendius reduction** is the reduction of a **nitrile (alkyl or aryl cyanide, R–C≡N) to a primary amine, R–CH₂–NH₂**, using **sodium metal and ethanol**. Four hydrogen atoms add across the C≡N triple bond; the nitrile carbon becomes the CH₂ group bonded to nitrogen. It is named after Otto Mendius, who reported this new conversion of nitriles in 1862.

<Eq ce="R-C#N ->[Na, C2H5OH] R-CH2-NH2" />

## Mechanism (dissolving-metal reduction)

Sodium acts as the electron source and ethanol as the proton source, the same pattern as in the [Bouveault–Blanc reduction](/reactions/bouveault-blanc-reduction/) of esters.

1. **Electron transfer.** Sodium gives one electron to the C≡N group, forming a radical anion.
2. **Protonation.** Ethanol protonates it; a second electron and a second proton complete the first addition of H₂, giving an imine, R–CH=NH.
3. **Second reduction.** The C=N of the imine is reduced in the same way (two electrons, two protons) to the amine R–CH₂–NH₂.

Overall, four sodium atoms and four ethanol molecules are used per nitrile, and sodium ethoxide is formed.

<Eq ce="R-C#N + 4Na + 4C2H5OH -> R-CH2-NH2 + 4C2H5ONa" />

<KeyPoint>
Every reagent that fully reduces a nitrile (Na/C₂H₅OH, LiAlH₄ then H₂O, or H₂/Ni) gives the **same** primary amine, R–CH₂–NH₂. Partial reduction to the aldehyde needs special reagents: SnCl₂/HCl ([Stephen reduction](/reactions/stephen-reduction/)) or DIBAL-H.
</KeyPoint>

## Comparison of nitrile reducing agents

| Reagent | Product from R–C≡N | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Na + C₂H₅OH (Mendius) | R–CH₂–NH₂ | dissolving-metal reduction |
| [LiAlH₄](/reagents/lithium-aluminium-hydride/), then H₂O | R–CH₂–NH₂ | the NCERT reagent |
| H₂ / Ni, Pd or Pt ([catalytic hydrogenation](/reactions/catalytic-hydrogenation/)) | R–CH₂–NH₂ | cheapest industrially; some 2° and 3° amine may form |
| SnCl₂ / HCl, then H₃O⁺ | R–CHO | Stephen reduction, stops at aldehyde |
| DIBAL-H, then H₂O | R–CHO | stops at aldehyde |

## Limitations and exceptions

- In catalytic hydrogenation the imine intermediate can be attacked by the amine product, giving some secondary and tertiary amine; catalyst choice controls this.
- Strongly acidic or basic aqueous conditions can hydrolyse the –CN group to an amide or acid instead of reducing it, so the reduction is done in alcohol.
- **Isocyanides behave differently:** R–N≡C is reduced to the secondary amine R–NH–CH₃, which is a common exam contrast with nitriles.

<ExamTip>
Count carbons. A nitrile reduction product keeps every carbon of R–CN, so R–CH₂NH₂ has one more carbon than the alkyl halide R–X used to make the nitrile. Hofmann bromamide degradation does the opposite (one carbon fewer).
</ExamTip>
