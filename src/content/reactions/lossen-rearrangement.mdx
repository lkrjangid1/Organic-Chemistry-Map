## What is the Lossen rearrangement?

The **Lossen rearrangement** is the base-promoted conversion of an **O-activated hydroxamic acid** (an acyl hydroxamate, R–CO–NH–O–COR′) into an **isocyanate**, R–N=C=O. In water the isocyanate is hydrolysed and loses CO₂, so the overall result is a **primary amine with one carbon fewer** than the starting acid derivative. It was described by Wilhelm Lossen in the 1870s and belongs to the same family as the [Hofmann](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/) and [Curtius](/reactions/curtius-rearrangement/) rearrangements.

## General reaction

A hydroxamic acid, R–CO–NH–OH, is made from an ester (or acid chloride) and hydroxylamine. Its OH is then converted into a better leaving group, usually by acylation with acetic anhydride or an acid chloride, or with a sulfonyl chloride or CDI. Base then triggers the rearrangement.

<Eq ce="R-CO-OR'' ->[NH2OH] R-CO-NH-OH ->[Ac2O] R-CO-NH-O-COCH3" />

## Mechanism

1. **Deprotonation.** The base removes the N–H proton, which is acidic because it is next to a C=O, giving the conjugate base R–CO–N⁻–O–COR′.
2. **1,2-Shift with loss of the leaving group.** In one concerted step the group R moves from the carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, the N–O bond breaks and the carboxylate R′COO⁻ leaves. The nitrogen lone pair forms the C=N bond of the isocyanate. In exam answers this migration step is taken as the rate-determining step.
3. **Hydrolysis.** Water adds to the isocyanate to give a carbamic acid, R–NH–COOH, which loses CO₂ to give the amine.

<Eq ce="R-CO-N^--O-COR' -> R-N=C=O + R'COO^-" />
<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + H2O -> [R-NH-COOH] -> R-NH2 + CO2" />

<KeyPoint>
Lossen is a **step-down** reaction: the C=O carbon leaves as CO₂, so the amine has one carbon fewer. The migrating group keeps its **configuration**, because it never separates from the molecule during the shift.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Hydroxide is a poor leaving group, so a free hydroxamic acid anion does not simply expel OH⁻. The oxygen must first be activated (O-acyl, O-sulfonyl); the leaving group is then a carboxylate or sulfonate. The rate rises as the leaving acid becomes stronger.
</ExamTip>

## Factors that affect the rate

- **Leaving group:** the stronger the conjugate acid of the leaving group (lower pKa, more electron-withdrawing substituents), the faster the rearrangement.
- **Migrating group:** in exam answers, electron-releasing groups on the migrating group are said to speed up the shift, since the group moves with its bonding pair to electron-poor nitrogen.
- **Stereochemistry:** a chiral migrating carbon shows **retention of configuration**.

## Examples

**Benzohydroxamic acid to aniline.** The O-acetyl derivative rearranges in base to phenyl isocyanate, which water converts into aniline.

<Eq ce="C6H5-CO-NH-O-COCH3 ->[OH-] C6H5-N=C=O ->[H2O] C6H5-NH2 + CO2" />

**Trapping the isocyanate.** Without water, the isocyanate can be captured: an alcohol gives a carbamate (urethane), and an amine gives a substituted urea.

<Eq ce="R-N=C=O + R'OH -> R-NH-CO-OR'" />

## Limitations and practical notes

- The acyl hydroxamate is rarely isolated; the activator is usually added to the hydroxamic acid and the rearrangement happens in the same pot.
- Compared with the Curtius route (azides, explosive) and the Hofmann route (bromine/hypobromite oxidant), the Lossen route avoids both hazardous reagents.
- A "self-propagating" version from the free hydroxamic acid is known, but it needs special conditions (a strong base and an added electrophile), not just aqueous hydroxide.
