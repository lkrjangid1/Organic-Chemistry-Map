## What is the Fittig reaction?

The **Fittig reaction** is the coupling of two molecules of an **aryl halide** with **sodium metal in dry ether**, giving a **biaryl** in which the two aryl groups are bonded directly. The standard example is **chlorobenzene → biphenyl**. NCERT describes it as the aryl analogue of the Wurtz reaction: aryl halides treated with sodium in dry ether give compounds in which two aryl groups are joined together. It is named after Wilhelm Rudolph Fittig.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="2 Ar-X + 2 Na ->[dry ether] Ar-Ar + 2 NaX" />

Here Ar is an aryl group (phenyl in most exam questions) and X is Cl, Br or I.

## Mechanism

The Fittig reaction is explained in the same way as the Wurtz reaction. Modern studies have not settled the mechanism, so two pictures are taught.

1. **Radical pathway.** Sodium transfers an electron to the aryl halide. The C–X bond breaks, giving an aryl radical (C₆H₅•) and X⁻. Two aryl radicals combine to give biphenyl. The formation of triphenylene when sodium reacts with chlorobenzene is evidence that radicals are involved.
2. **Organosodium pathway.** Sodium converts one aryl halide into phenylsodium, C₆H₅⁻Na⁺. This carbanion-like species attacks a second aryl halide, displacing X⁻ and forming the Ar–Ar bond.

<Eq ce="C6H5Cl + 2 Na -> C6H5^-Na+ + NaCl" />
<Eq ce="C6H5^-Na+ + C6H5Cl -> C6H5-C6H5 + NaCl" />

<KeyPoint>
The aryl C–X bond is stronger than an alkyl C–X bond because of resonance (partial double bond character) and sp² carbon. That is why the order of reactivity is **Wurtz &gt; Fittig**.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Chlorobenzene to biphenyl:**

<Eq ce="2 C6H5Cl + 2 Na ->[dry ether] C6H5-C6H5 + 2 NaCl" />

2. **Bromobenzene to biphenyl**, the same coupling with a better leaving group:

<Eq ce="2 C6H5Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] C6H5-C6H5 + 2 NaBr" />

3. **4-Bromotoluene to 4,4′-dimethylbiphenyl**, showing that substituents on the ring stay in place and the new bond forms where the halogen was:

<Eq ce="2 CH3C6H4Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] CH3C6H4-C6H4CH3 + 2 NaBr" />

## Fittig vs Wurtz vs Wurtz–Fittig

| Reaction | Halides used | Metal / solvent | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wurtz | 2 R–X (alkyl) | Na / dry ether | R–R (alkane) |
| Fittig | 2 Ar–X (aryl) | Na / dry ether | Ar–Ar (biaryl) |
| Wurtz–Fittig | Ar–X + R–X | Na / dry ether | Ar–R (alkylarene) |

<ExamTip>
Read the starting materials first: only aryl halides means Fittig, only alkyl halides means Wurtz, one of each means Wurtz–Fittig. All three use the same reagent, Na in dry ether, so the reagent alone does not tell you the name.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **For JEE/NEET**, the Fittig reaction is written simply as chlorobenzene + Na/dry ether → biphenyl.
- **In practice**, yields are poor and side products form (for example triphenylene from chlorobenzene). Coupling of aryl halides with sodium works better under ultrasound, and real biaryl syntheses use copper (Ullmann) or palladium catalysts instead.
- Groups that react with sodium (OH, NO₂, C=O) cannot be present on the ring.
- Two different aryl halides would give a mixture of three biaryls, so the method is used for symmetrical biaryls only.
