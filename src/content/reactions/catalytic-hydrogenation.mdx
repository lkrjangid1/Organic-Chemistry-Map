## What is catalytic hydrogenation?

**Catalytic hydrogenation** is the addition of a molecule of **hydrogen (H₂)** across a carbon–carbon double or triple bond in the presence of a finely divided metal catalyst, usually **nickel, palladium or platinum**. An alkene becomes an **alkane**, and an alkyne takes up two moles of H₂ to give the alkane. Both hydrogens add to the **same face** of the multiple bond (**syn addition**), and the reaction happens on the surface of the solid metal, so it is a heterogeneous, **adsorption-controlled** process.

Without a catalyst the reaction is exothermic but far too slow to observe, because its activation energy is high. The metal lowers that barrier.

## The catalysts

| Catalyst | Form used | Typical conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Nickel | Raney nickel (made from Ni–Al alloy and NaOH) | Higher temperature and pressure; cheap, used for oils |
| Palladium | Pd on charcoal (Pd/C) | Room temperature, ethanol solvent |
| Platinum | PtO₂, Adams' catalyst (reduced to Pt metal by H₂) | Ethanol or acetic acid |
| Rhodium (homogeneous) | [Wilkinson's catalyst](/reagents/wilkinsons-catalyst/), RhCl(PPh₃)₃ | Dissolves in the solvent; prefers unhindered C=C |

## Mechanism

1. **Adsorption of H₂.** Hydrogen molecules are adsorbed on the metal surface; the H–H bond breaks and the H atoms bind to metal atoms.
2. **Adsorption of the alkene.** The alkene lies flat on the surface and its π bond interacts with the metal, which weakens it.
3. **Hydrogen transfer.** Two surface hydrogens are transferred, one after the other, to the two alkene carbons. Because the alkene sits on the surface, both arrive from the **underside**, which gives syn addition.
4. **Desorption.** The alkane is held more weakly than the alkene, so it leaves the surface and frees the site for the next molecule.

<Eq ce="R2C=CR2 + H2 ->[Ni / Pd / Pt] R2CH-CHR2" />

<KeyPoint>
Three facts to remember: (1) it is a **syn addition**; (2) it is a **surface (adsorption) process** on the metal; (3) the catalyst is not used up and can be filtered off.
</KeyPoint>

## Heat of hydrogenation and alkene stability

The heat released (about 30 kcal per mole, roughly 120 kJ per mole, for a simple alkene) is called the **heat of hydrogenation**. When several alkenes give the **same alkane**, the one that releases less heat was lower in energy, that is, **more stable**. For the three C₅H₁₀ alkenes that give 2-methylbutane:

| Alkene | ΔH (kcal per mole) | Substitution |
|---|---|---|
| 3-Methylbut-1-ene | −30.3 | Monosubstituted |
| 2-Methylbut-1-ene | −28.5 | Disubstituted |
| 2-Methylbut-2-ene | −26.9 | Trisubstituted |

So stability rises with the number of alkyl groups on the double bond (hyperconjugation), and **trans** alkenes are more stable than **cis** ones. The full order is on [alkene stability and heat of hydrogenation](/comparisons/alkene-stability-heat-of-hydrogenation/).

<ExamTip>
In exam answers, the rate of catalytic hydrogenation is taken to run parallel to the heat of hydrogenation: the less substituted, less stable alkene (larger heat of hydrogenation) is hydrogenated faster, because it is less hindered and adsorbs more easily on the metal.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**Syn addition on a ring.** 1,2-Dimethylcyclohexene gives only the cis product, because both H atoms come from the catalyst side.

<Eq ce="C6H8(CH3)2 ->[H2, Pt] cis-C6H10(CH3)2" />

**Hydrogenation of oils (vanaspati).** The C=C bonds of glyceryl trioleate are saturated over nickel at high temperature and pressure to give glyceryl tristearate, a solid fat.

<Eq ce="C57H104O6 + 3H2 ->[Ni][\Delta, pressure] C57H110O6" />

**Counting double bonds.** Cyclohexene uses one equivalent of H₂ and cyclohexa-1,4-diene uses two, both giving cyclohexane. Measuring the H₂ taken up tells how many C=C bonds a compound has.

## Scope: what H₂ and a metal reduce

| Group | Product | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| C=C, C≡C | Alkane | Fast with Pd, Pt or Ni |
| C≡C with Lindlar or P-2 catalyst | cis-Alkene | See [Lindlar catalyst](/reagents/lindlar-catalyst/) |
| Aldehyde, ketone | 1° or 2° alcohol | Needs Raney Ni or forcing conditions |
| Nitrile, imine | Amine | Ni or Pd |
| Nitro group | Amine | H₂/Pd; see [reduction of nitrobenzene](/reactions/reduction-of-nitrobenzene/) |
| Benzene ring | Cyclohexane | Only at high pressure and temperature |
| COOH, ester, amide | Not reduced | Too stable for ordinary hydrogenation |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
"H₂/Ni reduces everything except COOH" is a useful exam shortcut, but it applies only under forcing conditions. Under the mild conditions used for alkenes (Pd/C, room temperature), ketones, esters, nitriles and benzene rings are **not** touched, so the C=C can be reduced selectively. Esters and amides, like acids, also resist ordinary catalytic hydrogenation.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- The catalyst can move the double bond before H₂ adds (isomerisation), so the syn result is not always clean.
- Partial reduction of an alkyne to an alkene is not possible with Pd/C or Pt: the alkene formed is reduced at once. A poisoned catalyst or a different method is needed.
- Heat in industrial oil hardening can convert some cis C=C bonds into trans ones (trans fats).
