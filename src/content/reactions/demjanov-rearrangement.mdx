## What is the Demjanov rearrangement?

The **Demjanov rearrangement** is the reaction of a **primary amine with nitrous acid (HNO₂)** in which the amino group is replaced by OH and the carbon skeleton rearranges on the way. With an aminomethyl group on a ring, the ring usually **expands by one carbon**, so an aminomethylcycloalkane gives a larger **cycloalkanol**. It was reported by N. J. Demjanov in 1903.

Its more useful cousin, the **Tiffeneau–Demjanov rearrangement**, starts from a **1,2-amino alcohol** (–C(OH)–C(NH₂)–, NH₂ and OH on adjacent carbons). With HNO₂ it gives a **ketone**; 1-aminomethylcyclohexanol, for example, gives **cycloheptanone**.

## Mechanism (Tiffeneau–Demjanov version)

1. **Diazotisation.** HNO₂ converts –CH₂NH₂ into –CH₂N₂⁺, an excellent leaving group.
2. **Loss of N₂.** N₂ leaves, so the CH₂ carbon becomes electron-deficient. In exam answers this is drawn as a separate primary carbocation and treated as the slow (rate-determining) step. The currently accepted picture is that N₂ leaves and the neighbouring bond shifts **at the same time**, because a free primary carbocation is too unstable.
3. **1,2-shift (ring expansion).** A ring C–C bond on the carbinol carbon moves to the CH₂ carbon. The ring gains a carbon and the positive charge lands on the carbon that carries OH.
4. **Deprotonation.** That cation is stabilised as an oxonium ion (C=OH⁺). Loss of H⁺ gives the ketone.

<Eq ce="R2C(OH)-CH2NH2 ->[HNO2] R2C(OH)-CH2N2+ ->[-N2][\text{1,2-shift}] R-C+(OH)-CH2R ->[-H+] R-CO-CH2R" />

<KeyPoint>
The driving force is the same as in the pinacol rearrangement: a poor (primary) cation is replaced by an oxygen-stabilised cation, which then becomes a strong C=O bond.
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism (plain Demjanov version)

With no OH group, the cation formed after the shift cannot become a C=O. Instead **water attacks it**, giving an alcohol. Water can also displace N₂ directly, without any shift, so a mixture forms:

<Eq ce="\text{cyclobutylmethylamine} ->[HNO2][H2O] \text{cyclopentanol (ring-expanded)} + \text{cyclobutylmethanol} + N2" />

Alkenes from loss of H⁺, and further rearrangements, add to the mixture. That is why the Tiffeneau–Demjanov variant, which always ring-expands and stops cleanly at the ketone, is preferred in synthesis.

## Examples

1. **Cyclohexane to cycloheptane ring:**

<Eq ce="\text{1-(aminomethyl)cyclohexanol} ->[NaNO2, HCl] \text{cycloheptanone} + N2 + H2O" />

2. **One-carbon ring expansion of a ketone (sequence):** cyclohexanone → cyanohydrin (HCN) → reduce CN to CH₂NH₂ (LiAlH₄) → HNO₂ → cycloheptanone. This homologation is the main practical use.

3. **Substituted ring:** when the two ring bonds that could shift are different, the more substituted (secondary) alkyl carbon migrates in preference, so 2-substituted aminomethylcyclohexanols give mainly the 3-substituted cycloheptanone.

<ExamTip>
Spot the pattern –C(OH)–C–NH₂ (NH₂ on the carbon next to the carbinol carbon) plus HNO₂ or NaNO₂/HCl. Remove N₂, shift a bond from the carbinol carbon, and turn the C–OH carbon into C=O. If the carbinol carbon is in a ring, the ring grows by one.
</ExamTip>

## Which group migrates?

The shift is controlled by how well the migrating bond can share positive charge, plus steric and conformational factors, so it is not always easy to predict. Useful exam rules: aryl and more substituted alkyl groups migrate in preference to primary alkyl, and the migrating group keeps its configuration. Aryl groups migrate through a bridged phenonium ion, and electron-rich aryls (such as anisyl) move faster than phenyl.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Plain Demjanov reactions give mixtures (rearranged and unrearranged alcohols, alkenes), so yields are often low.
- Ring expansion works best for making five-, six- and seven-membered rings; yields fall for larger rings.
- The reagent is nitrous acid (from NaNO₂ and acid), not nitric acid.
