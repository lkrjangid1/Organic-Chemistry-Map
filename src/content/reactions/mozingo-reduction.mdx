## What is the Mozingo reduction?

The **Mozingo reduction** converts the **C=O group of an aldehyde or ketone into CH₂**, giving an alkane, in two steps. First the carbonyl compound reacts with **ethane-1,2-dithiol** under acid catalysis to form a **cyclic thioacetal** (a 1,3-dithiolane). Then the thioacetal is heated with **hydrogen-saturated Raney nickel**, which strips out both sulfur atoms and leaves a CH₂ group. It is named after Ralph Mozingo, who reported C–S bond cleavage with Raney nickel in 1942.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-CO-R' + HSCH2CH2SH ->[H+] R-C(SCH2CH2S)-R' + H2O" />
<Eq ce="R-C(SCH2CH2S)-R' ->[Raney Ni (H2)] R-CH2-R'" />

## Mechanism

1. **Thioacetal formation.** Acid (a Brønsted acid or a Lewis acid) activates the C=O. One SH group adds to the carbonyl carbon, water is lost, and the second SH group closes a five-membered ring. Thioketals form readily under acid catalysis, unlike ordinary ketals, and ethane-1,2-dithiol is chosen because it gives high yields.
2. **Desulfurisation (hydrogenolysis).** On the Raney nickel surface each C–S bond is broken and replaced by a C–H bond, using the hydrogen adsorbed in the porous catalyst. The former carbonyl carbon ends up as CH₂.
3. **Fate of the sulfur.** The sulfur is captured by the nickel, which is irreversibly converted into **nickel sulfide**. That is why a large excess of Raney nickel is needed.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not show the dithiol HS–CH₂CH₂–SH coming back after the Raney nickel step. Both C–S bonds of each sulfur are cleaved, the sulfur stays on the nickel as nickel sulfide, and the dithiol is used up.
</ExamTip>

<KeyPoint>
Step 2 is often written in exams simply as "H₂/Ni" or "Raney Ni". Raney nickel already contains adsorbed hydrogen from its activation, so extra H₂ gas is not always added.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Cyclohexanone to cyclohexane.**

<Eq ce="C6H10O ->[1. HSCH2CH2SH, H+][2. Raney Ni] C6H12" />

**2. Acetophenone to ethylbenzene.** The same overall change as Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner, but under nearly neutral conditions.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 ->[1. HSCH2CH2SH, H+][2. Raney Ni, H2] C6H5CH2CH3" />

**3. An aldehyde.** Benzaldehyde gives toluene: the CHO group becomes CH₃.

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO ->[1. HSCH2CH2SH, H+][2. Raney Ni] C6H5CH3" />

## Choosing between the three C=O to CH₂ methods

| Method | Reagents | Medium | Use when the substrate is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clemmensen | Zn-Hg, conc. HCl | strongly acidic | stable to acid |
| Wolff–Kishner | NH₂NH₂, KOH, glycol, heat | strongly basic | stable to base |
| Mozingo | dithiol, H⁺; then Raney Ni | nearly neutral | sensitive to both, or giving poor yields with both |

<ExamTip>
If a question shows a ketone first treated with "HS–CH₂CH₂–SH / H⁺" and then "Raney Ni", the answer is the alkane with C=O replaced by CH₂. Without the Raney nickel step, the thioacetal is simply a protected carbonyl group.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Large excess of Raney nickel** is needed for good yields, and separating the product from the catalyst can be tedious.
- **Other reducible groups** can be hydrogenated by the hydrogen-loaded nickel, so C=C bonds can also be reduced in step 2.
- **Thiols are foul-smelling and toxic**, which limits use on a large scale.
