## What is the Balz–Schiemann reaction?

The **Balz–Schiemann reaction** (also called the Schiemann reaction) converts an **arenediazonium salt into an aryl fluoride**. The diazonium chloride is treated with **fluoroboric acid (HBF₄)**, which precipitates the **arenediazonium tetrafluoroborate**, ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻. This dry salt is then **heated**, and it decomposes to the **aryl fluoride, nitrogen and boron trifluoride**. Benzenediazonium chloride gives **fluorobenzene**.

It is named after the German chemists Günther Balz and Günther Schiemann, and it remains the traditional route to fluoroarenes.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="ArN2^+ Cl^- + HBF4 -> ArN2^+ BF4^- v + HCl" />

<Eq ce="ArN2^+ BF4^- ->[\Delta] Ar-F + N2 ^ + BF3" />

In short, the route has two steps: HBF₄ gives C₆H₅N₂⁺BF₄⁻, and heat gives C₆H₅F + N₂ + BF₃.

## Mechanism

1. **Salt exchange.** BF₄⁻ replaces Cl⁻. Because ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ is insoluble in water, it precipitates and can be filtered and dried.
2. **Loss of nitrogen.** On heating, the C–N bond breaks and N₂ leaves, generating a highly unstable **aryl cation**, Ar⁺. No copper or other promoter is needed.
3. **Fluoride capture.** The aryl cation pulls F⁻ from the neighbouring BF₄⁻, giving Ar–F and leaving BF₃.

<Eq ce="ArN2^+ ->[\Delta] Ar^+ + N2" />

<Eq ce="Ar^+ + BF4^- -> ArF + BF3" />

<KeyPoint>
The Balz–Schiemann reaction is believed to go through an aryl cation (an SN1-like path), which is different from the copper-catalysed radical mechanism of the Sandmeyer reaction. The Organic Chemistry Portal points out that the exact mechanism is still not fully settled.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Fluorobenzene from aniline (three steps):**

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 ->[NaNO2, HCl][273-278 K] C6H5N2^+ Cl^- ->[HBF4] C6H5N2^+ BF4^- ->[\Delta] C6H5F" />

**2. 4-Fluorotoluene from p-toluidine** (a high-yield literature example, about 89%):

<Eq ce="CH3C6H4NH2 ->[1. NaNO2, HBF4][2. \Delta] CH3C6H4F + N2 + BF3" />

**3. Nitrobenzene from the same fluoroborate (NCERT):** heating ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ with aqueous sodium nitrite in the presence of copper replaces the diazonium group by –NO₂.

<Eq ce="C6H5N2^+ BF4^- ->[NaNO2, Cu][\Delta] C6H5NO2 + N2" />

<ExamTip>
If a scheme shows "HBF₄, then Δ" on a diazonium salt, the product is the aryl fluoride and the reaction is Balz–Schiemann. Remember the by-products N₂ and BF₃; both appear in assertion–reason and match-the-column questions.
</ExamTip>

| F | HBF₄, then heat | Balz–Schiemann |
| Cl, Br | CuX/HX | Sandmeyer |
| Cl, Br | Cu powder/HX | Gattermann |
| I | KI | (no special name) |

## Limitations and exceptions

- The dry diazonium salt releases a lot of heat and gas when it decomposes; in practice it is mixed with sand or slurried in a high-boiling paraffin and heated (up to about 200 °C) to control the decomposition.
- HBF₄ is a costly fluoride source. Modern variants use PF₆⁻ or SbF₆⁻ salts, or carry out diazotization in liquid HF, sometimes without isolating the diazonium salt.
- For alkyl fluorides, the usual exam method is halogen exchange (the [Swarts reaction](/reactions/swarts-reaction/)), not Balz–Schiemann.
