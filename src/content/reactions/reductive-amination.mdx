## What is reductive amination?

**Reductive amination** (reductive alkylation) converts an **aldehyde or ketone into an amine**. The carbonyl compound first condenses with **ammonia or an amine** to give an **imine** (Schiff base) and water; the C=N bond is then **reduced**, by H₂ over Ni/Pt/Pd, by LiAlH₄, or, most conveniently, by **sodium cyanoborohydride (NaBH₃CN)** under neutral to weakly acidic conditions. The closely related **reduction of oximes** gives primary amines.

<Eq ce="R1R2C=O + H2N-R3 <=> R1R2C=N-R3 + H2O" />
<Eq ce="R1R2C=N-R3 ->[H2/Ni, LiAlH4 or NaBH3CN] R1R2CH-NH-R3" />

## Mechanism

1. **Addition.** The nitrogen lone pair attacks the carbonyl carbon, giving a hemiaminal (carbinolamine), R₁R₂C(OH)–NHR₃.
2. **Dehydration.** Loss of water gives the imine (or, under mild acid, the iminium ion). This step is reversible; removing water pushes it forward.
3. **Reduction.** Hydride (or H₂ on the metal surface) adds to the C=N carbon and the nitrogen is protonated, giving the amine.

<KeyPoint>
The class of amine formed is set by the nitrogen partner: **NH₃ → 1° amine, R–NH₂ → 2° amine, R₂NH → 3° amine**. The carbon skeleton comes from the carbonyl compound, and the old C=O carbon becomes the C–N carbon.
</KeyPoint>

## Choosing the reducing agent

| Reducing agent | Selectivity | When used |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ with Ni, Pt or Pd | low (also reduces C=C, C=O) | industrial and one-pot; the classic textbook method |
| [LiAlH₄](/reagents/lithium-aluminium-hydride/) | low; reduces C=O too | isolated imine or oxime, reduced in a separate step |
| [NaBH₄](/reagents/sodium-borohydride/) | reduces both imines and carbonyls | imine formed first, then reduced |
| NaBH₃CN | high: reduces iminium ions at pH about 6–7, carbonyls only at lower pH | one-pot "direct" reductive amination (Borch) |
| NaBH(OAc)₃ | high, less toxic than NaBH₃CN | modern lab alternative |

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The reagent is sodium cyanoborohydride, NaBH₃CN (Na⁺[BH₃CN]⁻): one cyano group replaces one hydrogen of BH₄⁻. It is weaker than NaBH₄, which is why it leaves the aldehyde or ketone alone and reduces only the iminium ion. Work-up can release toxic HCN.
</ExamTip>

## Reduction of oximes

An aldehyde or ketone reacts with **hydroxylamine (NH₂OH)** to form an **oxime**, R₁R₂C=N–OH, with loss of water. Reducing the oxime cleaves the N–O bond and saturates C=N, giving a **primary amine**.

<Eq ce="R1R2C=O + NH2OH -> R1R2C=N-OH + H2O" />
<Eq ce="R1R2C=N-OH ->[Na/C2H5OH or H2/Ni or LiAlH4] R1R2CH-NH2" />

Sodium (in ethanol or another alcohol), sodium amalgam, catalytic hydrogenation and metal hydrides all work. With aldoximes, a mixture of primary and some secondary amine can form unless conditions are controlled. The same Na/ethanol system reduces nitriles in the [Mendius reduction](/reactions/mendius-reduction/).

**Ethanal to ethanamine (1° amine)**

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 + NH3 + H2 ->[Ni] C6H5CH(NH2)CH3 + H2O" />

**Propanone and methanamine to a 2° amine**

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C=O + CH3NH2 ->[NaBH3CN][pH 6-7] (CH3)2CH-NH-CH3" />

**Cyclohexanone oxime to cyclohexanamine**

<Eq ce="C6H10=N-OH ->[Na, C2H5OH] C6H11-NH2" />

## Limitations

- Imine formation is an equilibrium; with water present the imine can hydrolyse back to the carbonyl compound.
- Unselective reductants (H₂/catalyst, NaBH₄) can reduce the aldehyde or ketone to an alcohol, or other reducible groups, before the imine forms.
- Primary amines made from NH₃ can react again with the carbonyl compound, so some over-alkylation can occur.
- An oxime treated with acid instead of a reductant gives an amide by the [Beckmann rearrangement](/reactions/beckmann-rearrangement/), not an amine.
