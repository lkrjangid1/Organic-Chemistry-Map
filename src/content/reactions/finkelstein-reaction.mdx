## What is the Finkelstein reaction?

The **Finkelstein reaction** is a halogen exchange in which an **alkyl chloride or alkyl bromide is converted into an alkyl iodide** by treatment with **sodium iodide in dry acetone**. It follows an **SN2** mechanism. The reaction is an equilibrium, but it goes almost to completion because the NaCl or NaBr formed is insoluble in acetone and precipitates out. It is named after the German chemist Hans Finkelstein (1910).

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-X + NaI ->[dry acetone] R-I + NaX v \quad (X = Cl, Br)" />

Some books also allow DMSO as the polar aprotic solvent. The driving force, however, is the solubility difference in acetone: NaI dissolves, NaCl and NaBr do not.

## Mechanism

1. **Nucleophile.** Sodium iodide dissolves in acetone to give free iodide ions. Iodide is large, polarisable and a strong nucleophile in a polar aprotic solvent.
2. **Backside attack (one step).** I⁻ attacks the carbon bearing the halogen from the side opposite the C–X bond. The C–I bond forms while the C–X bond breaks, through a single five-coordinate transition state.
3. **Inversion.** As in every SN2 reaction, the configuration at the carbon is inverted (Walden inversion). A chiral secondary halide gives the iodide of opposite configuration.
4. **Precipitation.** Na⁺ and the departing Cl⁻ or Br⁻ form NaCl or NaBr, which crash out of acetone. Removing a product shifts the equilibrium to the right (Le Chatelier's principle).

<Eq ce="I- + CH3CH2-Br -> [I\cdots CH2(CH3)\cdots Br]^{-} -> I-CH2CH3 + Br-" />

<KeyPoint>
The Finkelstein reaction is SN2: rate depends on both R–X and I⁻, it works best on methyl and primary halides, and it inverts the configuration at a stereocentre.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Ethyl bromide to ethyl iodide** (the standard textbook example):

<Eq ce="CH3CH2Br + NaI ->[acetone] CH3CH2I + NaBr v" />

**Methyl chloride to methyl iodide:**

<Eq ce="CH3Cl + NaI ->[acetone] CH3I + NaCl v" />

**Benzyl chloride to benzyl iodide** (benzylic halides react very fast in SN2):

<Eq ce="C6H5CH2Cl + NaI ->[acetone] C6H5CH2I + NaCl v" />

## Reactivity order

Because the reaction is SN2, steric hindrance at the carbon controls the rate:

- **Fast:** methyl, primary, allyl, benzyl and α-halo carbonyl halides.
- **Slow:** secondary halides.
- **Practically no reaction:** tertiary, neopentyl, vinyl and aryl halides.

This difference is the basis of the **sodium iodide in acetone test** in qualitative analysis: a quick white precipitate (NaCl or NaBr) indicates a reactive primary, allylic or benzylic halide.

<ExamTip>
If a question shows R–Cl or R–Br with NaI in acetone, write R–I and NaX as a precipitate, and say "SN2, driven by precipitation of NaX". For a chiral 2° halide, show the iodide with inverted configuration.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Fluorides do not react as substrates.** A common mistake is to include F among the halogens replaced, but iodide cannot displace fluoride: the C–F bond is very strong and F⁻ is a poor leaving group. Only chlorides, bromides and sulfonates (tosylates, mesylates) are converted.
- **Tertiary and aryl halides** do not undergo SN2. Aryl chlorides and bromides need a copper(I) iodide catalyst (the so-called aromatic Finkelstein reaction), which is outside the JEE/NEET syllabus.
- **Making fluorides by exchange** uses metal fluorides instead (Swarts reaction, or KF in a polar aprotic solvent). Fluoride is a poor leaving group, so once the C–F bond forms it stays.
