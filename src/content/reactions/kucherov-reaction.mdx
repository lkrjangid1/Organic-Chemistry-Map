## What is the Kucherov reaction?

The **Kucherov reaction** is the **acid-catalysed hydration of an alkyne** in the presence of a mercury(II) salt, usually **HgSO₄ in dilute H₂SO₄**. Water adds across the C≡C bond to give an **enol**, which immediately tautomerises to a **carbonyl compound**. Acetylene gives **acetaldehyde**; all other alkynes give **ketones**, and terminal alkynes give **methyl ketones**. The Russian chemist Mikhail Kucherov described the hydration of acetylene with mercury salts in 1881.

For propyne, the product is **acetone**.

## Mechanism

1. **Attack by Hg²⁺.** The mercury(II) ion acts as the electrophile and adds to the triple bond. The positive charge develops on the **more substituted carbon**, as in Markovnikov addition.
2. **Water adds.** A water molecule bonds to that more substituted carbon and loses a proton, giving a mercury-containing enol.
3. **Mercury is replaced by H.** In the acidic medium the C–Hg bond is protonated off, releasing Hg²⁺ (so it acts as a catalyst) and leaving a neutral **enol**.
4. **Keto–enol tautomerism.** The enol, CH₃–C(OH)=CH₂, shifts its OH hydrogen to carbon and becomes the ketone. The keto form is far more stable, so the equilibrium lies almost entirely on the ketone side.

<Eq ce="CH3-C#CH ->[H2O, Hg^2+, H+] CH3-C(OH)=CH2 <=> CH3-CO-CH3" />

<KeyPoint>
Kucherov hydration follows **Markovnikov's rule**: the oxygen ends up on the more substituted carbon of the triple bond. That is why a terminal alkyne gives a **methyl ketone** and never an aldehyde.
</KeyPoint>

## Why is the mercury salt needed?

Adding water to a triple bond needs strong acid, and even then it is slow without help. The Hg²⁺ ion coordinates to the triple bond first and makes the attack by water fast; it is released again at the end, so only a catalytic amount is consumed in principle. In exam answers, the reagent is written as HgSO₄ with dilute H₂SO₄ over the arrow.

## Examples

**1. Acetylene to acetaldehyde** (the only aldehyde case):

<Eq ce="HC#CH + H2O ->[HgSO4, dil. H2SO4] [CH2=CH-OH] -> CH3-CHO" />

**2. Propyne to acetone**:

<Eq ce="CH3-C#CH + H2O ->[Hg^2+/H2SO4] CH3-CO-CH3" />

**3. 3-Phenylprop-1-yne to phenylacetone (1-phenylpropan-2-one):**

<Eq ce="C6H5CH2-C#CH + H2O ->[HgSO4, H2SO4] C6H5CH2-CO-CH3" />

**Internal alkynes.** But-2-yne is symmetrical and gives a single ketone, butan-2-one. An unsymmetrical internal alkyne such as pent-2-yne gives a **mixture** of pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one, because the two carbons of the triple bond are similarly substituted.

<ExamTip>
The reagents may be written in three equivalent ways: Hg²⁺ + H⁺, Hg²⁺/dil. H₂SO₄, or HgSO₄/H₂SO₄. All mean the same Kucherov conditions. To get an aldehyde from a terminal alkyne instead, use hydroboration–oxidation (anti-Markovnikov).
</ExamTip>

## Comparison: alkyne hydration methods

| Method | Reagents | Product from propyne |
|---|---|---|
| Kucherov (Markovnikov) | HgSO₄, dil. H₂SO₄ | Acetone (methyl ketone) |
| Hydroboration–oxidation (anti-Markovnikov) | Bulky borane, then H₂O₂/OH⁻ | Propanal (aldehyde) |

## Limitations and exceptions

- Acetylene is the exception that gives an aldehyde, not a ketone.
- Unsymmetrical internal alkynes give mixtures of two ketones, so the reaction is useful mainly for terminal and symmetrical alkynes.
- Mercury salts are toxic. Industrially, acetaldehyde made this way was replaced by the Wacker oxidation of ethylene.
