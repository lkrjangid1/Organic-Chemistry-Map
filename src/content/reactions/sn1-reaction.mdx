## What is the SN1 reaction?

The **SN1 reaction** (substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular) is a nucleophilic substitution that takes place in two steps. First the C–X bond of an alkyl halide breaks on its own to give a **planar carbocation** and a halide ion; then a nucleophile, usually the solvent, bonds to the carbocation. It is typical of **tertiary, allylic and benzylic halides** in **polar protic solvents** such as water, alcohols or aqueous acetone. Because only the substrate takes part in the slow step, the rate law is **rate = k[R–X]** (first order).

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R3C-X ->[\text{slow}] R3C+ + X- ->[Nu-][\text{fast}] R3C-Nu" />

## Mechanism

1. **Ionisation (slow, rate-determining).** The polarised C–X bond breaks heterolytically. The carbon becomes sp² hybridised and trigonal planar, with an empty p orbital above and below the plane. NCERT describes this step as the slowest one and reversible.
2. **Nucleophilic attack (fast).** The nucleophile can approach either lobe of the empty p orbital, so it adds from the top face or the bottom face.
3. **Deprotonation (when the nucleophile is neutral).** If water or an alcohol attacks, an oxonium ion forms first and loses H⁺ to a solvent molecule.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-Br <=>[\text{slow}] (CH3)3C+ + Br- ; (CH3)3C+ + H2O -> (CH3)3C-OH2+ ->[-H+] (CH3)3C-OH" />

<KeyPoint>
SN1 goes through a **carbocation**, so **rearrangement is possible**; it is a two- (or more-) step reaction; it needs a **weak, neutral nucleophile**, a **good leaving group** and a **polar protic solvent**. Reactivity is proportional to the stability of the carbocation: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃.
</KeyPoint>

## Stereochemistry

When the carbon bearing the halogen is a stereocentre, attack on both faces of the planar cation gives **both enantiomers**. NCERT's example is optically active 2-bromobutane, which gives (±)-butan-2-ol. In real reactions racemisation is usually incomplete: the leaving halide stays close to the cation for a moment as an ion pair and blocks the front face, so the inverted product is slightly favoured.

## Leaving group ability

In short: leaving-group ability ∝ 1/basicity ∝ stability of the anion. Weak bases leave easily, so I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻. Groups such as –N₂⁺ (leaving as N₂) and –OH₂⁺ (leaving as H₂O) are excellent leaving groups, which is why alcohols react by SN1 only after protonation.

## Examples

**1. Hydrolysis of tert-butyl bromide (NCERT).** Water acts as the nucleophile; the rate depends only on [tert-butyl bromide].

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-Br + H2O -> (CH3)3C-OH + HBr" />

**2. Solvolysis in ethanol.** 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane in warm ethanol gives 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane, with some alkene from the competing E1 path.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2C(CH3)2Br + C2H5OH -> CH3CH2C(CH3)2OC2H5 + HBr" />

**3. Rearrangement: neopentyl bromide in methanol.** The neopentyl carbon has no hydrogen on its neighbour, so a **1,2-methyl shift** converts the primary cation into a tertiary one. The product is 2-methoxy-2-methylbutane, not neopentyl methyl ether.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-CH2Br ->[CH3OH][\Delta] (CH3)2C(OCH3)-CH2CH3 + HBr" />

<ExamTip>
Whenever an SN1 or E1 question gives a 1° or 2° carbon next to a branched carbon, check for a hydride or methyl shift before writing the product. Neopentyl systems always involve a methyl shift.
</ExamTip>

## Allylic rearrangement: SN1′ and SN2′

Allylic halides form a **resonance-stabilised allyl cation** with positive charge shared between two carbons, so the nucleophile can bond at either end. Substitution at the far end of the double bond, with the π bond moving, is called an **allylic shift**: SN1′ when it goes through the cation and SN2′ when the nucleophile attacks the alkene carbon in one concerted step.

A classic question: 3-chloro-3-methylcyclohexene with water gives two alcohols. Attack at the tertiary end gives **1-methylcyclohex-2-en-1-ol**; attack at the other end gives **3-methylcyclohex-2-en-1-ol**, which has the more substituted, more stable C=C. In exam answers the first is labelled the kinetically controlled product and the second the thermodynamically controlled product. With OH⁻, exam answers show the SN2′ path giving 3-methylcyclohex-2-en-1-ol directly.

## Limitations and exceptions

- **E1 competes**, especially on heating or with basic nucleophiles; tertiary substrates give alkenes easily.
- **Methyl and primary halides** do not ionise (their cations are too unstable); they react by SN2.
- **Aryl and vinyl halides** do not undergo SN1, because aryl and vinyl cations are very unstable.
