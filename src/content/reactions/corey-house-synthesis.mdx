## What is the Corey–House synthesis?

The **Corey–House synthesis** is the coupling of a **lithium dialkylcuprate** (**Gilman reagent**, R₂CuLi) with an **alkyl halide** R′–X to form an alkane **R–R′**. Because only the cross product forms, it is the standard method for making **unsymmetrical alkanes**, including those with an odd number of carbon atoms that the Wurtz reaction cannot make cleanly. It was developed in the late 1960s by E. J. Corey and Herbert House (with Posner and Whitesides).

## General reaction (three steps)

The full sequence starts from an alkyl halide:

1. **Alkyllithium formation:** R–X with lithium metal in dry ether.

<Eq ce="R-X + 2 Li ->[dry ether] R-Li + LiX" />

2. **Gilman reagent formation:** two alkyllithiums with copper(I) halide (usually CuI).

<Eq ce="2 R-Li + CuI -> R2CuLi + LiI" />

3. **Coupling:** the cuprate with the second alkyl halide.

<Eq ce="R2CuLi + R'-X -> R-R' + R-Cu + LiX" />

R and R′ may be the same or different. One R group of the cuprate is not transferred and ends up as an organocopper species.

## Mechanism

1. **Electron transfer to make the carbanion equivalent.** Each lithium atom gives one electron (2Li → 2Li⁺ + 2e⁻). Two electrons reduce R–X to R⁻ and X⁻, which pair with Li⁺ as RLi and LiX.
2. **Transmetalation.** RLi transfers its alkyl group to copper, giving the dialkylcuprate anion [R–Cu–R]⁻ Li⁺.
3. **SN2-like attack.** The cuprate acts as a soft carbon nucleophile and attacks the carbon of R′–X from the back, displacing X⁻. Sources describe this as giving a copper(III) intermediate.
4. **Reductive elimination.** The Cu(III) species releases R–R′ and returns to copper(I).

<KeyPoint>
In exam answers the coupling step is usually written as **SN2**; strictly it is SN2-like: configuration at R′ is inverted for alkyl bromides and tosylates, and the reaction works best for unhindered (methyl, 1°) halides, exactly as expected for backside attack. With alkyl iodides some radical character (loss of configuration) is seen.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Pentane from bromomethane and 1-bromobutane** (an odd-carbon alkane):

<Eq ce="2 CH3Li + CuI -> (CH3)2CuLi + LiI" />
<Eq ce="(CH3)2CuLi + CH3(CH2)3Br -> CH3(CH2)3CH3 + CH3Cu + LiBr" />

2. **Propane from ethyl and methyl groups:**

<Eq ce="(C2H5)2CuLi + CH3I -> C2H5-CH3 + C2H5Cu + LiI" />

3. **Aryl groups work too:** lithium diphenylcuprate with iodomethane gives toluene.

<Eq ce="(C6H5)2CuLi + CH3I -> C6H5-CH3 + C6H5Cu + LiI" />

<ExamTip>
To design a Corey–House route, split the target alkane at any C–C bond. Make the cuprate from the part that may be branched or hindered, and use the **less hindered (primary or methyl) part as R′–X**.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Hindered halides fail:** tertiary and open-chain secondary R′–X give poor yields or elimination.
- **Chlorides are marginal;** bromides, iodides and tosylates are preferred.
- **One R is wasted** as R–Cu in the simple version, which matters when R is valuable.
- **Moisture must be excluded:** RLi and R₂CuLi are destroyed by water and alcohols, just like Grignard reagents.
- Organolithium or Grignard reagents alone do not couple well with alkyl halides (metal–halogen exchange, reduction and elimination compete); copper is what makes the coupling selective.
