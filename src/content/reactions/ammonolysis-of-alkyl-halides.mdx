## What is ammonolysis of alkyl halides?

**Ammonolysis of alkyl halides** (Hofmann ammonolysis) is the replacement of the halogen in an **alkyl or benzyl halide by an amino group** using ammonia. The halide is heated with **ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube at about 373 K**; ammonia acts as a nucleophile and the C–X bond is cleaved. The first product is an alkylammonium salt, and a strong base such as NaOH releases the free amine. Because the amine formed reacts further, the usual product is a **mixture of 1°, 2° and 3° amines and a quaternary ammonium salt**.

## Mechanism

1. **Nucleophilic substitution.** The lone pair on NH₃ attacks the carbon bearing the halogen and X⁻ leaves (SN2 for primary halides), giving R–NH₃⁺ X⁻.
2. **Proton transfer.** The ammonium ion passes a proton to excess NH₃ (or to added base), giving the free amine R–NH₂.
3. **Further alkylation.** R–NH₂ is also a nucleophile, often a better one than NH₃, so it attacks another R–X. The cycle repeats.

<Eq ce="NH3 ->[R-X] RNH2 ->[R-X] R2NH ->[R-X] R3N ->[R-X] R4N+X-" />

Releasing the free amine from its salt:

<Eq ce="R-NH3+X- + NaOH -> R-NH2 + H2O + NaX" />

<KeyPoint>
Ammonolysis gives a **mixture** of primary, secondary and tertiary amines plus a quaternary ammonium salt. A **large excess of NH₃** makes the primary amine the major product. Reactivity of halides: **RI > RBr > RCl**.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Chloroethane with ethanolic ammonia (NCERT)**

<Eq ce="C2H5Cl + NH3 ->[ethanol][373 K] C2H5NH3+Cl- ->[NaOH] C2H5NH2" />

**Benzyl chloride to N,N-dimethylbenzylamine.** Ammonolysis gives benzylamine, and two moles of chloromethane then alkylate it twice.

<Eq ce="C6H5CH2Cl ->[NH3] C6H5CH2NH2 ->[2 CH3Cl] C6H5CH2N(CH3)2 + 2HCl" />

**Exhaustive alkylation.** With excess methyl iodide, an amine is converted all the way to a quaternary ammonium salt, the starting point for [Hofmann elimination](/reactions/hofmann-elimination/).

<Eq ce="RNH2 + 3CH3I -> RN(CH3)3+I- + 2HI" />

## Ammonolysis of alcohols

Alcohols can also be converted into amines by heating them with ammonia over a **dehydrating catalyst** such as alumina or an aluminosilicate. The catalyst makes the OH group a better leaving group (it is lost as water). This is how most alkylamines are made industrially, because alcohols are cheaper than halides and no salt waste is formed.

<Eq ce="R-OH + NH3 ->[Al2O3][\Delta] R-NH2 + H2O" />

Like the halide route, it is **poorly selective**. Methanol and ammonia, for example, give methanamine, N-methylmethanamine and N,N-dimethylmethanamine together; the reactant ratio decides the proportions, and a large excess of ammonia favours the primary amine.

<Eq ce="CH3OH ->[NH3, catalyst] CH3NH2 + (CH3)2NH + (CH3)3N" />

<ExamTip>
For "which method gives a pure 1° amine from R–X?", choose [Gabriel phthalimide synthesis](/reactions/gabriel-phthalimide-synthesis/), not ammonolysis. For "one carbon more", go through the nitrile and reduce it; for "one carbon fewer", use Hofmann bromamide degradation.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Mixtures:** over-alkylation is the main drawback; in the lab, direct alkylation is mostly used to make tertiary amines and quaternary salts, where over-alkylation does not matter.
- **Aryl halides** (chlorobenzene) do not react with ammonia under these conditions, because the C–X bond is strengthened by resonance with the ring; only rings activated by strong electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂ ortho or para) undergo [nucleophilic aromatic substitution](/reactions/nucleophilic-aromatic-substitution/).
- **Tertiary halides** tend to undergo elimination with ammonia (a base) rather than substitution.
- Industrially, ethane-1,2-diamine is made by ammonolysis of 1,2-dichloroethane.
