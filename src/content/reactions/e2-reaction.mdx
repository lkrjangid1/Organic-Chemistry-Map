## What is the E2 reaction?

The **E2 reaction** (elimination, bimolecular) is a **one-step, concerted β-elimination**: a strong base pulls off a hydrogen from the β-carbon at the same moment as the leaving group leaves from the α-carbon, and the C=C bond forms in a single transition state. It is the usual way an **alkyl halide is converted into an alkene** with alcoholic KOH or sodium ethoxide on heating (dehydrohalogenation).

Both the substrate and the base appear in the slow step, so **rate = k[RX][Base]**.

## Mechanism

1. The base (B⁻) approaches a β-hydrogen that is **anti-periplanar** to the leaving group (H–C–C–X dihedral angle 180°).
2. In one step the C–H bond breaks, the C=C π bond forms and the C–X bond breaks; X leaves with its electron pair.
3. Products: alkene + B–H + X⁻.

<Eq ce="B- + H-CR2-CR2-X -> R2C=CR2 + B-H + X-" />

<KeyPoint>
E2 is an **anti elimination**, it is a **concerted one-step mechanism**, and its **major product is the Saytzeff alkene** (the more substituted alkene) when a small base is used.
</KeyPoint>

Evidence for the single step: second-order kinetics and a large primary deuterium isotope effect (a C–D bond breaks more slowly than C–H), because the C–H bond is broken in the rate-determining step.

## Examples

**1. Aqueous versus alcoholic KOH.** The same alkyl halide gives different products depending on the solvent:

<Eq ce="CH3CH2Cl ->[aq. KOH] CH3CH2OH (substitution)" />
<Eq ce="CH3CH2Cl ->[alc. KOH][\Delta] CH2=CH2 (elimination)" />

In exam answers the reason given is that in water OH⁻ is heavily solvated and acts mainly as a nucleophile, while alcoholic KOH contains ethoxide ions, a stronger base, so elimination wins. In practice some alkene also forms with aqueous base and some substitution with alcoholic base.

**2. 2-Bromopentane with sodium ethoxide.** Removal of H from C-3 gives pent-2-ene (Saytzeff, mostly the *E* isomer); removal from C-1 gives pent-1-ene (minor).

<Eq ce="CH3CHBrCH2CH2CH3 ->[NaOEt, EtOH][\Delta] CH3CH=CHCH2CH3 (major) + CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3 (minor)" />

**3. Small base versus bulky base.** 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane with NaOEt gives mainly 2-methylbut-2-ene (Saytzeff). With bulky potassium tert-butoxide the more exposed CH₃ hydrogens are removed and 2-methylbut-1-ene (Hofmann product) becomes major.

## Stereospecificity

Because H and X must be anti, the configuration of the substrate fixes the geometry of the alkene. *meso*-1,2-Dibromo-1,2-diphenylethane gives only (*E*)-1-bromo-1,2-diphenylethene, while the (1*R*,2*R*) or (1*S*,2*S*) isomer gives only the (*Z*) alkene.

<Eq ce="(1R,2R)-PhCHBr-CHBrPh ->[KOEt][\Delta] (Z)-PhCBr=CHPh + HBr" />

In cyclohexanes the H and leaving group must both be **axial (trans-diaxial)**. If no such β-H exists, E2 cannot give the Saytzeff alkene and may give the less substituted one instead.

## Saytzeff or Hofmann?

| Condition | Major alkene |
|---|---|
| Small strong base (OH⁻, EtO⁻, MeO⁻), halide leaving group | Saytzeff (more substituted) |
| Bulky base (KOtBu, Et₃N) | Hofmann (less substituted) |
| Leaving group F, –NR₃⁺, –SR₂⁺ | Hofmann |
| Crowded substrate near the more substituted β-carbon | Hofmann |

<ExamTip>
For an E2 question, first find every β-hydrogen that can be anti-periplanar to the leaving group, then apply Saytzeff unless the base is bulky or the leaving group is F or an onium group. For cyclohexyl halides, draw the chair and look only at axial β-hydrogens.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Competition with SN2:** many bases (OH⁻, EtO⁻) are also nucleophiles. Elimination is favoured by a 2° or 3° substrate, a stronger or bulkier base, an alcoholic solvent and higher temperature.
- **Primary halides** with small bases give mainly SN2; with KOtBu they give mainly alkene.
- **Fluorides** react slowly because F⁻ is a poor leaving group, and they tend to give Hofmann products.
