## What is the Wolff–Kishner reduction?

The **Wolff–Kishner reduction** converts the **carbonyl group of an aldehyde or ketone into a CH₂ group**, so the carbonyl compound becomes an **alkane** (or alkylbenzene). The carbonyl compound is first condensed with **hydrazine** to a hydrazone, which is then heated with a strong base such as **KOH** in a high-boiling solvent like **ethylene glycol**; nitrogen gas is given off. It was reported by Nikolai Kishner in 1911 and Ludwig Wolff in 1912.

## General reaction

In short: R–CO–R treated with N₂H₄ and KOH, heated above 180 °C with ethylene glycol, giving R–CH₂–R.

<Eq ce="R-CO-R ->[NH2NH2, KOH][ethylene glycol, \Delta] R-CH2-R + N2 + H2O" />

## Mechanism

1. **Hydrazone formation.** Hydrazine adds to the C=O and water is lost, giving the hydrazone R₂C=N–NH₂. This is the usual addition–elimination of an ammonia derivative to a carbonyl group.
2. **Deprotonation of N–H.** The base removes a proton from the terminal NH₂. The negative charge is shared between nitrogen and carbon by resonance. Wikipedia describes this solvent-assisted deprotonation as the rate-determining step.
3. **Protonation on carbon.** The solvent puts a proton on carbon, giving R₂CH–N=N–H (a diimide).
4. **Loss of N₂.** A second deprotonation on nitrogen is followed by loss of N₂, leaving a carbanion R₂CH⁻.
5. **Protonation.** The carbanion takes a proton from the solvent to give the alkane R₂CH₂.

<Eq ce="R2C=O + H2N-NH2 -> R2C=N-NH2 + H2O" />
<Eq ce="R2C=N-NH2 ->[OH-][\Delta] R2CH- + N2 ->[H2O] R2CH2" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
After N₂ leaves, the carbon is left with the bonding pair it shared with nitrogen, so the intermediate is a **carbanion (R₂CH⁻)**, not a carbocation. The solvent (glycol or water) then protonates it. Draw a negative charge on carbon at this step.
</ExamTip>

<KeyPoint>
The driving force is the release of nitrogen gas: forming the very stable N≡N triple bond makes the decomposition of the hydrazone irreversible.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Acetophenone to ethylbenzene.**

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 ->[NH2NH2, KOH][ethylene glycol, \Delta] C6H5CH2CH3 + N2 + H2O" />

**2. Propiophenone to propylbenzene.** This is the standard second step after Friedel–Crafts acylation of benzene with propanoyl chloride, and it gives a straight-chain alkylbenzene that direct alkylation cannot.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH2CH3 ->[NH2NH2, KOH][\Delta] C6H5CH2CH2CH3" />

**3. An aldehyde.** Cyclopropanecarbaldehyde is reduced to methylcyclopropane under the same conditions; the CHO group becomes CH₃.

## Clemmensen versus Wolff–Kishner

| Feature | Clemmensen | Wolff–Kishner |
|---|---|---|
| Reagents | Zn-Hg, conc. HCl | NH₂NH₂, KOH, glycol, heat |
| Medium | strongly acidic | strongly basic |
| Use when the substrate is | base-sensitive | acid-sensitive |
| Overall change | C=O to CH₂ | C=O to CH₂ |

<ExamTip>
If the molecule also contains an acid-sensitive group, choose Wolff–Kishner; if it contains a base-sensitive group (for example an ester that hot KOH would hydrolyse), choose Clemmensen. In both, an aromatic ring attached to the C=O is left untouched.
</ExamTip>

## Huang–Minlon modification

In 1946 Huang Minlon simplified the procedure: the carbonyl compound is refluxed in 85% hydrazine hydrate with about three equivalents of NaOH in a glycol solvent, then water and excess hydrazine are distilled off and the temperature is allowed to rise to about 200 °C. Removing the water speeds up the reaction and raises the yield; this one-pot version is what most textbooks now describe.

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Base-sensitive substrates** (esters, some halides) do not survive the hot, strongly basic conditions.
- **Highly hindered ketones** may not form the hydrazone at all.
- **Azine formation.** The hydrazone can react with a second molecule of the carbonyl compound to give an azine; excluding water suppresses this.
- **Side reactions.** α-Substituted ketones can eliminate to alkenes, strained rings next to the C=O may open, and in α,β-unsaturated ketones the C=C can migrate.

## Related reactions

- [Wittig reaction](/reactions/wittig-reaction/) also removes the carbonyl oxygen but forms a C=C bond instead of CH₂.
