## What is the Gabriel phthalimide synthesis?

The **Gabriel phthalimide synthesis** converts a **primary alkyl halide into a primary amine** without contamination by secondary or tertiary amines. **Phthalimide** is first turned into **potassium phthalimide** with ethanolic KOH; the imide anion displaces halide from R–X in an **SN2** reaction, and the resulting **N-alkylphthalimide** is then hydrolysed with aqueous alkali (or cleaved with hydrazine) to release **R–NH₂**. It is named after Siegmund Gabriel (1887).

The phthalimide anion works as a protected, "one-use" version of NH₂⁻: once it carries one alkyl group, it has no N–H left and cannot be alkylated again.

## Mechanism

1. **Deprotonation.** The N–H of phthalimide is acidic because the anion is stabilised by both flanking C=O groups. Ethanolic KOH removes the proton, giving potassium phthalimide.

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2NH + KOH -> C6H4(CO)2N^-K+ + H2O" />

2. **N-Alkylation (SN2).** The nitrogen anion attacks the carbon of the alkyl halide from the back side and halide leaves, giving the N-alkylphthalimide. This needs an unhindered (primary, benzylic or allylic) carbon.

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2N^-K+ + R-X ->[\Delta] C6H4(CO)2N-R + KX" />

3. **Release of the amine.** Heating with aqueous NaOH or KOH attacks both C=O groups (nucleophilic acyl substitution), breaking the two C–N bonds. The primary amine is set free and the phthalic acid part is left as its salt.

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2N-R + 2NaOH ->[\Delta] R-NH2 + C6H4(COONa)2" />

<KeyPoint>
Only primary amines are formed. The N-alkylphthalimide has no N–H, so there is no over-alkylation, which is the main problem with [ammonolysis of alkyl halides](/reactions/ammonolysis-of-alkyl-halides/).
</KeyPoint>

## Ways to free the amine

| Cleavage step | Reagent | By-product | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkaline hydrolysis (NCERT) | aqueous NaOH or KOH, heat | disodium/dipotassium phthalate | the version asked in board and NEET papers |
| Acid hydrolysis | aqueous acid, heat | phthalic acid | amine comes out as its ammonium salt; often low yields |
| Ing–Manske procedure | hydrazine (N₂H₄), ethanol | phthalhydrazide (precipitates) | milder; common in the lab |

## Examples

**Ethanamine from bromoethane**

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2N^-K+ ->[CH3CH2Br][\Delta] C6H4(CO)2N-CH2CH3 ->[NaOH(aq)] CH3CH2NH2" />

**Benzylamine from benzyl chloride.** Benzylic halides react well by SN2, so C₆H₅CH₂Cl gives C₆H₅CH₂NH₂.

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2N^-K+ ->[C6H5CH2Cl] C6H4(CO)2N-CH2C6H5 ->[N2H4] C6H5CH2NH2" />

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Aromatic amines (aniline) cannot be made.** Aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution with the phthalimide anion: the C–X bond has partial double-bond character from resonance with the ring, and backside attack is impossible. Aniline is made by reducing nitrobenzene instead.
- **tert-Alkyl primary amines such as (CH₃)₃C–NH₂ cannot be made**, because tertiary halides do not undergo SN2. Secondary halides also react poorly unless especially reactive.
- **Secondary and tertiary amines are never products.** Gabriel synthesis is a method for 1° amines only.
- The hydrolysis step needs fairly harsh heating; this is why the hydrazine work-up is often preferred in practice.

<ExamTip>
If a question asks for a primary amine "free from 2° and 3° amines" starting from an alkyl halide, the answer is Gabriel synthesis. If the target is aniline, or a tert-alkyl amine, Gabriel is the wrong choice.
</ExamTip>
