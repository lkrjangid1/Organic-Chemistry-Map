## What is the Hunsdiecker reaction?

The **Hunsdiecker reaction** (also called the **Borodin** or **Hunsdiecker–Borodin reaction**) converts the **silver salt of a carboxylic acid** into an **alkyl halide** by heating it with **bromine** (or chlorine) in dry carbon tetrachloride. Carbon dioxide is lost, so the product has **one carbon atom fewer** than the acid. It is both a **decarboxylation** and a **halogenation**, and it runs by a **free-radical** mechanism.

<Eq ce="R-COOAg + Br2 ->[CCl4][\Delta] R-Br + CO2 + AgBr" />

Alexander Borodin, better known as a composer, first made methyl bromide this way from silver acetate in 1861; Cläre and Heinz Hunsdiecker developed it into a general method in the 1930s.

## Mechanism

1. **Silver salt.** The acid is first converted into its silver salt, R–COOAg. (It is sometimes written as R–COOH with AgOH; in practice a silver(I) base is used, and the salt must be pure and dry.)
2. **Acyl hypobromite.** The silver salt reacts with Br₂; AgBr precipitates and an **acyl hypobromite**, R–CO–O–Br, is formed.
3. **Homolysis.** The weak O–Br bond breaks on heating or in light, giving a **carboxyl radical** R–CO–O• and Br•.
4. **Decarboxylation.** The carboxyl radical loses CO₂, leaving an **alkyl radical** R•.
5. **Halogen transfer.** R• combines with a bromine atom (or takes Br from Br₂ or the hypobromite, continuing the chain) to give R–Br.

<Eq ce="R-COOAg + Br2 -> R-CO-O-Br + AgBr" />
<Eq ce="R-CO-O-Br -> R-CO-O^{.} + Br^{.}" />
<Eq ce="R-CO-O^{.} -> R^{.} + CO2" />
<Eq ce="R^{.} + Br^{.} -> R-Br" />

<KeyPoint>
Key facts: (1) it is a **decarboxylation** reaction, (2) it is a **step-down** reaction (one carbon fewer), and (3) the yield of halide follows **1° > 2° > 3°**.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Silver propanoate to bromoethane**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2COOAg + Br2 ->[CCl4][\Delta] CH3CH2Br + CO2 + AgBr" />

**2. Silver acetate to bromomethane (Borodin's original example)**

<Eq ce="CH3COOAg + Br2 ->[CCl4][\Delta] CH3Br + CO2 + AgBr" />

**3. Silver octanoate to 1-bromoheptane**

<Eq ce="CH3(CH2)6COOAg + Br2 ->[CCl4][\Delta] CH3(CH2)5CH2Br + CO2 + AgBr" />

<ExamTip>
To go down a homologous series, think "acid → silver salt → Br₂/CCl₄". Count carbons: the alkyl bromide always has one carbon fewer than the acid. Compare with Kolbe electrolysis, where two decarboxylated alkyl groups join, and with the Hofmann bromamide degradation, which also removes one carbon but gives an amine.
</ExamTip>

## Variations

- **Simonini reaction (iodine):** with silver salt : I₂ = 1 : 1 an alkyl iodide forms; with 2 : 1 an ester RCOOR is the main product, and 3 : 2 gives a 1 : 1 mixture of both.
- **Mercuric oxide (Cristol–Firth) modification:** the free acid with red HgO and Br₂ avoids the need to make the pure, dry silver salt.
- **Kochi reaction:** lead(IV) acetate with LiCl (or LiBr) carries out the same halodecarboxylation.

## Limitations and exceptions

- The silver salt must be **pure and dry**, which is often hard to achieve; this is the main practical drawback.
- **Protic solvents** (water, alcohols) decompose the acyl hypobromite, so a dry inert solvent such as CCl₄ is used.
- **Tertiary** acids give poor yields (1° > 2° > 3°).
- Acids that contain a **C=C** may add the hypobromite across the double bond instead, and **α,β-unsaturated** acids tend to polymerise, giving low yields.
- Iodine changes the outcome (ester formation), as described above. The method is used for X = Cl or Br.
