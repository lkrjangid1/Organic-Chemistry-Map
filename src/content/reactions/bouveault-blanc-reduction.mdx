## What is the Bouveault–Blanc reduction?

The **Bouveault–Blanc reduction** converts an **ester (R–COOR′) into a primary alcohol (R–CH₂OH)** plus the alcohol R′OH, using **sodium metal in absolute ethanol**. Sodium supplies electrons one at a time and ethanol supplies the protons, so it is a **dissolving-metal reduction**, a close relative of the [Birch reduction](/reactions/birch-reduction/). It was reported by Louis Bouveault and Gustave Louis Blanc in 1903, who reduced ethyl oleate to oleyl alcohol.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-COOR' ->[Na, C2H5OH] R-CH2OH + R'-OH" />

Written with the sodium salts that form before work-up, four sodium atoms are used per ester:

<Eq ce="RCOOR' + 4Na + 2C2H5OH -> RCH2ONa + R'ONa + 2C2H5ONa" />

Adding water then gives the free alcohols R–CH₂OH and R′–OH.

## Mechanism

Sodium transfers electrons at the metal surface, which is why finely divided sodium (or a sodium dispersion) is used. The usual textbook sequence is:

1. **First electron.** Na gives one electron to the ester C=O, forming a **radical anion** (ketyl).
2. **Protonation.** Ethanol protonates the oxygen anion, leaving a carbon radical.
3. **Second electron.** Another Na reduces the radical to a carbanion, which ethanol protonates. The result is a hemiacetal-type intermediate.
4. **Loss of R′O⁻.** This intermediate expels the alkoxide R′O⁻, giving an **aldehyde**.
5. **Two more electrons and two protons.** The aldehyde is reduced in the same way (electron, proton, electron, proton) to the primary alcohol.

<KeyPoint>
Four electrons (four Na) and four hydrogens are added overall: R–CO–OR′ + 4[H] → R–CH₂OH + R′OH. The ethanol is the proton donor; if it is absent, the radical anions **couple** instead, which is the acyloin condensation.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **The original 1903 example.** Ethyl oleate gives oleyl alcohol; the C=C double bond in the chain survives.
<Eq ce="C17H33COOC2H5 ->[Na, C2H5OH] C17H33CH2OH + C2H5OH" />

2. **A simple ester.** Ethyl butanoate gives butan-1-ol and ethanol.
<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH2COOC2H5 ->[Na, C2H5OH] CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + C2H5OH" />

3. **An aryl ether ester.** Ethyl phenoxyacetate gives 2-phenoxyethanol.
<Eq ce="C6H5OCH2COOC2H5 ->[Na, C2H5OH] C6H5OCH2CH2OH + C2H5OH" />

## Bouveault–Blanc vs LiAlH₄

| Point | Bouveault–Blanc (Na/EtOH) | [LiAlH₄](/reagents/lithium-aluminium-hydride/) |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Dissolving metal, single-electron transfer | Hydride transfer |
| Ester product | 1° alcohol + R′OH | 1° alcohol + R′OH |
| Solvent | Absolute ethanol | Dry ether or THF |
| Cost | Cheap; used industrially | Expensive; laboratory reagent |
| Isolated C=C | Not reduced | Not reduced |

Before metal hydrides were developed, this was the only practical way to reduce esters to alcohols. To stop at the aldehyde instead, use [DIBAL-H](/reagents/dibal-h/) at −78 °C.

<ExamTip>
Na/C₂H₅OH acting on an ester → **two alcohols**. Write the acyl carbon as CH₂OH and release the alkoxy group as its alcohol. Do not confuse it with the [Claisen condensation](/reactions/claisen-condensation/), where sodium ethoxide (a base, not sodium metal) joins two ester molecules into a β-keto ester.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Water must be excluded.** Wet ethanol consumes sodium and gives low yields.
- **Excess sodium** is needed, because some reacts with ethanol to give H₂.
- **No proton donor, no reduction:** in an aprotic solvent, the ester couples to an acyloin instead.
- Modern variants, such as alkali metal absorbed on silica gel, make the reaction safer while keeping its yields close to those of hydride reagents.
