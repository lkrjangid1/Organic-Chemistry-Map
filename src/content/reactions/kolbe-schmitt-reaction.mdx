## What is the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction?

The **Kolbe–Schmitt reaction**, called **Kolbe's reaction** in NCERT, is the **carboxylation of phenol**. Phenol is first converted to **sodium phenoxide** with NaOH. The phenoxide is then heated with **carbon dioxide under pressure** (about 125 °C and 100 atm), and the product is acidified. A **–COOH group enters ortho to the OH**, giving **salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid)**, the starting material for aspirin. It is named after Hermann Kolbe (1860) and Rudolf Schmitt (1885).

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5OH ->[NaOH] C6H5O^-Na+ ->[CO2][125 °C, 100 atm] 2-HOC6H4COO^-Na+ ->[H+] 2-HOC6H4COOH" />

Industrially, sodium phenoxide is heated with CO₂ at about **100 atm and 115–125 °C**, and the sodium salicylate is acidified with sulphuric acid. For exam answers the essential points are: phenoxide (not phenol), CO₂, heat and pressure, then acid.

## Mechanism

1. **Phenoxide formation.** NaOH removes the phenolic proton. The negative charge on oxygen is delocalised into the ring, so the **ortho and para carbons become strongly nucleophilic**.
2. **Attack on CO₂.** CO₂ is a weak electrophile. The electron-rich ring carbon (mainly **ortho**) attacks the carbon of CO₂, giving a cyclohexadienone intermediate carrying a –COO⁻ group.
3. **Rearomatisation.** A proton shift (tautomerisation) from the ring carbon to oxygen restores the aromatic ring, giving **sodium salicylate**.
4. **Acidification.** Dilute acid protonates the carboxylate, giving **salicylic acid**.

<Eq ce="C6H5O- + CO2 -> \text{dienone intermediate} ->[\text{tautomerisation}] 2-HOC6H4COO-" />

<KeyPoint>
The reaction works only on the **phenoxide ion**, not on phenol itself. NCERT stresses that phenoxide is far more reactive than phenol towards electrophilic substitution, which is why even a weak electrophile like CO₂ can react.
</KeyPoint>

## Sodium vs potassium phenoxide

| Base used | Phenoxide | Main product |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH | Sodium phenoxide | **Salicylic acid** (ortho), the NCERT product |
| KOH | Potassium phenoxide | Both isomers; **4-hydroxybenzoic acid** (para) favoured, used industrially for the para acid |
| Na or K phenoxide in DMSO | Dissolved phenoxide, about 100 °C | Mainly para (up to about 97 : 3) |

<ExamTip>
For NCERT/NEET questions, "phenol + NaOH, then CO₂, then H⁺" always gives **salicylic acid**. For JEE, remember the twist: the potassium salt pushes the carboxyl group to the **para** position, giving p-hydroxybenzoic acid.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

**1. Phenol → salicylic acid (NCERT):**

<Eq ce="C6H5OH ->[1. NaOH][2. CO2, \Delta, pressure] 2-HOC6H4COONa ->[H3O+] 2-HOC6H4COOH" />

**2. Potassium phenoxide → 4-hydroxybenzoic acid:**

<Eq ce="C6H5OK + CO2 ->[\Delta, pressure] 4-HOC6H4COOK ->[H3O+] 4-HOC6H4COOH" />

**3. Salicylic acid → aspirin (follow-up step):**

<Eq ce="2-HOC6H4COOH + (CH3CO)2O ->[H+] 2-CH3COOC6H4COOH + CH3COOH" />

**4. 2-Naphthol → 3-hydroxy-2-naphthoic acid:** the same carboxylation is used industrially on naphthols; here the position of the new –COOH depends on temperature.

## Limitations

- The classical process is a solid–gas reaction of the phenoxide salt with CO₂; solvent versions (DMSO, DMF) run at lower temperature but favour the para acid.
- High pressure and heat are needed because CO₂ is a weak electrophile.
- Strongly electron-withdrawing groups on the ring reduce the nucleophilicity of the phenoxide.
- The ortho/para ratio depends on the metal ion, temperature and solvent.
