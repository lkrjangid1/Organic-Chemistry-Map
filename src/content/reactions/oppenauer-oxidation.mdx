## What is the Oppenauer oxidation?

The **Oppenauer oxidation** is a gentle method for oxidising a **secondary alcohol to a ketone** with an **aluminium alkoxide**, classically **aluminium tert-butoxide**, Al(OC(CH₃)₃)₃, or **aluminium isopropoxide**, in the presence of a **large excess of acetone**. Acetone acts as the hydride acceptor and is reduced to propan-2-ol. The reaction is the reverse of the **Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley (MPV) reduction** and was introduced by R. V. Oppenauer in 1937 for steroid ketones.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R2CH-OH + (CH3)2C=O <=>[Al(OCMe3)3] R2C=O + (CH3)2CH-OH" />

The alcohol is oxidised and acetone is reduced; aluminium shuttles the hydride between them. Because all steps are reversible, excess acetone is used to drive the equilibrium to the right.

<KeyPoint>
Oppenauer oxidation acts only on the alcohol group (केवल alcohol को ऑक्सीकृत करता है). C=C bonds, amines and sulfides survive, and there is no over-oxidation to carboxylic acids.
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism

1. **Alkoxide exchange.** The substrate alcohol binds to aluminium and loses its proton, giving an aluminium alkoxide of the substrate, R₂CH–O–Al, and releasing tert-butanol.
2. **Coordination of acetone.** Acetone coordinates to the same aluminium through its carbonyl oxygen, which makes its carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
3. **Hydride shift.** The hydrogen on the carbinol carbon moves as a hydride to the acetone carbonyl carbon through a **six-membered cyclic transition state** (Al, two O, two C and the H).
4. **Release.** The new ketone, R₂C=O, leaves; the acetone has become an isopropoxide on aluminium, which exchanges with more substrate alcohol to continue the cycle.

<Eq ce="R2CH-O-Al + (CH3)2C=O -> R2C=O + (CH3)2CH-O-Al" />

## Examples

**1. Cyclohexanol to cyclohexanone.**

<Eq ce="C6H11OH + (CH3)2CO ->[Al(OCMe3)3][\Delta] C6H10O + (CH3)2CHOH" />

**2. Butan-2-ol to butan-2-one.** Any simple secondary alcohol behaves the same way (propan-2-ol would just give acetone, the same compound as the acceptor):

<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 ->[Al(OCMe3)3, acetone] CH3COCH2CH3" />

**3. Steroids.** Pregnenolone is oxidised to **progesterone**: the 3-OH becomes a ketone and the neighbouring C=C moves into conjugation with it, giving an α,β-unsaturated ketone. Codeine is similarly converted into codeinone.

<ExamTip>
If an exam question shows a secondary alcohol with "Al(O-t-Bu)₃, acetone" (or "Al(OiPr)₃, acetone"), write the ketone and leave every C=C in place. If the reagent is "Al(OiPr)₃, propan-2-ol" with a ketone, it is the reverse reaction, MPV reduction.
</ExamTip>

## Oppenauer oxidation vs MPV reduction

| Feature | Oppenauer oxidation | MPV reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | 2° alcohol | aldehyde or ketone |
| Product | ketone | alcohol |
| Excess reagent | acetone (hydride acceptor) | propan-2-ol (hydride donor) |
| Catalyst | Al(OtBu)₃ or Al(OiPr)₃ | Al(OiPr)₃ |
| Key step | hydride transfer via six-membered transition state | same |

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Primary alcohols** react, but the aldehydes formed undergo **aldol condensation** under the basic alkoxide conditions, so the method is mainly used for secondary alcohols. Aldehydes without α-H can instead give esters by the Tishchenko reaction.
- **Allylic alcohols** may show migration of the double bond into conjugation with the new ketone.
- **Tertiary alcohols** have no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon and are not oxidised.
- Substrates with a Lewis-basic nitrogen can tie up aluminium; the Woodward modification uses potassium tert-butoxide and benzophenone instead.
- Secondary alcohols react faster than primary ones, so some chemoselectivity is possible.
