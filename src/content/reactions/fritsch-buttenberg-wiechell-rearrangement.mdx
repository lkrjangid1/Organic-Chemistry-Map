## What is the Fritsch–Buttenberg–Wiechell rearrangement?

The **Fritsch–Buttenberg–Wiechell (FBW) rearrangement** converts a **1,1-disubstituted 2-haloalkene (R₁R₂C=CH–X) into a disubstituted alkyne (R₁–C≡C–R₂)** when it is treated with a **strong base**. In the classic version a **1,1-diaryl-2-bromoalkene** is heated with an alkoxide and gives a **1,2-diarylalkyne**; one aryl group moves across the double bond in a 1,2-shift. It was reported by Paul Fritsch, Wilhelm Buttenberg and Heinrich Wiechell in 1894.

## Mechanism

1. **Removal of the vinylic H.** The strong base pulls off the H on the carbon that carries the halogen, giving a **vinyl carbanion** (with Li or Mg it is a metal carbenoid).
2. **α-Elimination.** The halide leaves from that same carbon. This leaves an electron-deficient vinylidene carbon (a vinylidene carbene or carbenoid, R₁R₂C=C:).
3. **1,2-Shift.** A group on the neighbouring carbon (R₁, normally aryl) migrates to the electron-deficient carbon, and the C=C becomes a C≡C. The group **trans to the leaving halogen** is the one that migrates, and in carbenoids the shift happens as the halide leaves.

<Eq ce="R1R2C=CH-Br ->[\text{base}][-H+] R1R2C=C^{-}-Br ->[-Br-] R1R2C=C: ->[\text{1,2-shift of }R1] R1-C#C-R2" />

<KeyPoint>
The FBW rearrangement is an **α-elimination** (H and X leave from the same carbon) followed by a 1,2-shift, not a normal β-elimination. That is why a substituent ends up on the other alkyne carbon.
</KeyPoint>

## How it differs from ordinary dehydrohalogenation

In a normal elimination that makes an alkyne, H and X leave from **adjacent** carbons and no group moves. In the FBW rearrangement the carbon carrying X also carries the only H, and the other alkene carbon has two substituents and no H. A normal β-elimination is therefore impossible, so the molecule loses H and X from the **same** carbon and repairs the electron-deficient centre by shifting a group. The tell-tale sign in a product is that the two groups that started on one carbon finish on different carbons of the triple bond.

## Examples

1. **2-Bromo-1,1-diphenylethene → diphenylacetylene** (classic case):

<Eq ce="(C6H5)2C=CHBr ->[\text{RO-Na+}][\Delta] C6H5-C#C-C6H5 + HBr" />

2. **Mixed aryl substrates:** for (Ar)(Ar')C=CHBr the product is the unsymmetrical alkyne Ar–C≡C–Ar' whichever aryl migrates, because both end up on the triple bond.

3. **Modern alkyne and polyyne synthesis:** 1,1-dibromoalkenes made from aldehydes or ketones (Corey–Fuchs type) are treated with n-butyllithium at low temperature. The lithium carbenoid formed by Li–Br exchange rearranges, and when an alkynyl group migrates this builds conjugated di- and triynes. The reaction also works with alkyl groups, not only aryl groups.

<ExamTip>
Recognise the pattern: a vinyl halide with H on the halogen-bearing carbon and two groups on the other carbon, plus a strong base. Delete H and X from the same carbon, move one group across, and write the alkyne R₁–C≡C–R₂.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- The halogen-bearing carbon must carry an H (or be converted into a metal carbenoid by halogen–metal exchange).
- Geometry matters: a group trans to the leaving halogen migrates well; the cis group does not.
- Free vinylidene carbenes are not always involved: with magnesium carbenoids the two geometric isomers react differently, which points to the shift happening from the carbenoid itself.
- In exam questions NaNH₂ is sometimes drawn as the base as well; the classic literature conditions are alkoxide with heat or an organolithium.
