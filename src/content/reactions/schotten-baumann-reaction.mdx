## What is the Schotten–Baumann reaction?

The **Schotten–Baumann reaction** is the **acylation of an amine with an acid chloride in the presence of aqueous base**, giving an **amide**. The same conditions also turn alcohols and phenols into **esters**. When the acid chloride is **benzoyl chloride (C₆H₅COCl)** the reaction is called **benzoylation**; for example, aniline shaken with benzoyl chloride and aqueous NaOH gives **benzanilide**. Carl Schotten and Eugen Baumann described it in 1883.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-NH2 + R'-COCl ->[aq. NaOH] R-NH-CO-R' + HCl" />

The HCl does not accumulate: the base converts it into NaCl and water. NCERT carries out the same acylation with **pyridine**, a base stronger than the amine, for the same purpose.

## Mechanism

The reaction is a **nucleophilic acyl substitution** (addition followed by elimination).

1. **Attack.** The lone pair on nitrogen attacks the carbonyl carbon of the acid chloride, giving a tetrahedral intermediate.
2. **Elimination.** The C=O re-forms and chloride leaves.
3. **Deprotonation.** The base removes the proton from nitrogen, giving the neutral amide and neutralising HCl.

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 + C6H5COCl -> C6H5NH-CO-C6H5 + HCl" />
<Eq ce="HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O" />

<KeyPoint title="Why the base matters">
Without base, the HCl formed would protonate unreacted amine to R–NH₃⁺, which has no free lone pair and cannot attack. The base keeps the amine free and drives the equilibrium towards the amide.
</KeyPoint>

In the classic two-phase set-up, the amine, the acid chloride and the amide stay in the organic layer while the base stays in water and mops up the acid.

## Examples

**1. Benzoylation of aniline.**

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 + C6H5COCl ->[aq. NaOH] C6H5NHCOC6H5 + NaCl + H2O" />

**2. Benzoylation of methanamine** (the NCERT example): the product is N-methylbenzamide.

<Eq ce="CH3NH2 + C6H5COCl -> C6H5CONHCH3 + HCl" />

**3. Benzoylation of phenol.** In NaOH, phenol is present as phenoxide, which attacks the acid chloride to give the ester phenyl benzoate.

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + C6H5COCl ->[aq. NaOH] C6H5COOC6H5 + NaCl + H2O" />

## Acylation of amines: the family

| Acylating agent | Group introduced | Name of reaction | Product from R–NH₂ |
|---|---|---|---|
| R′–COCl | acyl, R′CO– | acylation | R–NH–CO–R′ |
| CH₃COCl or (CH₃CO)₂O | acetyl, CH₃CO– | acetylation | R–NH–CO–CH₃ |
| C₆H₅COCl | benzoyl, C₆H₅CO– | benzoylation | R–NH–CO–C₆H₅ |

Esters also acylate amines, more slowly: methyl ethanoate and methanamine give N-methylethanamide and methanol, again by attack at the carbonyl carbon.

<Eq ce="CH3COOCH3 + CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + CH3OH" />

<ExamTip>
Only primary and secondary amines are acylated, because a hydrogen on nitrogen must be replaced. Acetylation of aniline is also used to protect the –NH₂ group and moderate its activating effect before nitration or bromination; the acetyl group is removed afterwards by hydrolysis.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Tertiary amines** do not form amides (no N–H).
- **Hydrolysis of the acid chloride.** In water the acid chloride slowly hydrolyses to the carboxylic acid, so it is added gradually and the mixture is shaken well.
- **Carboxylic acids** are not used directly: at room temperature they simply form salts with amines.
