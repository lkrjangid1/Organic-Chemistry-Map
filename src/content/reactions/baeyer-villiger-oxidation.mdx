## What is the Baeyer–Villiger oxidation?

The **Baeyer–Villiger oxidation** converts a **ketone into an ester** by inserting an oxygen atom next to the carbonyl group. The oxidant is a **peroxyacid (R–CO₃H)**, most often **mCPBA** (meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid), peracetic acid or trifluoroperacetic acid; H₂O₂ also works with a Lewis acid catalyst. **Cyclic ketones give lactones**, so cyclohexanone becomes ε-caprolactone. Adolf von Baeyer and Victor Villiger reported it in 1899.

<Eq ce="R-CO-R' + R''CO3H -> R-CO-O-R' + R''COOH" />

## Mechanism

1. **Activation of the carbonyl.** The peroxyacid protonates the C=O oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
2. **Addition (Criegee intermediate).** The peroxyacid adds to the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral peroxy intermediate called the **Criegee intermediate**, R₂C(OH)–O–O–COR''.
3. **Concerted migration.** One group on the carbinol carbon moves to the nearer peroxide oxygen while the O–O bond breaks and a carboxylate leaves. This step is considered rate-determining.
4. **Deprotonation.** The resulting oxocarbenium ion loses H⁺, giving the ester.

<Eq ce="R2C=O ->[R''CO3H] R2C(OH)-O-O-COR'' ->[\text{R migrates}][-R''COOH] R-CO-O-R" />

<KeyPoint>
**Migratory aptitude:** tertiary alkyl &gt; cyclohexyl (secondary) &gt; phenyl &gt; primary alkyl &gt; methyl. Groups that better stabilise positive charge migrate. The migrating group keeps its configuration (retention).
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Cyclohexanone → ε-caprolactone (ring expansion to a seven-membered lactone):**

<Eq ce="\text{cyclohexanone} ->[mCPBA][CH2Cl2] \text{ε-caprolactone}" />

2. **Acetophenone → phenyl acetate:** phenyl outranks methyl, so O goes between C=O and the ring.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCH3 ->[mCPBA] CH3COOC6H5" />

3. **tert-Butyl methyl ketone (pinacolone) → tert-butyl acetate:** the tertiary group migrates.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-CO-CH3 ->[RCO3H] CH3-CO-O-C(CH3)3" />

4. **Cyclopentanone → six-membered lactone (δ-valerolactone).**

<ExamTip>
Find the group on the carbonyl carbon with the higher migratory aptitude and put the new oxygen between it and the C=O. That group ends up as the **alkoxy (–OR) part** of the ester. For a cyclic ketone, the more substituted α-carbon is the one bonded to the ring oxygen in the lactone.
</ExamTip>

## Predicting the product of an unsymmetrical ketone

Work through three checks:

1. **List the two groups** on the carbonyl carbon and rank them by migratory aptitude.
2. **Insert O** between the carbonyl carbon and the higher-ranked group. That group is now attached to oxygen, so it becomes the alcohol part of the ester.
3. **Keep stereochemistry**: if the migrating carbon is a stereocentre, its configuration is unchanged, because no bond to it is broken except the one that moves intact to oxygen.

For **2-methylcyclohexanone**, the ring CH(CH₃) carbon (secondary) outranks the ring CH₂ carbon (primary), so oxygen goes in on the substituted side. The product is the seven-membered lactone with the methyl group on the carbon next to the ring oxygen (7-methyloxepan-2-one). For **3-methylbutan-2-one**, isopropyl outranks methyl, so the product is isopropyl acetate, CH₃COOCH(CH₃)₂.

<KeyPoint>
mCPBA also epoxidises C=C bonds. In a molecule that contains both a ketone and an alkene, check which change the question intends before drawing the product.
</KeyPoint>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Aldehydes are oxidised too; hydrogen migrates preferentially, so most aldehydes give carboxylic acids.
- Ring strain and stereoelectronic effects can override the simple migratory-aptitude order.
- Peroxyacids also epoxidise C=C bonds (Prilezhaev reaction), so an alkene elsewhere in the molecule may react first.
- Plain H₂O₂ is much less reactive and needs a catalyst; BF₃ with H₂O₂ is used to encourage primary-group migration.
