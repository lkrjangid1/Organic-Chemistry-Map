## What is the Knoevenagel condensation?

The **Knoevenagel condensation** is the reaction of an **aldehyde or ketone** with an **active methylene compound** (Z–CH₂–Z′, where Z and Z′ are electron-withdrawing groups) in the presence of a **weakly basic amine** such as piperidine or pyridine. The carbanion adds to the C=O group and the adduct then **loses water**, giving an α,β-unsaturated product with a new C=C bond. It is a modification of the aldol condensation, named after the German chemist Emil Knoevenagel.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R2C=O + CH2ZZ' ->[amine base] R2C=CZZ' + H2O" />

Common active methylene partners:

| Compound | Z, Z′ | Approx. pKa of CH₂ |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylacetone (pentane-2,4-dione) | COCH₃, COCH₃ | 9 |
| Ethyl acetoacetate | COCH₃, COOEt | 11 |
| Diethyl malonate | COOEt, COOEt | 13 |
| Malonic acid, cyanoacetic acid, malononitrile | COOH / CN | acidic CH₂ |
| Nitromethane (one strong group) | NO₂ | about 9 |

Compare these with a simple ketone (pKa about 19) or ester (about 25): an amine base can remove a proton from the active methylene compound but barely touches the carbonyl partner.

<KeyPoint>
Why a **weak** base: Z must be strong enough to allow deprotonation by a mild amine. A strong base would also form the enolate of the aldehyde or ketone and trigger its self-condensation.
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism

1. **Carbanion (enol) formation.** Piperidine removes a proton from the CH₂ of diethyl malonate, giving a stabilised carbanion; the charge is shared by both ester carbonyls.
2. **Addition.** The carbanion attacks the aldehyde C=O, giving an alkoxide that is protonated to the aldol-type adduct R–CH(OH)–CH(COOEt)₂.
3. **Elimination.** The remaining CH between the two esters is very acidic, so base removes it and OH⁻ is expelled (an E1cB-type step). The C=C formed is conjugated with both ester groups.

<Eq ce="CH2(COOEt)2 + B <=> ^-CH(COOEt)2 + BH+" />
<Eq ce="RCHO + ^-CH(COOEt)2 -> RCH(OH)CH(COOEt)2 ->[-H2O] RCH=C(COOEt)2" />

An alternative picture, with piperidine acting as an organocatalyst, has the amine first convert the aldehyde into an **iminium ion**, which is a better electrophile than the aldehyde itself.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Do not stop at the β-hydroxy adduct. The Knoevenagel condensation is defined by the dehydration step, so the expected answer is the C=C product, for example CH₃CH=C(COOEt)₂ from acetaldehyde and diethyl malonate. Also note that the textbook base is a weak amine (piperidine or pyridine), not sodium ethoxide.
</ExamTip>

## Doebner modification

When one of the activating groups is a **carboxylic acid**, as in **malonic acid**, and the reaction is run in **pyridine**, the condensation is accompanied by **decarboxylation**. The product keeps only one COOH group.

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + CH2(COOH)2 ->[pyridine][\Delta] C6H5CH=CHCOOH + CO2 + H2O" />

Acrolein with malonic acid in pyridine gives *trans*-penta-2,4-dienoic acid, and crotonaldehyde gives **sorbic acid** (hexa-2,4-dienoic acid), a food preservative.

## Examples

**1. Benzaldehyde and diethyl malonate.**

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + CH2(COOEt)2 ->[piperidine] C6H5CH=C(COOEt)2 + H2O" />

**2. Acetaldehyde and diethyl malonate.**

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + CH2(COOEt)2 ->[piperidine] CH3CH=C(COOEt)2 + H2O" />

The intermediate CH₃CH(OH)CH(COOEt)₂ is the aldol-type adduct; under Knoevenagel conditions it dehydrates to diethyl ethylidenemalonate.

**3. Cinnamic acid (Doebner).** Benzaldehyde with malonic acid in pyridine, as shown above. This is an alternative to the [Perkin reaction](/reactions/perkin-reaction/) for making cinnamic acids.

## Applications

- The Hantzsch pyridine synthesis and several heterocycle syntheses contain a Knoevenagel step.
- A Knoevenagel condensation is a key step in the industrial synthesis of the antimalarial drug lumefantrine.
- The products, with C=C conjugated to two electron-withdrawing groups, are excellent [Michael acceptors](/reactions/michael-addition/).
