## What is the Zinin reduction?

The **Zinin reduction** is the reduction of an aromatic nitro compound to an aromatic amine using a **sulphide reagent**: sodium sulphide (Na₂S), sodium hydrogensulphide (NaSH), ammonium hydrogensulphide (NH₄SH), ammonium sulphide ((NH₄)₂S) or ammonium polysulphide. Its main exam use is **selective (partial) reduction**: in a dinitro compound only **one** nitro group is reduced, so **m-dinitrobenzene gives m-nitroaniline**. It is named after the Russian chemist **Nikolay Zinin**, who reduced nitrobenzene with hydrogen sulphide in 1842.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="O2N-C6H4-NO2 ->[Na2S/NaSH or (NH4)2S][H2O] O2N-C6H4-NH2" />

A possible balanced equation for the sodium sulphide reaction shows that water takes part and thiosulphate is formed:

<Eq ce="4ArNO2 + 6S^2- + 7H2O -> 4ArNH2 + 3S2O3^2- + 6OH^-" />

## Mechanism

1. **Active reductant.** Sulphide (or disulphide formed from it in the solution) transfers electrons to the nitro group, which is the most electron-poor group in the molecule.
2. **Nitroso stage.** The –NO₂ group loses one oxygen as water and becomes –N=O.
3. **Hydroxylamine stage.** Further reduction gives –NHOH.
4. **Amine.** The last N–O bond is cleaved, giving –NH₂.

Nitroso compounds and arylhydroxylamines are the probable intermediates, the same ones seen in the [reduction of nitrobenzene](/reactions/reduction-of-nitrobenzene/) with metal and acid.

<KeyPoint title="Why only one nitro group?">
Sulphide is a weak reducing agent. In exam answers the selectivity is explained like this: once the first –NO₂ has become –NH₂, the ring is much richer in electrons, so the remaining nitro group is reduced far more slowly. The reaction can therefore be stopped at the nitroaniline.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**m-Dinitrobenzene to m-nitroaniline (the standard example):**

<Eq ce="m-C6H4(NO2)2 ->[(NH4)2S] m-O2N-C6H4-NH2" />

This is also how 3-nitroaniline is made commercially, using hydrogen sulphide or sodium sulphide.

**Complete reduction for comparison:** metal and acid reduce both groups.

<Eq ce="m-C6H4(NO2)2 ->[Fe or Sn, HCl] m-C6H4(NH2)2" />

**Dinitrophenol:** sodium sulphide reduces 2,4-dinitrophenol selectively to 2-amino-4-nitrophenol, leaving the second nitro group in place.

| Reagent | m-Dinitrobenzene gives | Nitro groups reduced |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂S + NaSH, NH₄SH, (NH₄)₂S, (NH₄)₂Sₓ | m-Nitroaniline | One |
| Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl | m-Phenylenediamine | Both |
| H₂ / Ni or Pd | m-Phenylenediamine | Both |

<ExamTip>
If a question asks for "selective" or "partial" reduction of a dinitro compound, the answer is a sulphide reagent such as (NH₄)₂S, NH₄SH or Na₂S. Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl and catalytic hydrogenation reduce every nitro group present.
</ExamTip>

## Advantages and limitations

- **Chemoselective:** aryl halides and C=C bonds in the molecule are not attacked, which is useful when hydrogenation would also reduce them.
- **Water is needed:** the reaction is run in water or aqueous alcohol, because water is a reactant (see the balanced equation above).
- **Regioselectivity in substituted rings:** when the two nitro groups are in different environments (for example in substituted dinitrobenzenes), which group is reduced depends on the substituents and the conditions. Learn the specific example given in your textbook rather than applying a single rule.
