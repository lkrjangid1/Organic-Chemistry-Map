## What is the aldol condensation?

The **aldol condensation** is the base-catalysed reaction in which two molecules of an aldehyde or ketone that has **at least one α-hydrogen** join to form a **β-hydroxy aldehyde or β-hydroxy ketone** (an "aldol", from *ald*ehyde + alcoh*ol*). On warming, the aldol loses water to give an **α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound**. Dilute NaOH, Na₂CO₃ or Ba(OH)₂ are the usual catalysts; acids also work.

Strictly, the first stage is an *addition* (nothing is lost). It becomes a *condensation* only when water is eliminated.

## Mechanism

1. **Enolate formation.** OH⁻ removes an acidic α-H, giving a resonance-stabilised enolate ion.
2. **C–C bond formation.** The enolate carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule, giving an alkoxide.
3. **Protonation.** Water protonates the alkoxide to give the aldol (3-hydroxybutanal from ethanal).
4. **Dehydration (on heating).** Another α-H is removed and OH⁻ is expelled by an **E1cB** route, giving the conjugated C=C–C=O system. Under acid, dehydration goes through the enol instead.

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + OH- <=> ^{-}CH2CHO + H2O" />
<Eq ce="^{-}CH2CHO + CH3CHO -> CH3CH(O^{-})CH2CHO ->[H2O] CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO" />

<KeyPoint>
Every step up to the aldol is reversible. Heating drives the reaction forward because the dehydration to the conjugated enone is practically irreversible. That is why ketones such as acetone, whose aldol equilibrium is unfavourable, still give good yields of the condensation product.
</KeyPoint>

In exam answers the attack of the enolate on the second carbonyl group is usually treated as the slow step. Real kinetic studies show that the slow step depends on the substrate and the concentration, so learn this as an exam convention only.

## Examples

<Eq ce="2 CH3COCH3 <=>[Ba(OH)2] (CH3)2C(OH)CH2COCH3 ->[\Delta][-H2O] (CH3)2C=CHCOCH3" />

Acetone gives diacetone alcohol (4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one) and then mesityl oxide (4-methylpent-3-en-2-one).

<Eq ce="2 CH3CH2CHO ->[dil. NaOH] CH3CH2CH(OH)CH(CH3)CHO" />

Propanal gives 3-hydroxy-2-methylpentanal: the α-carbon (C-2) of one molecule bonds to the carbonyl carbon of the other.

## Intramolecular aldol condensation

A molecule with two carbonyl groups can cyclise. Heptane-2,6-dione (a 1,5-diketone) forms a six-membered ring: the enolate at C-1 attacks the C-6 carbonyl, the β-hydroxy ketone forms, and heating gives 3-methylcyclohex-2-en-1-one. Hexane-2,5-dione (a 1,4-diketone) gives 3-methylcyclopent-2-en-1-one in the same way.

<Eq ce="CH3CO(CH2)3COCH3 ->[OH-][\Delta] 3-methylcyclohex-2-enone + H2O" />

<KeyPoint>
Ring-size rule: **6 is preferred over 4 and 8; 5 is preferred over 3 and 7.** Because all steps are reversible, the strain-free five- or six-membered ring is the one that accumulates.
</KeyPoint>

## Retro-aldol reaction

The **retro-aldol** reaction is the reverse: a base breaks the bond between the α-carbon and the β-carbon of an aldol, giving two carbonyl compounds. Diacetone alcohol, for example, splits back into two molecules of acetone.

This is used in "equilibration" questions. 1-(2-Hydroxy-2-methylcyclobutyl)ethanone, a strained four-membered aldol, opens by retro-aldol to heptane-2,6-dione. The open diketone then closes again by aldol addition into the six-membered 3-hydroxy-3-methylcyclohexanone.

<Trick>
To find what an aldol came from, break the bond between the α-carbon and the carbon that carries the OH, and turn the OH carbon back into C=O.
</Trick>

## Vinylogous aldol condensation

In crotonaldehyde (CH₃–CH=CH–CHO) the conjugated C=C passes the carbonyl's activating effect to the end CH₃ group, so these **γ-hydrogens** are acidic, like an α-H. The resulting extended enolate reacts at the γ-carbon. Each condensation adds a –CH=CH– unit:

<Eq ce="CH3CH=CHCHO + CH3CHO ->[OH-][\Delta] CH3(CH=CH)2CHO + H2O" />

The product is hexa-2,4-dienal. For polyene questions, the end CH₃ of one molecule of CH₃(CH=CH)ₙCHO attacks the CHO of another, so self-condensation gives CH₃(CH=CH)₂ₙ₊₁CHO. So n = 200 gives n = 401, and getting n = 201 needs a starting n of 100.

## Directed aldol (LDA)

With a strong, bulky base such as **LDA (lithium diisopropylamide)** in THF at −78 °C, one partner is converted completely and irreversibly into its **lithium enolate**. With an unsymmetrical ketone, the proton is removed from the less hindered side (the kinetic enolate). The second carbonyl compound is added afterwards, so only one crossed product forms.

<Eq ce="CH3COCH3 ->[LDA][THF, -78 ^{\circ}C] CH2=C(OLi)CH3 ->[1. HCHO][2. H3O+] HOCH2CH2COCH3" />

The same lithium enolate reacts with CH₃Cl by SN2 to give butan-2-one. That is α-alkylation, not an aldol reaction.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
LDA stands for lithium di**isopropyl**amide, LiN(CH(CH₃)₂)₂, not "di-isobutyl" amide. It is a strong base that is too hindered to act as a nucleophile.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- HCHO, C₆H₅CHO and (CH₃)₃C–CHO have no α-H, so they cannot self-condense. In concentrated alkali they undergo the [Cannizzaro reaction](/reactions/cannizzaro-reaction/) instead.
- Aldols from R₂CH–CHO cannot dehydrate to an enone, because no α-H is left on the new quaternary carbon.
- 1,3-Dicarbonyls such as pentane-2,4-dione are simply deprotonated to a stable enolate and do not self-condense.
