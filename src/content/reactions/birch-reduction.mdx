## What is the Birch reduction?

The **Birch reduction** is the partial reduction of an **aromatic ring to a cyclohexa-1,4-diene** using an **alkali metal (Na or Li) dissolved in liquid ammonia**, with an **alcohol** as the proton source. Benzene gives **cyclohexa-1,4-diene**, not the conjugated 1,3-diene, and the ring is not reduced further. It is named after the Australian chemist **Arthur J. Birch**. In Indian exam usage, the same reagent acting on an **alkyne** to give a **trans-alkene** is also called Birch reduction; textbooks call that case a dissolving metal reduction.

## Mechanism (benzene)

1. **Solvated electrons.** Na dissolves in liquid NH₃ to give Na⁺ and blue, ammonia-solvated electrons: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻.
2. **First electron transfer.** An electron enters the ring's π system, giving a **radical anion**.
3. **First protonation.** The alcohol protonates the radical anion, giving a cyclohexadienyl radical. This is the slow step.
4. **Second electron transfer.** Another electron converts the radical into a cyclohexadienyl **carbanion**.
5. **Second protonation.** The carbanion is protonated at the carbon **para** to the first one, giving the 1,4-diene.

<Eq ce="C6H6 ->[Na, liq. NH3][C2H5OH] C6H8 (cyclohexa-1,4-diene)" />

<KeyPoint>
Electron, proton, electron, proton. The two hydrogens add **1,4 (para)** to each other, so the two remaining C=C bonds are **isolated**, not conjugated.
</KeyPoint>

## Substituent effects: EDG vs EWG

| Substituent on ring | Example | Where it ends up | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electron-donating (OCH₃, CH₃) | Anisole | On a **double-bond (sp²) carbon** | 1-Methoxycyclohexa-1,4-diene |
| Electron-withdrawing (COOH, COOR) | Benzoic acid | On an **sp³ carbon** | Cyclohexa-2,5-diene-1-carboxylic acid |

The reason: protonation happens **ortho** to an electron donor, keeping the donor on the more highly substituted double bond. With an electron-withdrawing group, protonation happens **para** to it, so the group sits on the carbon that becomes sp³, where it can stabilise the neighbouring carbanion.

<Eq ce="C6H5OCH3 ->[Na, liq. NH3, ROH] 1-methoxycyclohexa-1,4-diene" />

<Eq ce="C6H5COOH ->[Na, liq. NH3, ROH] cyclohexa-2,5-diene-1-carboxylic acid" />

Electron-withdrawing groups also **speed up** the reduction (they stabilise the radical anion), while electron-donating groups slow it down, the reverse of electrophilic substitution.

<ExamTip>
EDG: on the double bond. EWG: on the sp³ carbon. In both products the two CH₂ groups are para to each other.
</ExamTip>

## Alkynes: trans-alkenes

Sodium or lithium in liquid ammonia reduces an internal alkyne to the **trans (E) alkene**, for example 5-decyne gives trans-5-decene.

1. An electron adds to the triple bond, giving a **radical anion**.
2. NH₃ protonates it, giving a **vinyl radical**. Vinyl radicals flip between cis and trans forms quickly.
3. A second electron gives a **vinyl anion**, which forms in the less hindered **trans** shape.
4. NH₃ protonates the vinyl anion to give the **trans-alkene**.

<Eq ce="R-C#C-R' ->[Na, liq. NH3] trans-RCH=CHR'" />

This is the counterpart of H₂ with the [Lindlar catalyst](/reagents/lindlar-catalyst/), which gives the cis-alkene.

## What it does and does not reduce

- Reduced: benzene rings, internal alkynes, C=C conjugated with an aromatic ring, ketones and nitro groups (these are more easily reduced than the ring).
- Not reduced: isolated alkenes (usually), alcohols, ethers and carboxylic acid groups.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Sodium in **liquid ammonia** is the Birch reagent; the alcohol is only added as a proton source. Sodium in ethanol alone is a different reduction (used for esters in the [Bouveault–Blanc reduction](/reactions/bouveault-blanc-reduction/)), not the Birch reduction.
</ExamTip>

## Comparison with catalytic hydrogenation

| Feature | Birch (Na/liq. NH₃) | [Catalytic hydrogenation](/reactions/catalytic-hydrogenation/) |
|---|---|---|
| Benzene gives | Cyclohexa-1,4-diene | Cyclohexane (only at high pressure) |
| Alkyne gives | trans-Alkene | Alkane (cis-alkene with Lindlar) |
| Mechanism | Electron transfer and protonation | Syn addition on metal surface |
