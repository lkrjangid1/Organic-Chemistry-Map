## What is nucleophilic aromatic substitution?

**Nucleophilic aromatic substitution** is the replacement of a leaving group on a benzene ring, usually a halogen, by a nucleophile. Simple aryl halides react only under drastic conditions (chlorobenzene needs aqueous NaOH at about **623 K and 300 atm** to give phenol), but **electron-withdrawing groups such as –NO₂ at ortho or para positions** make it fast. The main pathway, called **SNAr** or, in Indian coaching classes, **ArSN2**, is an **addition–elimination**: the nucleophile adds to form a carbanion (Meisenheimer complex), then the halide leaves. Two other pathways exist: **ArSN1** through an aryl cation (diazonium salts) and the **benzyne** elimination–addition.

## Why ordinary SN1 and SN2 fail

- The C–X bond lies in the plane of the ring, so the ring blocks a **backside attack**: no SN2.
- An **aryl cation** is very unstable, so unaided ionisation (SN1) is unfavourable.
- Resonance gives the C–X bond partial double-bond character, making it harder to break.

## Mechanism of SNAr (ArSN2, addition–elimination)

1. **Addition (slow).** The nucleophile attacks the **ipso carbon** (the δ+ carbon bearing X). That carbon becomes sp³ and the ring loses aromaticity. This step is reversible.
2. **Carbanion intermediate.** The negative charge is delocalised over the carbons **ortho and para** to the site of attack. With –NO₂ at these positions the charge spreads onto the nitro oxygens. This resonance-stabilised anion is the **Meisenheimer complex**.
3. **Elimination (fast).** X⁻ leaves and aromaticity is restored, giving Ar–Nu.

<Eq ce="Ar-X + Nu- <=> [Ar(X)(Nu)]- -> Ar-Nu + X-" />

<KeyPoint title="ArSN2 checklist">
Strong nucleophile; high temperature and pressure for plain halobenzene; ortho and para स्थिति पर e⁻ आकर्षित समूह उपस्थित होना चाहिए (an electron-withdrawing group should be at ortho/para positions); reactivity ∝ stability of the carbanion; SN2 at an alkyl carbon is faster than ArSN2.
</KeyPoint>

A **meta** nitro group has no effect on the rate, because the carbanion's charge never reaches the meta carbon (NCERT). The more ortho/para nitro groups present, the milder the conditions: in exam teaching, with many electron-withdrawing groups the reaction is said to go even near room temperature.

## Examples

**1. Chlorobenzene to phenol (NCERT).**

<Eq ce="C6H5Cl ->[NaOH, 623 K][300 atm] C6H5ONa ->[H+] C6H5OH" />

**2. 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene with hydroxide.** Two nitro groups stabilise the Meisenheimer complex, so aqueous base replaces chlorine easily to give 2,4-dinitrophenol (as its anion in base).

<Eq ce="C6H3(NO2)2Cl + 2OH- -> C6H3(NO2)2O- + Cl- + H2O" />

**3. ArSN1: diazonium salt to phenol.** Benzenediazonium chloride loses N₂, an excellent leaving group, to give a phenyl cation, which water captures. Warming the aqueous diazonium salt gives phenol.

<Eq ce="C6H5N2+Cl- ->[\text{slow}][-N2] C6H5+ ->[H2O][-H+] C6H5OH" />

In exam answers N₂ loss is treated as the slow, reversible step, as in SN1. Not every diazonium replacement is ArSN1: the Sandmeyer reaction with Cu(I) salts goes by aryl radicals, not cations.

<ExamTip>
To rank SNAr reactivity, count the nitro (or –CN, –CHO, –COR) groups at ortho and para positions to the halogen. More groups at these positions means faster reaction; a group only at meta barely helps.
</ExamTip>

## For JEE vs in practice

For JEE, the conversion of chlorobenzene to phenol with NaOH at 623 K is written by the addition–elimination route. In practice, unactivated aryl halides under these forcing conditions often react through **benzyne**: 4-chlorotoluene heated with NaOH at about 340 °C gives an equal mixture of 3- and 4-methylphenol, which only an elimination–addition can explain. The true SNAr path needs activating groups.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Without activating groups, SNAr is very slow; strong bases such as NaNH₂ switch the reaction to the benzyne mechanism.
- Electron-donating groups (–CH₃, –OCH₃) slow SNAr by destabilising the carbanion.
- Diazonium replacements with Cu(I) (Sandmeyer, Gattermann) are radical reactions, not ArSN1.
