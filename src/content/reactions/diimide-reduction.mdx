## What is diimide reduction?

**Diimide reduction** is the conversion of an **alkene (or alkyne) into an alkane** by **diimide, HN=NH**, which hands over its two hydrogens and is released as **nitrogen gas**. Diimide is too unstable to store, so it is made in the flask, classically by oxidising **hydrazine with hydrogen peroxide**. The two hydrogens add to the **same face** of the C=C bond (**syn addition**), giving the same stereochemistry as catalytic hydrogenation but **without H₂ gas or a metal catalyst**.

## Generating diimide

<Eq ce="H2N-NH2 + H2O2 -> HN=NH + 2H2O" />

Here H₂O₂ is the oxidising agent. Other ways to make diimide are air oxidation of hydrazine with a copper(II) catalyst, and decarboxylation of potassium azodicarboxylate with an acid.

## Mechanism

1. **Formation of cis-diimide.** HN=NH exists as cis and trans isomers. Only the cis form can react, and acids help the two forms interconvert.
2. **Concerted hydrogen transfer.** cis-Diimide approaches one face of the alkene. Through a six-membered cyclic transition state, both N–H hydrogens move to the two alkene carbons at the same time while the N=N becomes N≡N.
3. **Loss of nitrogen.** N₂ gas escapes, which makes the step irreversible. The product has both new H atoms on the same side.

<Eq ce=">C=C< + HN=NH -> >CH-CH< + N#N" />

In the overall process, forming diimide is the slow step.

<KeyPoint>
Diimide reduction is a **syn addition** of two H atoms, metal-free, and **N₂ gas** is the only by-product of the hydrogen transfer.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Oleic acid to stearic acid.** This was the first reduction seen with hydrazine (1929), long before diimide was recognised as the reagent.

<Eq ce="C17H33COOH ->[N2H4, H2O2] C17H35COOH" />

**Alkyne to alkane.** But-2-yne takes up two molecules of diimide. The intermediate cis-but-2-ene reacts almost as fast as it forms, so butane is the product.

<Eq ce="CH3-C#C-CH3 ->[2 HN=NH] CH3CH2CH2CH3 + 2N2" />

**Selective reduction.** A molecule with a C=C bond and a peroxide (O–O) or N–O bond can have its C=C reduced by diimide while the O–O or N–O bond survives; metal hydrogenation would cleave it.

## Scope and selectivity

| Substrate | Result with HN=NH |
|---|---|
| Alkyne | Alkane (an alkene intermediate is hard to isolate) |
| Allene | Alkene, then alkane |
| Terminal or strained alkene | Fast reduction |
| Highly substituted alkene | Slow |
| trans-Alkene vs cis-alkene | trans reacts faster |
| C=O, C≡N, NO₂ | Generally not reduced |
| O–O, N–O bonds | Not affected |

Order of reactivity: **alkynes, allenes > terminal or strained alkenes > substituted alkenes**.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
HN=NH is **diimide (diazene)**, not an azine; an azine is R₂C=N–N=CR₂. And diimide does not reduce only alkenes: it reduces **any non-polar C=C or C≡C**, including alkynes. What it leaves alone are **polar** multiple bonds such as C=O.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- Diimide also destroys itself by disproportionation (2 HN=NH gives N₂H₄ + N₂), so a large excess of the precursor is needed.
- Reactions can be slow, and crowded (tri- or tetrasubstituted) alkenes react sluggishly.
- Stopping an alkyne at the alkene stage is difficult; use the [Lindlar catalyst](/reagents/lindlar-catalyst/) instead.
