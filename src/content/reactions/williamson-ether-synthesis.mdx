## What is the Williamson ether synthesis?

The **Williamson ether synthesis** is the reaction of a **sodium alkoxide (or phenoxide) with an alkyl halide** to give an **ether**, R–O–R′, and a sodium halide. The alkoxide ion attacks the alkyl halide by an **SN2** reaction, so the best results come with **methyl and primary alkyl halides**. It is the most important laboratory method for making both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers, and was developed by Alexander Williamson in 1850.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="R-O^{-}Na+ + R'-X -> R-O-R' + NaX" />

The alkoxide is usually prepared just before use, by adding sodium metal or sodium hydride to the alcohol. Phenols are acidic enough that aqueous NaOH (or K₂CO₃) converts them to phenoxide.

<Eq ce="2 ROH + 2 Na -> 2 RO^{-}Na+ + H2 ^" />

## Mechanism

1. **Formation of the nucleophile.** The alcohol is deprotonated to the alkoxide ion, RO⁻, a strong nucleophile and a strong base.
2. **SN2 attack.** The alkoxide oxygen attacks the carbon bearing the halogen from the back side. The C–O bond forms as the C–X bond breaks, in one step.
3. **Products.** The ether and NaX are formed. If the carbon attacked is a stereocentre, its configuration is inverted.

<KeyPoint>
The alkoxide can be primary, secondary or tertiary. The alkyl halide should be methyl or primary, because the halide carbon is where the SN2 attack happens.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**Diethyl ether** from sodium ethoxide and chloroethane:

<Eq ce="C2H5ONa + C2H5Cl -> C2H5OC2H5 + NaCl" />

**Anisole from phenol** (map edge: phenol → anisole):

<Eq ce="C6H5OH ->[NaOH] C6H5O^{-}Na+ ->[CH3I] C6H5OCH3 + NaI" />

**Allyl phenyl ether** (map edge: phenol → allyl phenyl ether), the substrate of the Claisen rearrangement:

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + CH2=CHCH2Br ->[K2CO3, acetone] C6H5OCH2CH=CH2" />

**tert-Butyl methyl ether**: the bulky part must be the alkoxide.

<Eq ce="(CH3)3C-O^{-}Na+ + CH3Br -> (CH3)3C-O-CH3 + NaBr" />

## Choosing the right pair (substitution vs elimination)

| Alkyl halide | Main outcome with RO⁻ |
|---|---|
| Methyl, 1° | SN2: ether (good yield) |
| 2° | SN2 and E2 compete: ether + alkene |
| 3° | E2 only: alkene, no ether |

For example, sodium methoxide with tert-butyl bromide gives only **2-methylpropene**, because the alkoxide removes a β-hydrogen instead of attacking the crowded carbon.

<Eq ce="CH3ONa + (CH3)3C-Br -> CH2=C(CH3)2 + CH3OH + NaBr" />

<ExamTip>
To plan an unsymmetrical ether, cut one C–O bond in your mind. Put the less hindered carbon on the halide side and the more hindered carbon on the alkoxide side. For alkyl aryl ethers the aryl part is always the phenoxide.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Tertiary halides** give alkenes only; secondary halides give mixtures.
- **Aryl and vinyl halides** cannot be attacked by SN2, so they cannot be the halide component.
- **Phenoxide is an ambident nucleophile**: under some conditions it can be alkylated on the ring carbon instead of oxygen.
- **Slow alkyl chlorides** can be speeded up by adding a little iodide salt, which converts R–Cl into the more reactive R–I in situ (a Finkelstein exchange).
