## What is Markovnikov's rule?

**Markovnikov's rule** predicts the major product when a polar reagent **H–X** (HCl, HBr, HI, or H₂O with acid) adds to an **unsymmetrical alkene**: the **hydrogen** goes to the double-bond carbon that already carries **more hydrogens**, and **X** goes to the carbon carrying **more alkyl groups**. Propene and HBr therefore give **2-bromopropane** as the major product. Vladimir Markovnikov proposed the rule in 1870; the modern reason is that the reaction goes through the **more stable carbocation**.

इसके अनुसार -ve charge वाला element वहाँ पर attack करता है जहाँ पर +ve charge More Stable होता है। (The negatively charged part attacks where the positive charge is more stable.)
</Trick>

## Mechanism

Addition of HX to an alkene is an **electrophilic addition** in two steps.

1. **Protonation.** The π electrons of C=C attack H⁺ of H–X. A new C–H bond forms and the other carbon becomes a **carbocation**; X⁻ is released. In exam answers this step is the slow, rate-determining step, and its rate depends on how stable the carbocation is.
2. **Capture by X⁻.** The halide ion attacks the carbocation, giving the alkyl halide.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH2 + H-X -> CH3-CH2+ + X- -> CH3-CH2-X" />

For propene, H⁺ can add to C1 (giving a **2°** cation) or to C2 (giving a **1°** cation). The 2° cation is more stable because of more hyperconjugation and +I effect of the alkyl groups, so it forms faster and leads to the major product.

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 ->[H+] CH3-CH+-CH3 ->[Br-] CH3-CHBr-CH3" />

<KeyPoint>
Rate of HX addition: HI greater than HBr greater than HCl, much greater than HF (the H–X bond gets weaker down the group). More alkyl groups on the C=C also speed up the reaction, because they stabilise the carbocation.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. 2-Methylpropene with HBr**

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C=CH2 + HBr -> (CH3)3C-Br" />

Only 2-bromo-2-methylpropane forms; the other orientation would need a primary carbocation.

**2. Acid-catalysed hydration**

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + H2O ->[H+] CH3-CH(OH)-CH3" />

OH goes to the more substituted carbon, giving propan-2-ol.

**3. Carbocation rearrangement**

If the first carbocation can become more stable by a 1,2-shift, it rearranges before X⁻ attacks. For example, isopropenylcyclobutane and HBr first give a tertiary cation next to the four-membered ring; a ring bond migrates (ring expansion to a less strained five-membered ring), a methyl group then shifts to make a tertiary cation, and Br⁻ finally gives **1-bromo-1,2-dimethylcyclopentane**. Each step appears as a separate hump on the energy diagram.

## Kinetic vs thermodynamic control: HBr with 1,3-butadiene

Protonation of buta-1,3-diene at C1 gives a resonance-stabilised **allylic cation**, with positive charge shared by C2 and C4. Br⁻ can attack either site.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH-CH=CH2 + HBr -> CH3-CHBr-CH=CH2 + CH3-CH=CH-CH2Br" />

| Feature | KCP: 1,2-adduct (3-bromobut-1-ene) | TCP: 1,4-adduct (1-bromobut-2-ene) |
|---|---|---|
| Favoured at | low temperature | higher temperature |
| Forms | faster (formed first) | slower |
| Activation energy | lower | higher |
| Stability | less stable (terminal C=C) | more stable (internal, disubstituted C=C) |
| Energy released | less exothermic | more exothermic |

At low temperature the products cannot revert to the allylic cation, so the faster-forming 1,2-product dominates. At higher temperature the steps become reversible, equilibrium is reached and the more stable 1,4-product wins.

<ExamTip>
"Low temp per banta h" (KCP) and "High temp per banta h" (TCP). In any KCP/TCP question, KCP = lower Ea = formed faster; TCP = more stable = more negative ΔH.
</ExamTip>

## Anti-Markovnikov addition

When **HBr** adds in the presence of a **peroxide**, the product is reversed: propene gives 1-bromopropane. This is the **peroxide (Kharasch) effect**, which runs through a free radical instead of a carbocation. A handy way to phrase it: the negative part ends up where a positive charge would be **less** stable. HCl and HI do not show this effect.

## Limitations and exceptions

- The rule applies to **unsymmetrical** alkenes; symmetrical alkenes give one product either way.
- Carbocation **rearrangements** (hydride, methyl or ring shifts) can give products that the simple rule does not predict.
- Radical additions (HBr/peroxide) and hydroboration–oxidation give anti-Markovnikov products.
- The less stable carbocation still forms to a small extent, so the other regioisomer appears as a minor product.
