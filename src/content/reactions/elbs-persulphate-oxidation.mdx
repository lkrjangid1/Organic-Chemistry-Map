## What is the Elbs persulphate oxidation?

The **Elbs persulphate oxidation** converts a **phenol** into a **para-dihydroxybenzene** using **potassium persulphate (K₂S₂O₈) in aqueous alkali**. The phenoxide ion attacks the persulphate ion, which puts an **–OSO₃⁻ (sulphate) group para to the OH**. Heating this aryl sulphate with dilute acid hydrolyses it to the second OH, so **phenol gives hydroquinone**. It was reported by Karl Elbs in 1893, who used it to make nitrohydroquinone.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + S2O8^2- + 2OH- -> 4-HOC6H4OSO3^- + SO4^2- + H2O" />

<Eq ce="4-HOC6H4OSO3^- + H2O ->[H+][\Delta] 1,4-C6H4(OH)2 + HSO4^-" />

## Mechanism

1. **Phenoxide formation.** OH⁻ removes the phenolic proton. The negative charge spreads into the ring, so the **para carbon** carries partial negative charge (the para-carbanion resonance form).
2. **Attack on the peroxide oxygen.** The para carbon attacks one oxygen of the O–O bond of ⁻O₃S–O–O–SO₃⁻. The O–O bond breaks and sulphate (SO₄²⁻) leaves. This is a nucleophilic displacement on oxygen, not a radical reaction.
3. **Rearomatisation.** The resulting cyclohexadienone loses the para hydrogen (tautomerisation) and becomes an aromatic **4-hydroxyphenyl sulphate**.
4. **Acid hydrolysis.** The aryl sulphate ester is hydrolysed in hot aqueous acid, replacing –OSO₃⁻ by –OH and giving **hydroquinone**.

<KeyPoint>
The attack comes directly from the **para carbon** of the phenoxide. When the alternative O-sulphate intermediate was made separately, it moved to the ortho position, not para, which rules out "O-attack first, then migration" for the Elbs reaction.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
The reagent is **potassium persulphate** (peroxydisulphate, K₂S₂O₈, with an O–O bond), not potassium sulphate (K₂SO₄). Also, plain water does not hydrolyse the sulphate during the reaction: the aryl sulphate is a stable, water-soluble intermediate that needs acid and heat for hydrolysis.
</ExamTip>

## Examples

| Phenol | Elbs product (after hydrolysis) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Phenol | Hydroquinone | para attack |
| 2-Nitrophenol | 2-Nitrohydroquinone | Elbs's original example |
| 2-Fluorophenol | 2-Fluorohydroquinone | halogens tolerated |
| 3-Methylphenol (m-cresol) | 2-Methylhydroquinone | OH enters para to the OH |
| 3,4-Dimethylphenol | 3,4-Dimethylcatechol | para blocked, so ortho attack |
| 4-Nitrophenol | 2-Hydroxy-5-nitrophenyl sulphate | para blocked, so ortho attack |

## Limitations

- **Yields are low to moderate** (often below 50%); some starting phenol is recovered while all the persulphate is used up.
- The reaction is run at **room temperature or below** to suppress side reactions involving free radicals.
- Higher yields are obtained when persulphate is added slowly so that phenol stays in excess. Potassium persulphate's low solubility helps here; sodium and ammonium salts dissolve better.
- Its strength is **selectivity and tolerance**: many other functional groups (nitro, halogen, carbonyl) survive, and the procedure is very simple.
