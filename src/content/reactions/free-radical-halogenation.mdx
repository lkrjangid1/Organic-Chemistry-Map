## What is free radical halogenation?

**Free radical halogenation** is the substitution of a hydrogen atom of an **alkane** by a halogen atom when the alkane is treated with **chlorine or bromine** under **UV light** or at high temperature. The general change is **R–H + X₂ → R–X + HX**. It goes by a **free-radical chain mechanism**, and with larger alkanes it gives a mixture of isomeric alkyl halides, plus more highly halogenated products if halogen is in excess.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Calling this "free radical substitution of alkene". The substrate is an **alkane**.
</ExamTip>

## Mechanism

Using the chlorination of methane:

1. **Initiation.** UV light breaks the weakest bond present, Cl–Cl, homolytically into two chlorine atoms.
2. **Propagation (a).** Cl• removes a hydrogen atom from methane, giving a methyl radical and HCl. In exam answers this is the slow, rate-determining step.
3. **Propagation (b).** The methyl radical takes a Cl atom from Cl₂, giving chloromethane and a new Cl•, which repeats step 2. Thousands of molecules react for each photon absorbed.
4. **Termination.** Two radicals meet and pair up: Cl• + Cl•, CH₃• + Cl•, or CH₃• + CH₃• (the last gives ethane as an impurity).

<Eq ce="Cl2 ->[UV light] 2 Cl^{.}" />
<Eq ce="CH4 + Cl^{.} -> CH3^{.} + HCl" />
<Eq ce="CH3^{.} + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl^{.}" />
<Eq ce="CH3^{.} + CH3^{.} -> CH3-CH3" />

Oxygen is a radical trap and slows the reaction; peroxides (radical initiators) speed it up.

### How radicals end the chain: combination and disproportionation

- **Combination:** two radicals join, R• + R• → R–R (two ethyl radicals give butane).
- **Disproportionation:** one radical takes a β-hydrogen from the other, giving an alkane and an alkene. The radical that loses H is oxidised (to the alkene); the one that gains H is reduced (to the alkane).

<Eq ce="2 CH3-CH2^{.} -> CH2=CH2 + CH3-CH3" />

Both processes are exothermic and need little or no activation energy. Disproportionation becomes more important as the number of transferable β-hydrogens rises, so tertiary radicals disproportionate more readily than primary ones, while unhindered primary radicals combine more readily.

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Saying that disproportionation needs high temperature and a non-zero Ea. Combination and disproportionation are both nearly barrier-free.
</ExamTip>

## Reactivity and selectivity

<KeyPoint>
**Reactivity of halogens:** F₂ much greater than Cl₂ greater than Br₂ greater than I₂. **Reactivity of hydrogens** ∝ stability of the radical formed: 3° greater than 2° greater than 1° greater than CH₃–H. **Selectivity ∝ 1/Reactivity**: the less reactive the halogen atom, the more selective it is.
</KeyPoint>

Relative rate of attack per hydrogen (1° : 2° : 3°):

| Halogen | 1° | 2° | 3° | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fluorination | 1 | about 1 | about 1 | almost no selectivity (exam figure) |
| Chlorination | 1 | 3.8 | 5 | slightly selective |
| Bromination | 1 | about 80 | about 1600 | highly selective (often quoted as 1 : 82 : 1600) |

**Fluorination** releases so much heat that it can be explosive and can cleave C–C bonds. It is done only with fluorine heavily diluted by an inert gas (written F₂ + N₂; low-temperature methods use helium). **Iodination** is endothermic and essentially does not occur directly.

## Examples

**1. Chlorination of propane (light, 25 °C)**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH3 + Cl2 ->[light] CH3CH2CH2Cl (45\%) + CH3CHClCH3 (55\%)" />

Six primary hydrogens against two secondary ones would predict 75 : 25; the real result shows secondary H is about 3.8 times more reactive.

**2. Bromination of propane (light, 25 °C)**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH3 + Br2 ->[light] CH3CH2CH2Br (3\%) + CH3CHBrCH3 (97\%)" />

**3. Chlorination of 2-methylpropane**

<Eq ce="(CH3)3CH + Cl2 ->[light] (CH3)3CCl (65\%) + (CH3)2CHCH2Cl (35\%)" />

The single tertiary hydrogen wins over nine primary ones.

<ExamTip>
For product-percentage questions multiply (number of equivalent H) × (relative rate) for each type, then convert to percentages. Use 1 : 3.8 : 5 for chlorination. With bromination, assume the most stable radical's product is almost the only one.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Chlorination rarely stops at one substitution; methane gives CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄. A large excess of alkane favours the monochloro product.
- Unsymmetrical alkanes give all possible isomers, so the reaction is poor for making a single pure product unless bromination's selectivity is used.
- Allylic and benzylic C–H bonds are weaker and react preferentially (see NBS bromination).
