## What is the Hofmann elimination?

The **Hofmann elimination** converts an **amine into an alkene**. The amine is first methylated with excess methyl iodide to a quaternary ammonium iodide, the iodide is exchanged for hydroxide with moist **silver oxide**, and the **quaternary ammonium hydroxide** is then heated. The C–N bond breaks, trimethylamine leaves, and the **least substituted alkene** (the Hofmann product) is formed, the opposite of the usual Saytzeff preference.

It is named after August Wilhelm von Hofmann, who reported it in 1851.

## Mechanism

1. **Exhaustive methylation.** The nitrogen lone pair attacks CH₃–I repeatedly (SN2) until a quaternary ammonium iodide forms: R–NH₂ → R–N⁺(CH₃)₃ I⁻.
2. **Ion exchange.** Moist Ag₂O (in effect AgOH) precipitates AgI and leaves R–N⁺(CH₃)₃ OH⁻.
3. **E2 elimination on heating.** Hydroxide removes a β-hydrogen while the neutral amine N(CH₃)₃ leaves and the C=C forms, in one step. The usual geometry is anti, although syn elimination is also seen with these salts.

<Eq ce="R-CH2-CH2-N(CH3)3+ OH- ->[\Delta] R-CH=CH2 + N(CH3)3 + H2O" />

<KeyPoint>
The –N⁺(CH₃)₃ group is large. To reach the anti-periplanar arrangement needed for the Saytzeff alkene, alkyl groups crowd against it, so the base takes the most exposed hydrogen, usually on a CH₃ group. This is the **Hofmann rule**: the least substituted alkene is the major product.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Butan-2-yltrimethylammonium hydroxide.** β-Hydrogens are available on C-1 (a CH₃) and C-3 (a CH₂). The more accessible C-1 hydrogen is removed, so **but-1-ene** is the main alkene.

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH(CH3)N(CH3)3+ OH- ->[\Delta] CH2=CHCH2CH3 (major) + N(CH3)3 + H2O" />

**2. 1-Methylbutylamine.** After exhaustive methylation and Ag₂O, heating gives mainly **pent-1-ene** rather than pent-2-ene.

**3. Hofmann exhaustive methylation (HEM) of a cyclic amine.** N-Methylpyrrolidine needs two full cycles:

- CH₃I makes N,N-dimethylpyrrolidinium iodide; OH⁻/Δ opens the ring to give N,N-dimethylbut-3-en-1-amine (CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₂–N(CH₃)₂).
- A second CH₃I gives the open-chain quaternary salt; OH⁻/Δ removes the nitrogen as trimethylamine and leaves **buta-1,3-diene**.

<Eq ce="C4H8N-CH3 ->[1) CH3I, 2) Ag2O, \Delta] CH2=CHCH2CH2N(CH3)2 ->[1) CH3I, 2) Ag2O, \Delta] CH2=CH-CH=CH2 + N(CH3)3" />

In exam answers, the number of methylation–elimination cycles needed to remove the nitrogen equals the number of C–N bonds that must be broken: one for an open-chain amine, two for a ring nitrogen such as in pyrrolidine.

<ExamTip>
To predict the Hofmann product: list every β-carbon that has a hydrogen, then remove H from the least substituted one (CH₃ first, then CH₂). One mole of CH₃I is used per N–H bond plus one more to quaternise; for a ring nitrogen, expect one ring opening per cycle.
</ExamTip>

## Why onium leaving groups favour the Hofmann alkene

There are three situations in which the Hofmann alkene becomes major in any β-elimination: a leaving group that is poor or positively charged (F, –NR₃⁺, –SR₂⁺), a bulky base such as potassium tert-butoxide, and a crowded substrate near the more substituted β-carbon. The Hofmann elimination combines the first and third: a charged, bulky leaving group on the substrate itself.

## Limitations

- Only amines with a β-hydrogen can eliminate; if there is none, the salt instead loses CH₃ (substitution) on heating.
- Mixtures can form when two β-carbons are similar.
- The method is mainly of historical and exam importance today; the milder [Cope elimination](/reactions/cope-elimination/) of amine oxides gives the same Hofmann-type selectivity.
