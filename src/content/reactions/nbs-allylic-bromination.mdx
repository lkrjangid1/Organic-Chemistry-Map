## What is NBS allylic bromination?

**NBS allylic bromination**, named the **Wohl–Ziegler reaction**, replaces a hydrogen on the **allylic carbon** (the sp³ carbon next to a C=C) with bromine, using **N-bromosuccinimide (NBS)** and light or a radical initiator. The double bond is left intact. Propene gives **3-bromoprop-1-ene (allyl bromide)**, and succinimide is formed as the by-product. It is a **free-radical substitution**, not an addition.

<Eq ce="CH2=CH-CH3 ->[NBS][light, CCl4] CH2=CH-CH2Br" />

## Mechanism

Commercial NBS always contains a trace of Br₂, and that Br₂ is the species that actually starts the chain.

1. **Initiation.** Light (or a peroxide/AIBN initiator) splits Br₂ into two bromine atoms.
2. **H-abstraction.** Br• removes an **allylic hydrogen**, giving HBr and the **allyl radical**. This radical is resonance-stabilised, which is why the allylic position is attacked. In exam answers this is marked as the slow step.
3. **Br₂ regeneration.** The HBr just formed reacts with NBS to give succinimide and a fresh molecule of Br₂.
4. **Bromination.** The allyl radical takes a Br atom from Br₂, giving allyl bromide and a new Br• that repeats step 2.

<Eq ce="Br2 ->[light] 2 Br^{.}" />
<Eq ce="CH2=CH-CH3 + Br^{.} -> CH2=CH-CH2^{.} + HBr" />
<Eq ce="(CH2CO)2NBr + HBr -> (CH2CO)2NH + Br2" />
<Eq ce="CH2=CH-CH2^{.} + Br2 -> CH2=CH-CH2Br + Br^{.}" />

<KeyPoint>
NBS is mainly a **bromine reservoir**. It keeps Br₂ and HBr at a very low, steady level. Addition of Br₂ across C=C needs a high Br₂ concentration, so at low concentration the radical **substitution** at the allylic carbon wins.
</KeyPoint>

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Drawing the older picture in which NBS itself splits into a succinimidyl radical (N•) that removes the allylic H. Current accounts reject this because the N–Br bond is much stronger than the Br–Br bond; the Br• chain above is the accepted mechanism.
</ExamTip>

<KeyPoint>
Reactivity ∝ stability of the free radical formed. Allylic and benzylic hydrogens are replaced first, and among allylic positions the more substituted one reacts faster. Under these conditions **only monobromination** takes place.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Propene**

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 ->[NBS][light] Br-CH2-CH=CH2" />

**2. Chlorine analogue and the high-temperature route**

In exam answers, N-chlorosuccinimide (NCS) with light is written as the chlorine analogue, giving allyl chloride. Industrially, propene and chlorine at about 500 °C give allyl chloride by a free-radical path, while at lower temperature the main product is the addition product, 1,2-dichloropropane.

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + Cl2 ->[500 °C] Cl-CH2-CH=CH2 + HCl" />

**3. Cyclohexene**

<Eq ce="C6H10 ->[NBS][light, CCl4] C6H9Br" />

Cyclohexene gives 3-bromocyclohexene; the ring double bond stays and Br goes on the CH₂ next to it.

<ExamTip>
Read the reagent carefully: Br₂ in CCl₄ at room temperature in the dark adds across C=C (vicinal dibromide), but **NBS with hν or peroxide** substitutes at the allylic carbon. Br₂ at high temperature (about 500 °C) also gives allylic substitution.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Unsymmetrical allylic radicals** can react at either end, so mixtures of allylic bromides (with a shifted double bond) may form; bromination is favoured at the more substituted position.
- The CCl₄ must be **anhydrous**; water can hydrolyse the allylic bromide. Barium carbonate is sometimes added to keep the mixture dry and acid-free.
- Too much free Br₂ or HBr brings back ionic addition to the double bond.
- CCl₄ is now largely avoided for safety and environmental reasons; acetonitrile is one usable replacement.
- Benzylic C–H (for example the CH₃ of toluene) reacts in the same way, giving benzyl bromide.
