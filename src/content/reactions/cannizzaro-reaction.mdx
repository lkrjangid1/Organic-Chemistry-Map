## What is the Cannizzaro reaction?

The **Cannizzaro reaction** is the **base-induced disproportionation of an aldehyde that has no α-hydrogen**. Two molecules react in **concentrated NaOH or KOH**: one is **reduced to a primary alcohol** and the other is **oxidised to a carboxylate salt**. Benzaldehyde, for example, gives benzyl alcohol and sodium benzoate. Stanislao Cannizzaro reported the reaction in 1853.

Typical substrates are formaldehyde, benzaldehyde and other aromatic aldehydes, (CH₃)₃C–CHO and furfural, all of which lack α-hydrogen.

## Mechanism

Formaldehyde is the simplest case.

1. **Hydroxide adds to the carbonyl.** OH⁻ attacks the carbonyl carbon of the first HCHO, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide.
2. **Hydride transfer.** The alkoxide re-forms C=O and pushes a hydride ion (H⁻) onto the carbonyl carbon of a second HCHO. In exam answers this is the rate-determining step.
3. **Proton exchange.** The formic acid formed gives its proton to the methoxide ion, leaving formate and methanol.

<Eq ce="HCHO + OH- <=> H2C(OH)O-" />
<Eq ce="H2C(OH)O- + HCHO -> HCOOH + CH3O-" />
<Eq ce="HCOOH + CH3O- -> HCOO- + CH3OH" />

<KeyPoint>
The molecule attacked by OH⁻ is the one that is **oxidised** (it loses H⁻ and becomes the carboxylate). The molecule that receives the hydride is **reduced** to the alcohol. The reaction is a redox reaction of one compound with itself, i.e. a disproportionation.
</KeyPoint>

The hydride moves directly from carbon to carbon. When the reaction is run in D₂O, the alcohol formed carries no deuterium on its carbon, which shows the new C–H hydrogen comes from the other aldehyde, not from the solvent.

### Kinetics

- Normal conditions: **rate = k[RCHO]²[OH⁻]**, overall order **3**.
- Very high base concentration: the tetrahedral alkoxide loses a second proton to form a **dianion**, which transfers hydride even more readily. An extra term **k′[RCHO]²[OH⁻]²** appears, so the order becomes **4**.

## Examples

**Formaldehyde:**

<Eq ce="2 HCHO + NaOH ->[conc.] CH3OH + HCOONa" />

**Benzaldehyde:**

<Eq ce="2 C6H5CHO + KOH ->[conc.] C6H5CH2OH + C6H5COOK" />

**Furfural** gives furfuryl alcohol and 2-furoic acid; this conversion is used commercially because both products are useful.

## Crossed Cannizzaro reaction

A plain Cannizzaro reaction can give at most 50% of the alcohol, because half of the aldehyde becomes acid. In the **crossed (intermolecular) Cannizzaro reaction**, a cheap "sacrificial" aldehyde, **formaldehyde**, is mixed with a valuable non-enolisable aldehyde. Formaldehyde acts as the hydride donor and is **oxidised to sodium formate**, while the other aldehyde is **reduced to its alcohol** in high yield.

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + HCHO ->[conc. NaOH] C6H5CH2OH + HCOONa" />

<ExamTip>
In any crossed Cannizzaro question that includes HCHO, write formate (HCOO⁻) as the oxidation product and the alcohol of the other aldehyde as the reduction product.
</ExamTip>

## Tollens condensation: synthesis of pentaerythritol

Acetaldehyde has α-hydrogens, so with excess formaldehyde and base it first undergoes **three aldol additions**, one per α-hydrogen. The product, (HOCH₂)₃C–CHO (pentaerythrose), has no α-hydrogen left, so a **crossed Cannizzaro reaction** with a fourth formaldehyde reduces it to **pentaerythritol**. Bernhard Tollens first reported this compound in 1891, and the aldol-then-Cannizzaro sequence is often called the Tollens condensation in exam books.

<Eq ce="CH3CHO + 3 HCHO ->[OH-] (HOCH2)3C-CHO" />
<Eq ce="(HOCH2)3C-CHO + HCHO + OH- -> C(CH2OH)4 + HCOO-" />

## Intramolecular Cannizzaro reaction

When both aldehyde-level carbons are in one molecule, hydride shifts within the molecule. α-Keto aldehydes give α-hydroxy acids in this way; phenylglyoxal gives mandelic acid after acidification.

<Eq ce="C6H5COCHO ->[1. OH-][2. H3O+] C6H5CH(OH)COOH" />

This is the benzilic acid rearrangement pattern with hydride as the migrating group.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Aldehydes **with α-hydrogen** (e.g. CH₃CHO) give **aldol** products in base, not Cannizzaro products.
- With an **alkoxide or aluminium alkoxide** instead of hydroxide, two aldehydes join into an ester: the [Tishchenko reaction](/reactions/tishchenko-reaction/).
- Today other reagents reduce or oxidise aldehydes more cleanly, so the reaction is mainly important as a concept and as a side reaction whenever aldehydes meet strong base.
