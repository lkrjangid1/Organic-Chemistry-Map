## What is the peroxide effect?

The **peroxide effect**, also called the **Kharasch effect**, is the **anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr** to an unsymmetrical alkene when an organic peroxide is present. Bromine ends up on the carbon that carries **more hydrogens**, so propene gives **1-bromopropane** instead of 2-bromopropane. The peroxide changes the mechanism from an ionic (carbocation) pathway to a **free-radical chain** pathway. Morris Kharasch, who explained it in the 1930s, gave it the name "peroxide effect".

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + HBr ->[(C6H5CO)2O2] CH3-CH2-CH2Br" />

## Mechanism

The reaction is a radical chain with initiation, propagation and termination steps.

**I. Initiation**

1. The weak O–O bond of the peroxide breaks **homolytically** on warming or under light, giving two alkoxy radicals.
2. An alkoxy radical removes the hydrogen atom from HBr, producing a **bromine atom** (Br•).

<Eq ce="R-O-O-R ->[light or heat] 2 R-O^{.}" />
<Eq ce="R-O^{.} + H-Br -> R-OH + Br^{.}" />

**II. Propagation**

3. Br• adds to the **terminal CH₂** of propene. Two radicals are possible; the one with the unpaired electron on the **secondary carbon** is more stable (more hyperconjugation), so it is the one formed.
4. This carbon radical removes a hydrogen atom from **another molecule of HBr**, giving 1-bromopropane and a fresh Br•, which carries the chain forward.

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + Br^{.} -> CH3-\dot{C}H-CH2Br" />
<Eq ce="CH3-\dot{C}H-CH2Br + HBr -> CH3-CH2-CH2Br + Br^{.}" />

**III. Termination**

5. The chain stops when any two radicals meet and pair up, for example Br• + Br• → Br₂, or a carbon radical with Br•.

<KeyPoint>
The regiochemistry is decided in step 3. Br• adds where it leaves the **more stable radical** (2° rather than 1°). Because Br adds first and H adds last, the final product has Br on the less substituted carbon. In exam answers this addition of Br• is often marked as the slow step.
</KeyPoint>

## Why only HBr shows the peroxide effect

A radical chain only runs well if **both** propagation steps release energy.

- **HBr:** both steps are exothermic, so the chain is fast and beats the slower ionic addition, especially in non-polar solvents.
- **HCl:** the H–Cl bond is strong, so the carbon radical cannot easily take H from HCl (step 4 is endothermic).
- **HI:** the iodine atom is too weak an attacker, so adding I• to the C=C (step 3) is endothermic.
- **HF:** radical formation is extremely unfavourable.

## Examples

**1. Propene, with and without peroxide**

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + HBr -> CH3-CHBr-CH3" />
<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + HBr ->[H2O2 or (C6H5CO)2O2] CH3-CH2-CH2Br" />
<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH2 + HCl ->[peroxide] CH3-CHCl-CH3" />

With HCl the product is 2-chloropropane whether or not peroxide is present.

**2. 2-Methylpropene (isobutylene)**

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C=CH2 + HBr ->[peroxide] (CH3)2CH-CH2Br" />

Without peroxide, the product is 2-bromo-2-methylpropane; with peroxide it is 1-bromo-2-methylpropane.

**3. But-1-ene**

<Eq ce="CH3CH2CH=CH2 + HBr ->[peroxide] CH3CH2CH2CH2Br" />

Historically, early chemists got 1-bromobutane only when their samples were contaminated with peroxides, which is how the effect was discovered.

<ExamTip>
Check three things in any HX + alkene question: (i) is the reagent HBr, (ii) is a peroxide (R₂O₂, H₂O₂, benzoyl peroxide) mentioned, and (iii) is the alkene unsymmetrical? Only when all three are true is the product anti-Markovnikov. For a symmetrical alkene such as but-2-ene, both pathways give the same product.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Only **HBr** gives anti-Markovnikov addition by this route; HCl, HI and HF follow Markovnikov's rule even with peroxide.
- Because the intermediate is a radical, not a carbocation, **no carbocation rearrangements** (hydride or methyl shifts) occur.
- Radical traps such as oxygen slow the chain, and concentrated alkenes can also undergo radical polymerisation.
- For water, the anti-Markovnikov alcohol is made by hydroboration–oxidation, not by a peroxide.
