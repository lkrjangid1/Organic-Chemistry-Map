## What is the Wurtz reaction?

The **Wurtz reaction** is the coupling of two molecules of an **alkyl halide** with **sodium metal in dry ether** to give a **higher alkane** containing twice the number of carbon atoms. The two alkyl groups are joined by a new C–C bond and the halogen leaves as sodium halide: 2R–X + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX. It is the standard NCERT method for preparing **symmetrical alkanes** with an even number of carbon atoms, named after Charles Adolphe Wurtz (1855).

## General reaction

<Eq ce="2 R-X + 2 Na ->[dry ether] R-R + 2 NaX" />

The ether must be completely free of moisture: sodium reacts violently with water, and water would destroy the reactive intermediate. Unreactive **polar aprotic solvents** (dry ether, THF, dioxane) are used.

## Mechanism

The exact mechanism is still debated, and exam answers accept both versions.

**1. Free radical mechanism**

1. Sodium gives up an electron: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻.
2. The electron breaks the C–X bond, giving an alkyl radical and a halide ion: R–X + e⁻ → R• + X⁻.
3. Two alkyl radicals combine to form R–R; Na⁺ and X⁻ form NaX.

<Eq ce="R-X + Na -> R^. + NaX" />
<Eq ce="R^. + R^. -> R-R" />

**2. Ionic (organosodium) mechanism**

1. Two sodium atoms supply two electrons to one R–X, giving a carbanion: R–X + 2e⁻ → R⁻ + X⁻ (that is, an organosodium compound R⁻Na⁺).
2. The carbanion attacks a second molecule of R–X as a nucleophile, displacing X⁻ in an SN2-like step, to form R–R.

<Eq ce="R-X + 2 Na -> R^-Na+ + NaX" />
<Eq ce="R^-Na+ + R-X -> R-R + NaX" />

<KeyPoint>
Sources describe the first step as a metal–halogen exchange (like forming a Grignard reagent) that may pass through radicals. The organosodium intermediate then behaves like a nucleophile in an SN2-type substitution.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Bromomethane to ethane** (NCERT):

<Eq ce="2 CH3Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] CH3-CH3 + 2 NaBr" />

2. **Bromoethane to n-butane** (NCERT):

<Eq ce="2 C2H5Br + 2 Na ->[dry ether] CH3CH2CH2CH3 + 2 NaBr" />

3. **2-Chloropropane to 2,3-dimethylbutane**, a secondary halide giving a branched symmetrical alkane:

<Eq ce="2 (CH3)2CHCl + 2 Na ->[dry ether] (CH3)2CH-CH(CH3)2 + 2 NaCl" />

## Intramolecular Wurtz reaction (dihalides)

When both halogens are in the same molecule, sodium can close a ring. The classic case is **1-bromo-3-chlorocyclobutane**, which gives the highly strained **bicyclo[1.1.0]butane** in about 95% yield. In the published procedure the reaction is run in refluxing dioxane, where sodium is molten.

<Eq ce="C4H6BrCl + 2 Na ->[dioxane][reflux] C4H6 + NaBr + NaCl" />

In exam answers the two outcomes are contrasted: Na/ether at low temperature joins two molecules (intermolecular coupling at the C–Br carbon, giving a bicyclobutyl with both Cl atoms kept). Na/ether at high temperature, 2 equivalents of Na, or Na in dioxane favour ring closure to bicyclobutane. Vicinal dihalides give alkenes and geminal dihalides give alkynes under Wurtz conditions.

<KeyPoint>
Remember: "मुक्त मूलक की अभिक्रियाशीलता का क्रम: 1° &gt; 2° &gt; 3°" (order of free-radical reactivity, that is, of coupling: 1° &gt; 2° &gt; 3°). In exam answers, primary halides give the best Wurtz yields; tertiary halides mostly give alkenes and alkanes by disproportionation or elimination instead of coupling.
</KeyPoint>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Odd-carbon alkanes:** using two different halides (for example CH₃Br + C₂H₅Br) gives a mixture of ethane, propane and butane. NCERT asks why the method is not preferred for odd-carbon alkanes.
- **No methane:** at least two carbon atoms are joined, so ethane is the smallest product.
- **Tertiary halides** give mostly elimination products, not coupling.
- **Functional groups** that react with hot sodium (OH, C=O, NO₂) cannot be present.
- In real lab practice, yields are low, so the reaction is mainly of teaching value except for small rings. Other metals (Zn, Ag, activated Cu, In) have been tried to improve it.

<ExamTip>
To get an unsymmetrical alkane R–R′ cleanly, answer **Corey–House synthesis**, not Wurtz. If the question pairs an aryl halide with an alkyl halide and sodium, it is the **Wurtz–Fittig reaction**.
</ExamTip>
