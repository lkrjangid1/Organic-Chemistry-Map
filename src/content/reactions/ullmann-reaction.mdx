## What is the Ullmann reaction?

The **Ullmann reaction** (Ullmann coupling) is the **copper-mediated coupling of two aryl halides** to give a **symmetrical biaryl**. An aryl iodide, or an aryl halide carrying an electron-withdrawing group, is heated with an excess of **copper powder** at about 200 °C, and the two aryl groups join through a new C–C bond: **iodobenzene → biphenyl**. Fritz Ullmann and his student Bielecki reported it in 1901; it was the first reaction to show that a transition metal could join two aryl groups.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="2 Ar-X + 2 Cu ->[\Delta] Ar-Ar + 2 CuX" />

In short: iodobenzene →(Cu) Ph–Ph.

## Mechanism

The classical Ullmann reaction does **not** go through free radicals; electron spin resonance studies rule out radical intermediates. The accepted simplified picture is:

1. **Formation of an arylcopper(I) species.** Copper metal reacts with the aryl halide (an oxidative step) to give an organocopper compound, Ar–Cu, plus CuX.
2. **Reaction with a second aryl halide.** The arylcopper reacts with another molecule of Ar–X (oxidative addition, then reductive elimination of the two aryl groups), forming the Ar–Ar bond and releasing CuX.

<Eq ce="Ar-X + 2 Cu -> Ar-Cu + CuX" />
<Eq ce="Ar-Cu + Ar-X -> Ar-Ar + CuX" />

<KeyPoint>
Unlike the Fittig reaction (Na, radical or carbanion pathway), the Ullmann reaction is a transition-metal (copper) mediated coupling through an organocopper intermediate.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

1. **Iodobenzene to biphenyl**:

<Eq ce="2 C6H5I + 2 Cu ->[\Delta] C6H5-C6H5 + 2 CuI" />

2. **2-Chloronitrobenzene to 2,2′-dinitrobiphenyl**, the textbook classic with copper-bronze:

<Eq ce="2 O2N-C6H4-Cl + 2 Cu ->[\Delta] O2N-C6H4-C6H4-NO2 + 2 CuCl" />

3. **4-Chloronitrobenzene to 4,4′-dinitrobiphenyl**:

<Eq ce="2 p-O2NC6H4Cl + 2 Cu ->[\Delta] p-O2NC6H4-C6H4NO2-p + 2 CuCl" />

## Effect of the halogen and of electron-withdrawing groups

The key exam point: **chlorobenzene does not give the Ullmann reaction, but if an electron-withdrawing group such as –NO₂ is on the ring, the reaction takes place.** Sources agree that the traditional Ullmann reaction is limited to **electron-deficient aryl halides**, which is why the standard example is 2-chloronitrobenzene.

- Aryl **iodides** react most easily (weakest C–X bond), then bromides; plain aryl chlorides fail.
- A **nitro group ortho or para** to the halogen activates an aryl chloride.

<ExamTip>
If a JEE question asks which aryl halide gives the Ullmann reaction with Cu/heat, pick iodobenzene or a nitro-substituted chloro/bromobenzene, not plain chlorobenzene.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- **Harsh conditions:** the classical reaction needs stoichiometric (excess) copper and high temperatures (about 200 °C), and yields can be erratic.
- **Symmetrical products:** coupling two different aryl halides gives mixtures. Unsymmetrical biaryls are made by modified routes (for example arylcopper reagents formed at low temperature).
- **Modern versions** use copper with bidentate ligands, or palladium and nickel catalysts, under milder conditions. In real synthesis it is often replaced by palladium couplings such as the Suzuki reaction.
- **Ullmann-type reactions** (a separate meaning of the name) are copper-catalysed substitutions of aryl halides by phenoxides or amines, for example the Ullmann ether synthesis of diaryl ethers.
