## What is the Malaprade oxidation?

The **Malaprade oxidation** (Malaprade reaction) is the cleavage of a **vicinal diol**, –CH(OH)–CH(OH)–, by **periodic acid (HIO₄)** or a periodate salt such as NaIO₄. The C–C bond joining the two OH-bearing carbons breaks, and each of those carbons becomes a **C=O group**, so one diol gives **two aldehydes or ketones**. Iodine(VII) is reduced to iodic acid, HIO₃. Léon Malaprade reported the reaction in 1928.

## Which compounds are cleaved

The essential condition (आवश्यक शर्त) is two **neighbouring carbons that each carry O or N**:

| Group in the molecule | Cleaved by HIO₄? | Fragments formed |
|---|---|---|
| –CH(OH)–CH(OH)– (1,2-diol) | yes | two aldehydes / ketones |
| –CO–CH(OH)– (α-hydroxy carbonyl) | yes | acid + aldehyde / ketone |
| –CO–CO– (1,2-dicarbonyl) | yes | two carboxylic acids |
| –CH(OH)–CH(NH₂)– (1,2-amino alcohol) | yes | two carbonyl compounds + NH₃ |
| –CH(OH)–CH₂–CH(OH)– (1,3-diol) | no | no reaction |

In exam lists the α-amino carbonyl group, –CO–CH(NH₂)–, is added to this table on the same reasoning.

## Mechanism

1. **Cyclic periodate ester.** The two OH groups of the diol condense with HIO₄ to form a five-membered cyclic iodine(VII) diester. This is why the OH groups must be on adjacent carbons, and why **cis-diols react faster than trans-diols** in rings.
2. **Fragmentation.** The ring breaks: the C–C bond cleaves and two C=O bonds form, while iodine is reduced from +7 to +5 (HIO₃).

<Eq ce="R2C(OH)-C(OH)R2 + HIO4 -> 2R2C=O + HIO3 + H2O" />

## How to count the products

Cut every C–C bond that joins two O-bearing carbons, then add OH to each cut end:

- a **terminal CH₂OH** carbon becomes **HCHO**;
- an **inner CHOH** (cut on both sides) becomes **HCOOH**;
- a **CHO** carbon cut on one side becomes **HCOOH**;
- an **inner C=O** (ketone carbon cut on both sides) becomes **CO₂**.

One HIO₄ is used for each C–C bond broken.

<KeyPoint>
Periodic acid counts the adjacent OH groups for you: moles of HIO₄ used = number of C–C bonds cleaved. This is how it was used historically to work out the structures and ring sizes of sugars.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Butane-2,3-diol** gives two acetaldehyde:

<Eq ce="CH3CH(OH)CH(OH)CH3 + HIO4 -> 2CH3CHO + HIO3 + H2O" />

**2. Pinacol** gives two acetone:

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C(OH)C(OH)(CH3)2 + HIO4 -> 2(CH3)2C=O + HIO3 + H2O" />

**3. Glucose (open chain, an aldohexose).** Five C–C bonds are cut. C-1 (CHO) and C-2 to C-5 (CHOH) each become formic acid; C-6 (CH₂OH) becomes formaldehyde.

<Eq ce="OHC-(CHOH)4-CH2OH + 5HIO4 -> 5HCOOH + HCHO + 5HIO3" />

**4. Fructose (open chain, a ketohexose).** Again five bonds break. C-1 and C-6 (CH₂OH) become formaldehyde, C-3, C-4 and C-5 become formic acid, and the ketone carbon C-2 is cut on both sides and leaves as CO₂.

<Eq ce="HOCH2-CO-(CHOH)3-CH2OH + 5HIO4 -> 3HCOOH + 2HCHO + CO2 + H2O + 5HIO3" />

<ExamTip>
Glucose: 5 HCOOH + 1 HCHO. Fructose: 3 HCOOH + 2 HCHO + 1 CO₂. Both use 5 HIO₄. The extra HCHO and the CO₂ from fructose come from its two terminal CH₂OH groups and its central ketone carbon.
</ExamTip>

## Lead tetraacetate: the Criegee variant

**Lead tetraacetate, Pb(OAc)₄**, cleaves vicinal diols in the same way and gives the same carbonyl fragments. Strictly, this is called the **Criegee oxidation**. It is milder and is usually run in dry organic solvents (it is hydrolysed by water), whereas periodate is used in water. Both pass through a cyclic ester with the two OH groups.

## Limitations and exceptions

- Only **1,2-relationships** count; 1,3- and 1,4-diols survive.
- A rigid **trans-diol** that cannot form the five-membered cyclic ester reacts very slowly or not at all.
- Diols on simple alkyl chains are usually prepared first by dihydroxylation of an alkene (cold dilute KMnO₄ or OsO₄); the two-step sequence then mimics [ozonolysis](/reactions/ozonolysis/).
