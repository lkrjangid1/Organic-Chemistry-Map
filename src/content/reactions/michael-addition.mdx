## What is the Michael addition?

The **Michael addition** (Michael reaction) is the **1,4- or conjugate addition** of a carbon nucleophile, usually a **stabilised enolate**, to the **β-carbon of an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound**. The nucleophile is called the **Michael donor**, the unsaturated compound the **Michael acceptor**, and the product the **Michael adduct**. It was studied by Arthur Michael in 1887 and is one of the mildest ways to make a new C–C bond.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="CH2(EWG)2 + CH2=CH-CO-R ->[base] (EWG)2CH-CH2-CH2-CO-R" />

The new C–C bond joins the **α-carbon of the donor** to the **β-carbon of the acceptor**. With a carbonyl group on each side, the product has a **1,5-dicarbonyl** pattern.

| Michael donors | Michael acceptors |
|---|---|
| Diethyl malonate, CH₂(COOEt)₂ | Propenal (acrolein), CH₂=CHCHO |
| Ethyl acetoacetate (β-keto ester) | But-3-en-2-one (methyl vinyl ketone) |
| β-Diketones, e.g. pentane-2,4-dione | Ethyl propenoate (ethyl acrylate) |
| β-Keto nitriles | Propenenitrile (acrylonitrile) |
| Nitroalkanes | Propenamide; nitroethylene |

<KeyPoint>
The best Michael reactions use a **doubly stabilised donor** (a 1,3-dicarbonyl compound such as a malonate or β-keto ester) and an **unhindered acceptor** such as an enone or enal. Simple ketone enolates add less cleanly; for them the enamine route of the Stork reaction works better.
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism

1. **Enolate formation.** Ethoxide removes the acidic CH₂ proton of diethyl malonate. The carbanion is stabilised by both ester groups.
2. **Conjugate addition.** The carbanion attacks the **β-carbon** (the CH₂= end) of acrolein. The π electrons move toward oxygen, giving a new enolate of the acceptor.
3. **Protonation and tautomerism.** The enolate takes a proton from ethanol. Protonation on oxygen gives the enol (EtOOC)₂CH–CH₂–CH=CH–OH, which tautomerises to the aldehyde; protonation on carbon gives the aldehyde directly. Ethoxide is regenerated.

<Eq ce="CH2(COOEt)2 + EtO- <=> ^-CH(COOEt)2 + EtOH" />
<Eq ce="^-CH(COOEt)2 + CH2=CHCHO -> (EtOOC)2CH-CH2-CH=CH-O^-" />
<Eq ce="(EtOOC)2CH-CH2-CH=CH-OH <=> (EtOOC)2CH-CH2-CH2-CHO" />

Why 1,4 and not 1,2? Stabilised enolates are **soft** nucleophiles, and the LUMO of an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl has a large coefficient on the β-carbon, so orbital interactions direct the attack there. The product also keeps its C=O bond, which makes the 1,4-adduct more stable.

## Examples

**1. Diethyl malonate and acrolein.**

<Eq ce="CH2(COOEt)2 + CH2=CHCHO ->[EtONa] (EtOOC)2CHCH2CH2CHO" />

**2. Ethyl acetoacetate and methyl vinyl ketone.** This is the textbook example; the product is a 1,5-diketone carrying an ester.

<Eq ce="CH3COCH2COOEt + CH2=CHCOCH3 ->[EtONa] CH3COCH(COOEt)CH2CH2COCH3" />

**3. First step of the Robinson annulation.** The enolate of a ketone adds to methyl vinyl ketone; the resulting 1,5-diketone then undergoes an intramolecular aldol condensation to give a cyclohexenone ring.

<ExamTip>
To find the Michael product, draw the acceptor as C=C–C=O, number the carbonyl carbon 1, and attach the donor carbon to carbon 4 of the O=C–C=C chain (the β-carbon). The C=O stays; the C=C becomes a single bond.
</ExamTip>

## Variations

- **Aza-Michael and oxa-Michael:** amines, alcohols and water can also add 1,4 to α,β-unsaturated carbonyls. Some authors restrict the name "Michael" to carbon nucleophiles.
- **Enamine donors:** neutral enamines add in the same way in the [Stork enamine reaction](/reactions/stork-enamine-reaction/).
- **Asymmetric versions:** chiral catalysts give enantioselective Michael additions, widely used in modern synthesis.

## Limitations

- Strongly basic, hard nucleophiles (Grignard and organolithium reagents) usually add **1,2** to the C=O instead.
- Hindered acceptors react poorly.
- If the product can still enolise, it may react further, for example in an intramolecular aldol step.
