## What is the Reimer–Tiemann reaction?

The **Reimer–Tiemann reaction** is the **ortho-formylation of phenols**: phenol is treated with **chloroform (CHCl₃) and aqueous sodium hydroxide**, and a **–CHO group is introduced at the ortho position** of the ring. Acidifying the mixture gives **salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde)**. The reactive species is **dichlorocarbene, :CCl₂**, made from chloroform by the base. The reaction was reported by Karl Reimer and Ferdinand Tiemann in 1876.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="C6H5OH ->[CHCl3, aq. NaOH][\Delta] 2-NaOC6H4CHO ->[H3O+] 2-HOC6H4CHO" />

NCERT describes the intermediate as a **substituted benzal chloride** (a –CHCl₂ group on the ring), which is hydrolysed by the alkali to the aldehyde.

## Mechanism

1. **Carbene formation.** OH⁻ removes the proton from CHCl₃, giving CCl₃⁻; this loses Cl⁻ (α-elimination) to give **:CCl₂**.
2. **Phenoxide formation.** OH⁻ also deprotonates phenol. The negative charge of the phenoxide is spread into the ring, making the ortho and para carbons strongly nucleophilic.
3. **Attack on the carbene.** The ortho carbon of the phenoxide attacks the electron-poor carbene carbon. A proton shift restores aromaticity, giving an o-(dichloromethyl)phenoxide.
4. **Hydrolysis.** Under the basic conditions the two C–Cl bonds are replaced by OH, and the gem-diol loses water to give the –CHO group (as the sodium phenoxide).
5. **Acid work-up.** Dilute acid protonates the phenoxide, giving salicylaldehyde.

<Eq ce="CHCl3 + OH- -> CCl3^- + H2O -> :CCl2 + Cl-" />

<Eq ce="C6H5O- + :CCl2 -> 2-(CHCl2)C6H4O- ->[OH-] 2-(CHO)C6H4O-" />

<KeyPoint>
Dichlorocarbene is a neutral but strongly electron-deficient electrophile, so it needs a very electron-rich ring. That is why the reaction works on phenoxide ions and electron-rich heterocycles, but not on benzene itself.
</KeyPoint>

## Examples

**1. Phenol to salicylaldehyde (NCERT):**

<Eq ce="C6H5OH + CHCl3 + 3NaOH ->[\Delta] 2-HOC6H4CHO + 3NaCl + 2H2O" />

Both o-hydroxybenzaldehyde and p-hydroxybenzaldehyde can form. The ortho isomer is the major product; in exam answers the para isomer is listed as the minor product.

**2. Carbon tetrachloride variant:** using CCl₄ in place of CHCl₃ introduces –COOH, so phenol gives **salicylic acid**.

<Eq ce="C6H5OH ->[CCl4, NaOH][H3O+] 2-HOC6H4COOH" />

**3. Abnormal Reimer–Tiemann reaction (ring expansion):** pyrrole with chloroform and base gives **3-chloropyridine**. Dichlorocarbene adds across a C=C of the pyrrole ring, and the bicyclic intermediate opens to a six-membered ring. This is the **Ciamician–Dennstedt rearrangement**.

<Eq ce="C4H4NH + CHCl3 ->[KOH] C5H4ClN" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Writing the reagent of the abnormal reaction as "CHCl₂". The correct reagent is chloroform, CHCl₃, which with base gives :CCl₂. In JEE questions, pyrrole + CHCl₃/base → 3-chloropyridine is the standard "abnormal" product: the ring grows by one carbon and keeps a chlorine.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- The reaction is run in a two-phase mixture (aqueous NaOH and chloroform), so rapid stirring, a phase-transfer catalyst or a co-solvent such as 1,4-dioxane is needed.
- It needs warming to start but can then become strongly exothermic.
- Substrates with alkenes or amino groups are unsuitable, because :CCl₂ also forms dichlorocyclopropanes and isocyanides ([carbylamine reaction](/reactions/carbylamine-reaction/)).
- Compounds that cannot survive hot hydroxide do not tolerate the conditions.
- Unlike Gattermann–Koch formylation, it works on phenols and needs no acid or dry conditions.
