## What is decarboxylation?

**Decarboxylation** is the removal of a carboxyl group as **carbon dioxide**, usually replacing –COOH by –H. In the classic NCERT method, the **sodium salt of a carboxylic acid is heated with soda lime (NaOH and CaO in the ratio 3 : 1)** to give a **hydrocarbon with one carbon atom fewer**. Acids that carry a carbonyl group on the β-carbon, such as **β-keto acids and malonic acids**, lose CO₂ simply on heating, through a six-membered cyclic transition state.

## 1. Decarboxylation with soda lime

<Eq ce="R-COOH ->[NaOH] R-COONa ->[NaOH + CaO][\Delta] R-H + Na2CO3" />

- First the acid is neutralised to its sodium salt.
- The dry salt is then heated strongly with soda lime. The carboxylate carbon leaves as carbonate, and R picks up a hydrogen.
- This is a **step-down reaction**: the product has one carbon fewer than the acid.

**Mechanism (simplified).** The C–COO⁻ bond breaks so that R leaves with the bonding pair, as a carbanion-like R⁻ group, while CO₂ is captured by the base as carbonate. R⁻ then takes a proton. Because of this, the ease of decarboxylation depends on the **stability of R⁻**: groups that stabilise a negative charge make the reaction easier.

<KeyPoint>
Rate of soda-lime decarboxylation follows carbanion stability (not carbocation stability). Acids with an electron-withdrawing group next to COOH, and aromatic acids, decarboxylate more easily than simple alkanoic acids.
</KeyPoint>

**Examples:**

<Eq ce="CH3COONa + NaOH ->[CaO][\Delta] CH4 + Na2CO3" />

<Eq ce="C6H5COONa + NaOH ->[CaO][\Delta] C6H6 + Na2CO3" />

## 2. Thermal decarboxylation of β-keto acids

Simple carboxylic acids need very high temperatures to lose CO₂, but β-keto acids decarboxylate on gentle warming. Acetoacetic acid gives acetone:

<Eq ce="CH3COCH2COOH ->[\Delta] CH3COCH3 + CO2 ^" />

**Mechanism (cyclic transition state):**

1. The –COOH hydrogen is held by an intramolecular hydrogen bond to the β-carbonyl oxygen, forming a six-membered ring.
2. In one concerted step, the O–H hydrogen moves to the keto oxygen, the Cα–COOH bond breaks and CO₂ leaves. When drawing it, label the bonds **f** (forming) and **d** (dissociating).
3. The product is an enol, which tautomerises to the ketone.

## 3. Malonic (gem-dicarboxylic) acids

When two COOH groups sit on the same carbon, one of them acts as the "β-carbonyl" for the other. On heating, one CO₂ is lost and a monocarboxylic acid remains. This is the final step of the malonic ester synthesis.

<Eq ce="(CH3)2C(COOH)2 ->[\Delta] (CH3)2CH-COOH + CO2 ^" />

The general rule: any acid of the type –A(=B)–CH₂–COOH, with a double-bonded group two atoms from the carboxyl carbon, loses CO₂ on heating to give –A(=B)–CH₃.

## 4. β,γ-Unsaturated acids (exam convention)

In exam answers, a C=C between the β- and γ-carbons plays the same role as the β-carbonyl. The acid hydrogen goes to the far alkene carbon through a six-membered cyclic transition state, the double bond shifts, and CO₂ leaves:

<Eq ce="CH3-CH=CH-CH2-COOH ->[\Delta] CH3-CH2-CH=CH2 + CO2 ^" />

<ExamTip>
Before heating any acid, check the β-position. A C=O, C=C or second COOH there means easy thermal loss of CO₂. If the β-carbon instead carries OH or NH₂, the acid usually loses H₂O or NH₃ (with the α-H) to give an α,β-unsaturated acid. For example, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOH on heating gives CH₃CH=CHCOOH.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations and exceptions

- Simple aliphatic acids do not lose CO₂ on heating alone; they need soda lime (or electrolysis).
- Free acids decarboxylate faster under conditions that turn COOH into the carboxylate, which is why the sodium salt is used rather than the acid itself.
- **Kolbe electrolysis** is a different decarboxylation: the radicals formed couple, giving an alkane with **twice** the number of carbons in the alkyl group.
