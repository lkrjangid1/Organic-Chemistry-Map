## What is the Perkin reaction?

The **Perkin reaction** converts an **aromatic aldehyde** into an **α,β-unsaturated aromatic acid** by heating it with an **acid anhydride** and the **sodium (or potassium) salt of the same acid**. The salt acts as the base. Benzaldehyde, acetic anhydride and sodium acetate give **cinnamic acid**, C₆H₅CH=CHCOOH. It was developed by William Henry Perkin in 1868 and is an aldol-type condensation.

## General reaction

<Eq ce="ArCHO + (RCH2CO)2O ->[RCH2COONa][\Delta] ArCH=C(R)COOH + RCH2COOH" />

With acetic anhydride, R = H and the product is a cinnamic acid. With propanoic anhydride and sodium propanoate, R = CH₃ and the product is an α-methylcinnamic acid.

<KeyPoint>
Substrate rule: an **aromatic aldehyde** (no α-H, so it cannot self-condense) plus an **anhydride that has α-hydrogens**. The base is the carboxylate salt of the acid that matches the anhydride.
</KeyPoint>

## Mechanism

The steps below are the version used in exam answers. The exact pathway is still debated (versions with and without transfer of the acetyl group have been proposed), but the product is the same.

1. **Carbanion formation.** Acetate removes an α-hydrogen from acetic anhydride, giving ⁻CH₂–CO–O–CO–CH₃.
2. **Addition to the aldehyde.** The carbanion attacks the C=O carbon of benzaldehyde. After protonation, the aldol-type adduct is C₆H₅–CH(OH)–CH₂–CO–O–CO–CH₃. This is where the condensation step itself ends.
3. **Hydrolysis.** Acid work-up (H₃O⁺) cleaves the anhydride, giving the β-hydroxy acid C₆H₅–CH(OH)–CH₂–COOH and acetic acid.
4. **Dehydration.** The OH and an α-H are lost as water on heating, giving the C=C that is conjugated with both the benzene ring and the COOH group.

<Eq ce="(CH3CO)2O + CH3COO- <=> ^-CH2COOCOCH3 + CH3COOH" />
<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + ^-CH2COOCOCH3 -> C6H5CH(OH)CH2COOCOCH3" />
<Eq ce="C6H5CH(OH)CH2COOCOCH3 ->[H3O+] C6H5CH(OH)CH2COOH ->[\Delta][-H2O] C6H5CH=CHCOOH" />

<Trick>
**Perkin यहीं तक है** ("Perkin goes only up to here"): the actual condensation stops at the aldol-type adduct of the anhydride. Hydrolysis and dehydration are what turn it into cinnamic acid.
</Trick>

## Examples

**1. Cinnamic acid from benzaldehyde.**

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + (CH3CO)2O ->[CH3COONa][\Delta] C6H5CH=CHCOOH + CH3COOH" />

The product is mainly the more stable *trans* (E) isomer.

**2. Coumarin from salicylaldehyde.** With 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde the new acid side chain closes onto the neighbouring OH to form a six-membered lactone, coumarin, a fragrance compound. Perkin first reported the reaction in this context.

**3. α-Substituted cinnamic acids.** With propanoic anhydride and sodium propanoate the α-carbon carries a methyl group, giving C₆H₅CH=C(CH₃)COOH. The same idea with aryl-substituted partners has been used in laboratory syntheses of the stilbene resveratrol.

<Eq ce="C6H5CHO + (CH3CH2CO)2O ->[CH3CH2COONa][\Delta] C6H5CH=C(CH3)COOH + CH3CH2COOH" />

<ExamTip>
To predict the product, join the aldehyde carbon to the α-carbon of the anhydride with a **double bond**, and keep the carbonyl of the anhydride as **–COOH**. Benzaldehyde + acetic anhydride → C₆H₅–CH=CH–COOH.
</ExamTip>

## Limitations

- **Aliphatic aldehydes with α-hydrogens** are poor substrates because they undergo their own aldol reactions.
- The anhydride needs **two α-hydrogens** on the same carbon; (CH₃)₂CH–CO anhydrides with only one α-H cannot form the C=C bond this way.
- High temperatures and long heating are typical, and yields can be moderate.
