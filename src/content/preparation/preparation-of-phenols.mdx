## What is the preparation of phenols?

Phenols are made by putting an **–OH group directly on an aromatic ring**. Because the ring resists ordinary nucleophilic substitution, the –OH is introduced either by **forcing a leaving group off the ring** (–Cl, –SO₃H, –N₂⁺), by a **rearrangement of an oxidised side chain** (cumene process), or by **direct hydroxylation**. NCERT lists four standard methods: from haloarenes, from benzenesulphonic acid, from diazonium salts and from cumene.

## Quick summary table

| Starting material | Reagents and conditions | Intermediate | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorobenzene | NaOH, 623 K, 320 atm; then H⁺ | Sodium phenoxide | Dow process |
| Benzene | Oleum, then fused NaOH; then H⁺ | Benzenesulphonic acid, sodium phenoxide | Sulphonate fusion |
| Aniline | NaNO₂ + HCl, 273–278 K; then warm H₂O | Benzenediazonium chloride | Diazonium hydrolysis |
| Cumene | O₂ (air); then dilute acid | Cumene hydroperoxide | Cumene (Hock) process |
| Benzene | FeSO₄ + H₂O₂ | Hydroxyl radical | Fenton hydroxylation |
| Salicylic acid | Heat | – | Decarboxylation |
| Anisole | Conc. HI, heat | – | Ether cleavage |

## 1. From haloarenes (Dow process)

Chlorobenzene is fused with **sodium hydroxide at 623 K and 320 atm**. Sodium phenoxide forms and is acidified to phenol.

<Eq ce="C6H5Cl + 2NaOH ->[623 K][320 atm] C6H5ONa + NaCl + H2O" />
<Eq ce="C6H5ONa + HCl -> C6H5OH + NaCl" />

Isotope-labelling experiments show that much of the reaction goes through a **benzyne** intermediate (elimination–addition) rather than direct displacement. See [Benzyne mechanism](/reactions/benzyne-mechanism/).

<KeyPoint>
Aryl halides need such harsh conditions because the C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character from resonance and the ring repels nucleophiles. Electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para (as in 4-nitrochlorobenzene) make the substitution much easier.
</KeyPoint>

## 2. From benzenesulphonic acid

Benzene is **sulphonated with oleum**. Benzenesulphonic acid is then **fused with molten NaOH**, giving sodium phenoxide, which is acidified. This was the first commercial route to phenol.

<Eq ce="C6H6 ->[oleum] C6H5SO3H ->[NaOH, fuse] C6H5ONa ->[H+] C6H5OH" />

Net conversion: C₆H₅SO₃H + 2NaOH → C₆H₅OH + Na₂SO₃ + H₂O.

## 3. From diazonium salts

An aromatic primary amine is diazotised with **NaNO₂ + HCl at 273–278 K**. When the cold diazonium solution is **warmed with water** (or treated with dilute acid), N₂ is lost and phenol forms. See [Diazotization reaction](/reactions/diazotization-reaction/).

<Eq ce="C6H5NH2 ->[NaNO2, HCl][273-278 K] C6H5N2^+ Cl^- ->[H2O][warm] C6H5OH + N2 + HCl" />

This is a laboratory method only; the diazonium precursor is too expensive for bulk phenol.

## 4. From cumene (industrial)

Most of the world's phenol comes from the **cumene process** (also called the Hock process).

1. **Cumene formation:** benzene is alkylated with propene over an acid catalyst (often phosphoric acid).
2. **Air oxidation:** cumene reacts with O₂ by a radical chain at its tertiary benzylic C–H, giving **cumene hydroperoxide**.
3. **Acid cleavage (Hock rearrangement):** with dilute acid, the hydroperoxide is protonated on its terminal oxygen, the **phenyl group migrates from carbon to oxygen** as water leaves, and the resulting carbocation is captured by water. The hemiacetal-like adduct breaks into **phenol and acetone**.

<Eq ce="C6H5CH(CH3)2 ->[O2] C6H5C(CH3)2OOH ->[H+][H2O] C6H5OH + CH3COCH3" />

<ExamTip>
The cumene process gives **two products**: phenol and **acetone**. The phenyl group, the best migrating group, moves to the electron-deficient oxygen, which is the same idea as in the [Baeyer–Villiger oxidation](/reactions/baeyer-villiger-oxidation/). Full mechanism: [Cumene process](/reactions/cumene-process/).
</ExamTip>

## 5. Other methods

- **Fenton's reagent:** FeSO₄ and H₂O₂ produce hydroxyl radicals that hydroxylate benzene directly (radical substitution). It is a classic laboratory route; direct oxidation of benzene is not used commercially.
- **Oxidation of toluene (Dow):** molten sodium benzoate with air and a copper catalyst gives phenol, CO₂ and water.
- **Raschig–Hooker process:** chlorobenzene hydrolysed by steam.
- **Decarboxylation of salicylic acid:** heating removes CO₂ and gives phenol. See [Decarboxylation](/reactions/decarboxylation/).
- **Cleavage of aryl alkyl ethers:** anisole with hot concentrated HI gives phenol and methyl iodide; the halide always attacks the alkyl side. See [Ether cleavage with HX](/reactions/ether-cleavage-with-hx/).
- **Dihydric phenols:** the [Dakin reaction](/reactions/dakin-reaction/) converts o- or p-hydroxybenzaldehyde into catechol or hydroquinone with alkaline H₂O₂.

<Eq ce="C6H6 + H2O2 ->[FeSO4] C6H5OH + H2O" />

<ExamTip title="Common mistake">
Aqueous NaOH at room temperature does **not** convert chlorobenzene into phenol. Without high temperature and pressure (or strong ortho/para electron-withdrawing groups), aryl halides do not undergo hydrolysis.
</ExamTip>
