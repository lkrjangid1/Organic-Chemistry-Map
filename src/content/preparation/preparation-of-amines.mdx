## What is the preparation of amines?

Amines are made in three broad ways: by **reducing** a nitrogen compound that already has the right carbon skeleton (nitro compounds, nitriles, amides, imines, oximes), by **alkylating ammonia or a nitrogen nucleophile** (ammonolysis, Gabriel synthesis), or by **rearranging an acid derivative** so that a carbon group moves onto nitrogen and the C=O carbon is lost (Hofmann, Curtius, Lossen, Schmidt). The choice depends on the class of amine wanted and whether the carbon count must go up, stay the same or go down.

## 1. Reduction of nitro compounds

Nitroarenes and nitroalkanes are reduced by **hydrogen over finely divided Ni, Pd or Pt**, or by **metals in acid** (Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl, or similar). **Iron scrap with HCl** is preferred industrially: the FeCl₂ formed is hydrolysed and releases HCl, so only a little acid is needed to start the reaction. See [reduction of nitrobenzene](/reactions/reduction-of-nitrobenzene/).

<Eq ce="C6H5NO2 + 6[H] ->[Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl] C6H5NH2 + 2H2O" />

Metal hydrides behave differently with aryl nitro compounds: **LiAlH₄ converts nitrobenzene mainly into azobenzene**, so it is not used to make aniline. Nitroalkanes, however, are reduced to alkanamines. Sodium sulphide can reduce one nitro group of a dinitro compound selectively ([Zinin reduction](/reactions/zinin-reduction/)).

<Eq ce="2C6H5NO2 ->[LiAlH4] C6H5-N=N-C6H5" />

## 2. Ammonolysis of alkyl halides and alcohols

An alkyl or benzyl halide heated with **ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube at 373 K** gives an alkylammonium salt; NaOH releases the amine. The amine reacts further, so 1°, 2°, 3° amines and a quaternary salt are formed; **excess NH₃** favours the primary amine. Reactivity: RI > RBr > RCl. Alcohols react with NH₃ over a dehydrating catalyst (alumina) in the same unselective way. Details: [ammonolysis of alkyl halides](/reactions/ammonolysis-of-alkyl-halides/).

<Eq ce="R-X + NH3 ->[373 K] R-NH3+X- ->[NaOH] R-NH2" />

## 3. Reduction of nitriles

Nitriles are reduced to primary amines by **LiAlH₄**, by **catalytic hydrogenation**, or by **sodium and ethanol** ([Mendius reduction](/reactions/mendius-reduction/)). Since R–CN is made from R–X and cyanide, this route gives an amine with **one more carbon** than R–X: an ascent of the amine series.

<Eq ce="R-C#N ->[LiAlH4 or H2/Ni or Na/C2H5OH] R-CH2-NH2" />

## 4. Reduction of amides

**LiAlH₄** reduces amides to amines with the C=O converted to CH₂. Primary amides give 1° amines; N-substituted amides give 2° and 3° amines, which makes this a useful route to unsymmetrical amines.

<Eq ce="R-CO-NH2 ->[1) LiAlH4][2) H2O] R-CH2-NH2" />

## 5. Gabriel phthalimide synthesis

Phthalimide with ethanolic KOH gives potassium phthalimide; heating with a primary alkyl halide and then alkaline hydrolysis releases a **pure primary amine**. Aryl amines cannot be made, because aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution with the phthalimide anion. See [Gabriel phthalimide synthesis](/reactions/gabriel-phthalimide-synthesis/).

<Eq ce="C6H4(CO)2N^-K+ ->[R-X] C6H4(CO)2N-R ->[NaOH(aq)] R-NH2 + C6H4(COONa)2" />

## 6. Hofmann bromamide degradation

A primary amide warmed with **Br₂ and aqueous or ethanolic NaOH** gives a primary amine with **one carbon fewer**; the alkyl or aryl group migrates from the carbonyl carbon to nitrogen. Benzamide gives aniline. See [Hofmann bromamide degradation](/reactions/hofmann-bromamide-degradation/).

<Eq ce="R-CO-NH2 + Br2 + 4NaOH -> R-NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2NaBr + 2H2O" />

## 7. Curtius, Lossen and Schmidt rearrangements

These three, like Hofmann, form an **isocyanate** R–N=C=O by a 1,2-shift onto electron-deficient nitrogen; water hydrolyses it to the amine and CO₂. The migrating group keeps its configuration. They differ in the starting material and the group that leaves:

- [Curtius rearrangement](/reactions/curtius-rearrangement/): an **acyl azide** (from RCOCl + NaN₃) is heated; N₂ leaves.
- [Lossen rearrangement](/reactions/lossen-rearrangement/): an **O-acyl hydroxamic acid** is treated with base; a carboxylate leaves.
- [Schmidt reaction](/reactions/schmidt-reaction/): a **carboxylic acid** is heated with **HN₃ and conc. H₂SO₄**; N₂ leaves.

<Eq ce="R-CO-N3 ->[\Delta][-N2] R-N=C=O ->[H2O][-CO2] R-NH2" />

## 8. Reductive amination and reduction of oximes

An aldehyde or ketone condenses with ammonia or a primary amine to form an imine, which is reduced in the same pot by **H₂/Ni** or **NaBH₃CN** (LiAlH₄ also reduces an isolated imine). Ammonia gives a 1° amine, a 1° amine gives a 2° amine. Oximes, from carbonyl compounds and NH₂OH, are reduced to primary amines by Na/C₂H₅OH, H₂/Ni or LiAlH₄. See [reductive amination](/reactions/reductive-amination/).

<Eq ce="R1R2C=O + H2N-R3 ->[-H2O] R1R2C=N-R3 ->[H2/Ni or NaBH3CN] R1R2CH-NH-R3" />

<KeyPoint>
**Count carbons first.** Same carbon count: reduce a nitro compound, amide, imine or oxime, or use Gabriel/ammonolysis. **One carbon more:** R–X → R–CN → reduce. **One carbon fewer:** Hofmann bromamide (or Curtius, Lossen, Schmidt).
</KeyPoint>

## Exam tips

<ExamTip>
NCERT lists six methods: reduction of nitro compounds, ammonolysis of alkyl halides, reduction of nitriles, reduction of amides, Gabriel phthalimide synthesis and Hofmann bromamide degradation. Board and NEET questions usually ask which of these gives a pure 1° amine (Gabriel, Hofmann), which changes the carbon count (nitrile reduction +1, Hofmann −1), and which cannot make aniline (Gabriel, ammonolysis of chlorobenzene).
</ExamTip>

- Aniline is made by reducing nitrobenzene, or from benzamide by Hofmann degradation; never by Gabriel synthesis.
- tert-Alkyl primary amines cannot be made by Gabriel synthesis (no SN2 at a tertiary carbon), but they can be made from the corresponding amide by Hofmann degradation (the migrating group keeps its configuration).
- For 2° and 3° amines with different groups, reduce an N-substituted amide or use reductive amination.
